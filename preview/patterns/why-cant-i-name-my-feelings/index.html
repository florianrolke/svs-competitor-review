Why can't I name what I'm feeling?

The short answer

Because naming is a separate skill from feeling, and it never got enough practice. The sensation arrives on time. The word that fits it does not, so what comes out of your mouth is "I don't know" or "stressed, I guess". Naming works as a learned capacity, built early by having your feelings named accurately for you, and it can still be built later.

Read these slowly and notice which ones are already in your own voice.

  • "It's my fault that I'm not getting the love I need."
  • "I'm always lacking something that prevents me from being fully loved."
  • "My emotional needs are too much for anyone to fulfill."
  • "Having emotional needs makes me weak and burdensome."

Sentences like these keep running underneath a feeling that never gets a name, and an unnamed feeling is one nobody else can help you with.

How the seminar works with the gap between a feeling and the words for it

Somebody asks how you feel about it. There is no edge in the question and no trap in it, just an ordinary Thursday evening and a person who genuinely wants to know. Then comes the pause. You can tell that something is happening in there, roughly the way you can tell a room is cold before you look at the thermostat, and what arrives in your mouth is "I don't know", or "stressed, I guess", or a tidy opinion about what somebody else should have done instead. The conversation moves on. Ten minutes later you are rinsing a pan, faintly irritated with yourself, and you have no word for that either.

Those four seconds are the entire pattern, and they are also the only place it can change. Everything below is about what happens inside them.

The first thing worth saying is that the feeling is present. The shortage sits one layer above it, in the equipment that turns a sensation into a word you can hand to somebody else. That distinction matters, because it changes what there is to work on.

One line before anything practical, since it outranks every technique here. If the blank arrives alongside hopelessness that has not lifted for months, a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, an addiction, or any thought of harming yourself however briefly it passes, that belongs with a licensed professional before it belongs with a page like this. The same holds if trying to describe what you feel reliably brings back memories of being hurt. What follows is built for the everyday version: the Thursday question, the joke that arrived too fast, the list you drafted instead of saying the sentence.

What Does It Sound Like From the Inside When Somebody Asks?

It sounds like a very narrow set of answers standing in for a very wide range of states.

Asked directly, in a room with no pressure in it, people in this pattern reply in almost identical language, whether or not they have ever met each other:

  • "I don't know."
  • "Stressed, I guess."
  • "Fine."
  • "I think they shouldn't have done that."
  • "Something is off, I just can't say what."

Look at the fourth one again. An opinion is often the closest available substitute when the feeling underneath has no name yet, which is why so many conversations about feelings turn into conversations about who was right. The fifth is the most honest of the set and gets dismissed as vague, though it is an accurate report of exactly how much resolution the internal picture actually has at that moment.

Underneath the shortage of words sits an older layer, and it runs on needs rather than vocabulary. These are sentences people in this pattern find going through their own heads, in their own phrasing:

  • "I have to go above and beyond to keep them interested."
  • "Only someone else’s love can fill my emptiness."
  • "If I'm not shown affection, it's because there’s something wrong with me."
  • "If I find the right person, they’ll fill this emotional void."

Notice how few of them sound like a language problem. From the inside this feels like a shortage of love, or of luck, or of whatever other people apparently have. Somebody can hold every one of those beliefs and still describe their emotional life as fine, because fine is the only word the system ever needed.

What Can a Professional Do Here That a Page Cannot?

Assess, which is the one thing writing cannot do at a distance.

A qualified professional can work out whether what you are describing is the everyday version of this or the more severe and persistent form, and there are established scales for exactly that. They can also tell whether something else is producing the flatness, since depression, medication, exhaustion, and long stretches of high stress all mute the emotional signal in ways that look similar from the outside and call for very different responses. Nobody can do that from a paragraph, and any page claiming otherwise is selling something.

The other thing worth knowing is that this specific gap responds well to structured help. Therapies that work in the body, that slow a conversation down and ask what is happening in your chest right now rather than what you think about it, are aimed directly at the missing step. If you have tried talking therapy and found yourself producing intelligent accounts of your childhood while feeling nothing, that experience is common with this pattern and it is worth saying out loud to whoever you are working with.

Where Does the Missing Vocabulary Actually Come From?

From a home where naming a feeling was either risky or pointless, repeated long enough that the naming step stopped being attempted at all.

Two conditions produce this, and either one alone is enough.

The first is unpredictability. A child adapting to a caregiver whose emotional responses swung without warning learns to control the output of feeling rather than to describe it. Direct expression got suppressed because it might have been met with volatility. Different parts of an emotional life got compartmentalized so they could be managed separately. Expressions got borrowed from whoever was in the room, which is faster than generating one and much safer than getting one wrong. None of those adaptations require a vocabulary. Every one of them works by bypassing the naming step. What that house does to a child more broadly has its own page.

The second is absence. Emotional presence and needs went unregistered rather than punished, so there was nobody consistently asking what a child felt and nobody reflecting a word back to be checked against. Nothing dramatic happened, which is precisely why so many people in this pattern describe their childhood as ordinary and mean it. The quieter formation is described in full here.

Naming is a mirrored skill. A child learns that disappointed fits this particular sensation by having an adult say the word at the moment the sensation is happening, watching it land or miss, and adjusting. Run that cycle a few thousand times and the words become automatic, arriving before you have consciously looked for them. Run it rarely, or on terms where a wrong answer was expensive, and what is left in adulthood is a felt state with nothing attached to it, plus a set of substitutes built specifically to communicate without naming.

What Was the Vagueness Protecting?

Two different things, on two different fronts, and both of them made sense at the time.

Inside an unpredictable household, a clearly named feeling was a piece of information that could be used. Saying exactly what hurt handed the room something specific to react to, so vagueness kept the target smaller. Humor deflected the moment before it needed a name. A borrowed expression supplied something to look at without a real feeling going on record. Compartmentalizing let one part of an emotional life keep running without ever being reconciled with the rest.

Inside a home where emotional needs went unregistered, the calculation was different and it arrived at the same place. Developing sharp language for feelings that nobody was going to respond to had no payoff worth the effort. The vagueness matched the amount of response actually available.

Lists deserve their own mention, because they are the substitute people are least likely to recognize as one. Writing out what is wrong in bullet form is a structured outlet, and it does real work. It also externalizes the content of a feeling without requiring the harder, more exposing act of saying "I feel small right now" and owning it as a state you are in. A list can be handed over and defended point by point. A feeling can only be admitted.

Intelligence and self-awareness sit outside this entirely. What got built was a system, assembled piece by piece, designed to avoid the specific costs that precision used to carry.

Is This Emotional Numbness, or Something Else?

Worth checking, because five fairly different things produce a similar-looking silence and the useful response to each one differs completely.

What it might be How it actually works What tells it apart from this
Emotional numbness The signal itself is missing or dampened below noticing Ask whether "something is off" feels true with conviction. In this pattern it usually does. In numbness it often does not reach that far
Emotional suppression A feeling is identified, then deliberately pushed down Suppression knows what it is pushing down. This pattern never got that far, so there is nothing yet to push
Emotional invalidation You were taught that a feeling, once named, was not allowed Invalidation sits later in the chain. It punishes an identified feeling. This one stalls before identification happens
Introversion or privacy A preference about how much to share, with the label intact inside Alone, in a notebook, does an accurate word arrive? A private person generally has one and chooses to keep it
A plain vocabulary gap Not enough words available for the states you have A feelings wheel would fix a pure vocabulary gap in a week. If the substitutes keep running anyway, the habit is the thing in charge

The row that matters most is the first. Numbness is the absence of the signal, and this pattern has the opposite shape: a signal loud enough to be uncomfortable, with a labeling layer too thin to convert it. People confuse the two constantly, including in therapy rooms, and the confusion sends them toward the wrong exercises. Invalidation is the other frequent mix-up, and it is related without being the same, since being taught that a feeling was unacceptable assumes the feeling got named first.

Where Does It Show Up When You Would Not Call It a Naming Problem?

In seven places, and most people recognize themselves in two or three of them before the rest come into focus.

Where it shows up What it looks like from outside What it is standing in for
Lists A written itemization of everything that is wrong, prepared before a hard conversation "I feel hurt", said out loud and owned
Humor A joke landing the half second a conversation turns serious, every time The name of the feeling that was about to be required
Mimicry Your face matching a friend's reaction to news before you have checked your own An expression generated from what is actually there
Opinions in groups Holding back in a meeting, then offering a position rather than a reaction A feeling that has no clear label yet, so even an opinion feels exposing
Compartments A work self, a family self, and a friend self that never have to agree One coherent felt state that would have to be reconciled
Appearance New hair, a rearranged room, a different style, arriving in a difficult month An internal shift with no other channel available to travel through
Hyper-vigilance Reading everybody else's mood in the room with unusual accuracy Attention that never got pointed inward, because outward was where the danger was

The hyper-vigilance row is the one that surprises people. Plenty of people in this pattern are unusually good at emotions, as long as the emotions belong to somebody else. Growing up somewhere unpredictable makes reading a room a survival skill, and it gets practiced daily for years while the inward-facing version of the same skill gets almost no use at all.

Why Does "How Does That Make You Feel" Land Like an Impossible Question?

Because it assumes the exact capacity that is missing, and it assumes it can be produced on demand in under two seconds.

The question is asked kindly, usually by somebody who has never had this problem and therefore has no idea it is a problem. For them the word is simply there, the way a first language is there. Being asked to produce one on request when the labeling layer is thin feels roughly like being asked to name a colour you can see but have never been told the word for. The frustration that follows generally has nothing to do with the person asking.

There is a version of the answer that works, and it involves changing what you are answering with. Report the sensation and the timing rather than the label: something tightened in my chest when you said that, it started about ten minutes ago, and I do not have the word for it yet. That is a complete and honest answer. It gives the other person something real to work with, and it lets you practise the first half of the skill without waiting until the second half is finished.

What Does the Other End of This Actually Look Like?

Considerably less impressive than people expect, and specific enough that you would recognize it on a Thursday.

Every one of these patterns has two ends, and a description of the wounded end alone leaves you with an accurate account of your problem and nowhere to walk. So here is the other end, translated out of abstraction.

The unnamed end The named end What that looks like on an ordinary Thursday
Four seconds of blank, then "fine" A word arriving within a few seconds Someone asks how the meeting went and you say you felt dismissed, before you have thought about it
Two or three words covering everything Each state getting its own name Disappointment stops getting filed under the same generic bad as grief, anger, and shame
An answer made of facts An answer made of a feeling Asked how something landed, you say how it landed rather than recounting what happened
The joke arriving automatically Humor becoming a choice You can tell the difference between something being funny and a feeling needing somewhere to go
A borrowed expression Your own expression You check your own face before you check anyone else's
A list handed over A list plus one sentence The bullet points stay, and at the bottom you write the actual feeling
Nobody able to help Somebody able to respond A named feeling gives another person something specific to answer, instead of a signal they can only guess at
Work self, home self, friend self One state you can describe The same honest answer would work in all three rooms

Read the right-hand column carefully, because it is deliberately unremarkable. Nobody in it is making a confession or having a breakthrough. The named end of this pattern mostly consists of a person who can answer a question about their own week accurately on the first attempt.

The old substitutes do not vanish. Humor still shows up, lists still get made, and under enough stress the borrowed expression still arrives first. What changes is that they stop being the only available response.

How Do You Actually Build the Vocabulary?

By running the mirroring cycle yourself, slowly and on purpose, in rooms where a wrong answer costs nothing.

  1. Locate it in the body before you look for the word. Chest, throat, stomach, jaw, hands. The sensation is the part that is reliably available, so start where the data is rather than where the language is.
  2. Commit to a word that might be wrong. The approximate label is the entry point. "Annoyed" is a fine first guess even when the truer answer turns out to be "let down".
  3. Then correct it. "I guess I am annoyed? No, more like let down." That sentence, said aloud or written, is one complete repetition of the cycle that never ran often enough in childhood. The correction is where the learning lives.
  4. Keep the list and add a line at the bottom. No need to give up a habit that works. Write the bullet points, then write one sentence naming what you feel about the whole page. The list gets you to the door, and the sentence goes through it.
  5. Catch the joke once a week and reverse it. You will feel the swerve arriving a beat before it does. Say "actually, let me try to say what that was" and take another run at it. Once a week is a serious practice.
  6. Check your own face first. When news lands in a group, take one second to notice what happened in your body before you look at anybody else. Mimicry is fast, so this only works if you get there first.
  7. Practise in calm rooms. Pressure takes the vocabulary before it takes anything else, so an argument is the worst possible place to learn this. Build it on ordinary Thursdays and it will eventually turn up on the hard days.
  8. Say one of them to another person. Naming it privately builds the label. Naming it out loud to somebody safe is what disconnects the old cost from the sentence, and that is a separate piece of work.

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. The thing I was slowest to notice was how much of this is a skill rather than a temperament. For years I could give an intelligent account of why I was the way I was and still go blank when somebody asked what I was feeling right then, and I read that blank as evidence there was nothing there. Where it sits today is worth saying plainly: the words come faster than they did, they still go missing under real pressure, and I would rather tell you that than sell a finished version of something nobody finishes.

Is any of this comfortable? No. It is slow and freaking repetitive, and it produces sentences that sound clumsy for months before they sound like yours.

What Do You Say to the People Who Keep Asking?

Say the true thing about the gap, once, in plain words, and watch how much it changes.

Most partners, friends, and family who ask about feelings and get silence back conclude one of two things: that you have no feelings, or that you have feelings you are deliberately withholding from them. Both readings are painful for them and both are wrong, and neither one gets corrected by more silence.

The sentence that works is short. Something like: I do feel things, and I am genuinely slow at finding words for them, and it is something I am working on. Then give them the version you can offer, which is the sensation and the timing. Ask them to give you a few minutes rather than a few seconds, and to ask again later in the evening if nothing arrives the first time.

There is one thing worth watching for in yourself here. Being with somebody who guesses your feelings for you is an enormous relief, because they do the missing step on your behalf and it can feel like being deeply understood. That closeness is real. It also means the skill stays outsourced. Keep the person, and do some of the naming yourself anyway, badly, in the low-stakes moments where nothing depends on getting it right.

How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Longer than a conversation, and the honest answer runs at two speeds.

Recognition arrives quickly. Within a couple of weeks of paying attention, most people can catch the joke as it leaves their mouth, notice the list being drafted, and feel the blank arrive on cue. Catching it counts, even on the days you do it anyway. Watching yourself deflect while knowing precisely what you are doing feels uncomfortable and it is a genuine step.

The vocabulary takes months. This behaves like learning a second language rather than having an insight, and it moves at the pace of accumulated repetitions instead of the pace of understanding. Somewhere in the first month or two the words start turning up during conversations rather than in the car afterward. Holding up in an argument, when the old system runs at full volume, takes longer again.

Is that frustrating? Yes, and I would rather say so than offer a faster route that does not exist. The people who actually shift this are the ones still doing two-line entries in a phone note in month four, long after the idea stopped feeling interesting.

What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Try to Explain Yourself to Anyone?

Set three alarms on your phone, spread across tomorrow, and give yourself ninety seconds at each one. Write two lines in a note. The first line says where in your body something is happening: chest, throat, stomach, jaw, hands. The second line is the nearest word you have for it, and it is allowed to be wrong. If a better word turns up a moment later, write that one underneath the first. Three alarms, two lines each, four days.

At the end of the four days you will have twelve entries. Read them in one sitting. Most people find two things they were not expecting: the same two or three words doing all of the work, and a handful of second guesses that were noticeably more accurate than the first ones. That gap between the first word and the second is the skill you are building, and starting on it costs you about six minutes a day.

Naming Your Feelings Questions

Why can't I name what I'm feeling?

Because naming is a separate skill from feeling, and it usually never got built. The sensation shows up on schedule while the word that fits it does not, so the honest answer to a direct question comes out as 'I don't know' or 'stressed, I guess'. Accurate labels get learned by having your feelings named accurately for you, mostly in childhood. Where that mirroring was unreliable or largely absent, the labeling layer stayed thin.

Is alexithymia a form of autism?

They are different things. Alexithymia is a research term for persistent difficulty identifying and describing feelings, measured with questionnaires rather than handed out as a standalone diagnosis. Autism is a developmental diagnosis made by a clinician after assessment. Researchers have studied an overlap between the two, and plenty of people show one without the other. No article can tell you whether either applies to you. A qualified professional can.

What are the main signs of alexithymia?

The features usually described are difficulty identifying which feeling is present, difficulty describing feelings to other people, a thinking style anchored in external events and practical detail, and limited use of inner imagery or imagination. Bodily sensations often get noticed clearly while the emotional label stays out of reach. These are traits measured on a scale rather than a yes or no verdict, and only a qualified professional can assess where someone sits on it.

What is it called when you can't label your emotions?

The usual word is alexithymia, from Greek roots meaning roughly 'no words for feeling'. It describes difficulty identifying and describing emotions, and it is measured as a trait on a spectrum rather than diagnosed as a condition of its own. Milder everyday versions are extremely common and carry no clinical label at all. If you can tell something is happening in your body while no word arrives for it, that is the territory.

What's it called when you can't describe how you feel?

Most people land on the word alexithymia, though the everyday version has no formal name and needs none. In plain terms what is happening is a gap between a sensation and a word precise enough to say out loud. The feeling is present and the label is missing. Describing it that way tends to be more useful day to day than any term, because it points straight at the part that can be practiced.

Why can't I name my emotions even when I have plenty of time to think about it?

Because time is not the missing ingredient. Thinking about a feeling for an hour produces analysis, a good account of the situation, and a list of what other people did wrong. The labeling step happens closer to the body than to the reasoning, so the move that actually works is to notice where the sensation sits, chest, throat or stomach, then try a word against it. Extra thinking tends to route around that step.

How do I name my feelings?

Start smaller than feels satisfying. Three times a day, notice where in your body something is happening, then put the nearest word to it out loud or in writing, accepting that it may be wrong. Try a second word that might fit better. That correction is the actual mechanism. A feelings list or wheel works well as a supply of words, and the words only become usable through repeated attempts in real time.

How do I name my emotions in the middle of an argument?

Buy yourself the four seconds. Say out loud that you need a moment to work out what you are feeling, which is true and far more useful than a guess delivered fast. Then name the sensation before the story: tight chest, hot face, heavy stomach. One plain sentence is enough. Precision under pressure arrives late in this skill, so an approximate word said calmly beats a perfect word found next Tuesday.

What are the signs of a lack of emotional clarity?

Common signs include going blank when asked how you feel, answering with facts or opinions instead of feelings, reaching for the same two or three words across very different states, drafting a list of what is wrong rather than saying what hurt, joking the second a conversation turns emotional, and copying other people's reactions before checking your own. Knowing something is off while being unable to say what is the clearest one.

What causes a lack of emotional clarity?

Two childhood conditions produce it, and either one alone is enough. In the first, emotional responses at home were unpredictable, so a child learned to manage the output of feelings rather than name them, since a precisely named feeling gave the room something specific to react to. In the second, emotional presence simply went unregistered, so nobody consistently asked what was felt or reflected a word back to be checked against.

Why do I have so little clarity about my own emotions compared to other people?

Usually because they got more practice. Anyone whose feelings were regularly named out loud by an adult, and corrected when the word missed, accumulated thousands of small repetitions before adulthood. Someone whose household ran on unpredictability or on quiet absence got very few. From the outside the result looks like a personality difference. In practice it behaves like a skill difference, which is the better of the two, because skills respond to repetition.

How do you stop being unclear about your own emotions?

By treating it as language learning rather than insight. Pick three moments a day, locate the sensation in your body, and commit to a word even when it feels approximate. Correct it when a better one turns up. Do this in low-stakes settings first, alone or with one safe person, because precision collapses under pressure long after it works in calm. Months of small repetitions move this. A single realization rarely does.

Why can't I describe what I'm feeling, even to myself?

Describing a feeling is a mirrored skill. A child learns that a particular word fits a particular sensation by having an adult name it accurately and often, then correct the guess when it misses. Where that mirroring ran on unsafe terms or barely ran at all, the sensation still arrives in adulthood with nothing attached to it. Privacy makes no difference to this, which is why the blank turns up even when you are alone.

Is this the same as being emotionally numb?

No. Numbness means the signal itself is missing or dampened below noticing, so there is very little to report. This pattern has the opposite shape. The signal is present, sometimes uncomfortably so, and the gap sits in finding a word for it. A useful test is whether you can say 'something is off' with real conviction. People in this pattern usually can. People who are numb often cannot reach even that much.

Why do I make lists instead of just saying how I feel?

A list externalizes what is wrong without exposing you. Itemizing grievances, tasks, or everything that went badly can be handed to somebody and defended point by point, and none of it requires the more exposing sentence 'I feel small right now'. It is a genuine attempt to communicate, routed around the naming step. Keeping the list and adding one feeling word at the bottom of it works well as a bridge.

Why do I crack a joke the second a conversation gets emotional?

Defensive humor tends to arrive a half second before a feeling would have to be named, which is exactly what makes it so effective. In a household where direct emotional expression could be met with volatility, deflecting was cheaper than being precise, and the habit outlives the household by decades. Notice that the joke is fast, usually faster than any decision to make it. That speed is the tell.

Why do I copy other people's reactions instead of having my own?

Emotional mimicry develops as a way to blend in and avoid conflict where a wrong emotional read carried real cost. Matching an available expression is faster and safer than generating one from scratch, especially when generating one would require a label you do not have. From the inside it can feel like performing. Underneath it there is usually something real that simply has not been located yet.

Is it normal to go blank when a therapist asks me to name a feeling in my body?

It is extremely common with this pattern, and it is rarely resistance. The sensation can be real and even strong while the naming layer that would let you answer quickly was never built out. Saying that plainly to the person asking usually helps more than guessing, because it changes the question they ask next. Blanking is the gap itself showing up on cue rather than evidence of failure.

Why do I feel embarrassed saying 'I feel hurt' out loud, even alone?

Because a precisely named feeling used to be usable information. In an unpredictable house, saying exactly what hurt handed the room a target, so vagueness kept you smaller. The embarrassment now is the old cost still attached to a sentence that no longer carries it. Saying the words aloud in an empty room, on purpose, a few dozen times is one of the faster ways to separate the two.

Could I just have a low emotional IQ?

Not by definition. What is thin here is one specific mirrored skill, the naming of internal states, and it sits alongside empathy, insight, and social intelligence that are often fully intact. Many people in this pattern read other people accurately and fast, which is part of how they learned to cope. The gap faces inward, and it responds to practice in a way a fixed trait never would.

Why do I know something is 'off' but can't say what?

That is the pattern in its most literal form. Detecting that something has changed and producing an accurate word for it are two different skills, and only the first one tends to build itself. The vague signal is real data, so treat it as a starting point. Where is it in your body, when did it start, and what happened in the hour before it are three questions that usually narrow it down.

Does using a feelings wheel actually fix this?

It supplies the missing words, which is genuinely useful, and it does not install the habit of reaching for them. The skill gets built by repetition under real conditions: noticing, choosing a word, getting it wrong, adjusting. Reading the wheel once changes very little. Keeping it open and using it three times a day for a month changes a great deal, because at that point the wheel has become practice.

Why does my partner say I'm 'checked out' when I don't feel checked out at all?

Compartmentalizing separates parts of an emotional life so they never have to be reconciled, and from the outside that reads as absence. Something real is usually still running underneath, just not accessible as one coherent feeling and therefore not available to communicate. Saying 'something is happening and I do not have the word for it yet' is more accurate than either of you guessing, and it lands very differently.

Is this just introversion or being a private person?

They look similar from the outside and work differently underneath. Introversion is a preference about how much to share and how much social contact costs you, with the internal label usually available. This pattern is a gap in generating the label at all, private or not. Quick check: alone, in a notebook, with nobody watching, does an accurate word arrive? A private person generally has one and keeps it.

Why do I change my hair, my style, or rearrange my room when something's bothering me?

Changing something external works as a substitute channel. When an internal shift has no word attached to it, altering appearance or surroundings carries the signal instead, to you as much as to anyone else. It is a real communication in a language that skips the naming step. Treating the impulse as information helps: something has changed, and this is currently the only route open for saying so.

Will my partner think I don't have feelings if I can't name them?

It is a common misread and worth heading off directly. This pattern involves a gap between having a feeling and finding language for it, so silence gets read as indifference by somebody who has never had that problem. Explaining the mechanism once, calmly, tends to change how your quiet gets interpreted. Asking for a slower question, or for time to answer, also helps more than most people expect.

Why do I feel like I'm performing an emotion instead of having one?

That sensation is close to what mimicry produces from the inside. Adopting an available expression fills the social gap while the actual feeling stays unlocated, and the mismatch registers as performance. Something real is usually there underneath. A useful move in the moment is to let the expression happen, then quietly check the body afterward for what was going on while your face was busy.

Could this be a sign of something more serious, like alexithymia?

Possibly, and only a qualified professional can say. Alexithymia describes a more severe and persistent version of the same difficulty, measured on a scale. The everyday pattern described here is milder, situational, and often traceable to specific childhood conditions. If the gap feels total rather than partial, holds in every setting including when you are alone, and has been that way for as long as you can remember, take it to somebody qualified.

Why do I get frustrated with people who ask 'but how does that make you feel'?

Because the question assumes a skill that was never built, so it lands as an impossible demand delivered in a friendly voice. The frustration is usually aimed at the gap rather than at the person asking. Telling them what you can offer instead helps: where the sensation is, what happened just before it, and the nearest approximate word. Most people asking will happily work with that.

Is it my parents' fault I can't name my feelings?

Describing a mechanism and assigning blame are different activities. Either inconsistent emotional responses or a chronic absence of emotional registration will produce this, and both are usually passed down by people who never had the words either. Understanding how it formed tends to be more useful than a verdict, mainly because the mechanism points straight at what to practice now. The repair is yours to run regardless of the origin.

How long does it take to actually build this vocabulary?

Months of active, low-stakes practice rather than a single conversation or realization. Expect the first few weeks to feel clumsy and approximate. Somewhere in the first month or two, words start arriving during conversations instead of hours afterward. Holding up under real pressure, in an argument or a hard moment at work, usually takes longer again. This behaves like learning a second language, and the timeline is comparable.

Why do I feel silly using a feeling word that turns out to be wrong?

Getting it wrong and correcting it is the mechanism by which the skill gets built, so the clumsy attempts are the practice working. 'I guess I am annoyed? No, more like let down' is a complete and successful repetition. Nobody learns a language without a stretch of sounding approximate. The silliness fades at roughly the pace the accuracy improves, and never quite completely, which turns out to be fine.

Can I have this and still be very emotionally expressive, like crying easily?

Yes, and the combination is common. This pattern concerns precise verbal labeling rather than emotional intensity or expressiveness. Someone can cry readily, laugh loudly, react hard to a film, and still be unable to say in words what any of it was. Visible emotion and named emotion are separate capacities, which is how 'you are clearly feeling something' and 'I have no idea what' end up both being true.

Why do I feel closer to people who guess my feelings for me than to people who ask me to name them?

Because someone else supplying the label does the missing step for you, and the relief of that can feel like being deeply understood. The closeness is real and it carries a cost, since the skill stays outsourced and never gets built through your own use of it. Keeping those people close while naming things yourself sometimes, badly, in low-stakes moments, gets you the connection and the capacity both.
