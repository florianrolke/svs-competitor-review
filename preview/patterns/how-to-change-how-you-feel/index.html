How do you change how you feel, on purpose?

The short answer

You change how you feel by changing what produces the feeling: how you are using your body, where your attention is pointed, and the language running underneath. All three are live at every moment, and moving one while the other two stay put is why a better thought fades in minutes. Moved together, a state can shift in under a minute. Changing your default takes months.

A state does its talking in sentences like these. Notice which one arrives in your own voice.

  • "I replay conversations over and over, worrying about how I came across."
  • "If I let my guard down, I'll just get hurt again."
  • "Deep down, I don't feel like I truly belong anywhere."
  • "I must keep my problems to myself; otherwise, I'm a burden."

Lines like those run underneath a mood all day, settling what your morning means before you have had any say in it.

How the seminar works with the state that decides your day before you notice it

You sit down at your desk at nine in the morning and you already know, before you have opened a single tab, that today is a write-off. Nothing has actually happened yet. The email that would confirm it has not arrived, the conversation that would prove it has not been had, and the verdict is already in.

A low state presents itself as an accurate report on how bad things genuinely are right now, delivered with all the authority of a fact. That felt accuracy is what makes it so hard to move, and it is the first thing worth understanding, because everything else on this page depends on seeing it clearly. What follows is the machinery underneath a state, why the obvious fix keeps failing, and what you can do about it in the next two minutes.

Why Does a Bad Mood Feel Like an Accurate Report?

Because the state you are in selects what gets noticed and what it means before you consciously evaluate anything, so the read arrives already formed and feels like it came from the situation.

Watch how it runs. Your state decides which meaning gets assigned to whatever is in front of you. The meaning decides what you do next. What you do produces an outcome. The outcome arrives back as evidence, and the evidence feeds the state that started the whole thing. Five nodes in a loop, running several times an hour, mostly below the level where you would notice it happening.

A flat, depleted state reads a stalled reply as avoidance, a missed deadline as a pattern, a curt tone as confirmation of something wrong with you. The identical facts, met from a state built five minutes earlier out of different posture, different focus and different internal language, read as an ordinary Tuesday on which three people happened to be busy. The situation held completely still. What moved was which meaning got assigned, and it moved before any conscious evaluation of the situation had a chance to happen.

There is no internal marker separating "this is genuinely how bad things are" from "this is my current state talking". Both are experienced identically from the inside, which is why the same person, an hour apart, can feel equally certain they are seeing clearly in two incompatible directions. The certainty you feel about a low read is the loop checking itself and finding, correctly, that the interpretation matches the state that produced it.

Which is also why arguing with a low state loses. You argue on its terms, using the vocabulary it selected, about the evidence it chose to hand you. The argument feels rigorous and the deck was cut before you sat down.

What Are the Three Levers a State Actually Runs On?

Three, and every one of them is running right now, whether or not you are operating it on purpose.

How the body is being used. Posture, the depth and rate of your breathing, muscular tension in the jaw, the shoulders, the hands, and whether you are moving at all. Nobody sits in an open, upright, mobile body and produces a convincing sense of hopelessness. The body is one of the ingredients of the feeling, and it is supplying its share continuously.

Where attention is pointed. Out of everything available to notice in any given minute, a small fraction gets selected and the rest is discarded. A state narrows the aperture, then hands you what it found through it and calls that the situation. Two people in the same meeting genuinely leave with different meetings.

The language running underneath. The label you attach to what is happening, and the sentence repeating below the surface, usually in words too familiar to hear. "This is a disaster" and "this is a Tuesday" are different instructions, and the rest of the system treats them as instructions rather than as descriptions.

The lever What it is doing while you read this How to check it in ten seconds What happens if you move only this one
Body Holding a posture, a breath rate, a level of jaw and shoulder tension Notice the length of your exhale and where your shoulders are sitting A real shift that clears within minutes, with nothing stored in it
Attention Selecting a fraction of what is available and discarding the rest Ask what you have checked or replayed three times already today A short interruption, with the state waiting intact underneath it
Language Running a label and a repeating sentence, mostly below hearing Catch the exact words of the last thing you said to yourself A sentence you have to defend consciously until it loses

All three are live simultaneously, which changes what a mood actually is. A mood is a configuration held in place by three inputs at once, rebuilt continuously rather than switched on once and left running. That is a stranger idea than it looks, because nobody experiences those three as inputs they are operating. They are experienced as a faithful description of how things are.

It is worth separating three words that get used interchangeably. A state is what is running right now. A mood is the same configuration held long enough that it starts to feel like the baseline you are reporting from. A default state is the one you return to under load, regardless of what is going on around you. Same mechanism, three timescales, and only the last one takes months to move.

When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, before any technique, because this line matters more than everything underneath it.

If a low state has stopped lifting at all, if hopelessness has been sitting there for months, if there is any thought of harming yourself however quickly it passes, that belongs with a licensed professional now. The same holds alongside an addiction that has started organising your week, a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, or a bereavement in the last few weeks. What follows here is a practice for the ordinary version of a bad state, the Tuesday that went grey at ten in the morning for no reason anybody could name. It sits alongside professional care rather than in place of it, and asking in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve.

There is a second situation worth naming plainly. If your state is being produced by somebody else's behaviour, if your day is spent tracking a mood in the house so that nothing goes off, the levers will help you cope and they will leave the actual problem exactly where it is. That situation has its own page.

Why Does Thinking Positive Fail So Reliably?

Because almost every version of it moves one lever and leaves the other two running at full volume.

Think positive. Reframe it. Find the silver lining. Repeat the affirmation. Talk yourself into gratitude. Every one of those is an instruction aimed at language, and as an instruction it is perfectly reasonable. It arrives into a body that has stayed exactly as it was, shoulders dropped, breath shallow and high in the chest, and into an attention still scanning for whatever the low state was primed to find. Those two carry on manufacturing the original state directly underneath the new sentence.

So the sentence has to be held. Consciously, continuously, against a body and a focus that are both voting the other way, which is exhausting in a way nobody warns you about. Five minutes later, mid-meeting, back at the desk, your attention drifts for a second, the old configuration reasserts, and it drags the interpretation back with it.

Mechanically, a one-lever intervention lost to a two-lever holdout. What it gets read as is "affirmations don't work for me" or "I'm just not a positive person", which is a verdict about your character standing in for a description of something that was never actually attempted. Working on the sentence itself is worth doing, and it works far better with the other two levers moving.

The reverse failure is quieter and just as common. Go for a walk, stretch, breathe properly, and the state genuinely lifts. Nothing is wrong with that, and the body is the fastest lever there is. Then the state clears on its own, as every state does, and because nothing new was stored while it was up, the old interpretations are sitting exactly where you left them. A good hour, and Monday still means what it meant.

What Do the Sentences Underneath a Stuck State Sound Like?

Like a set of rules about what is safe, so old that they stopped registering as thoughts a long time ago.

These are the lines people actually run, in their own phrasing rather than a tidied version of it. They recur with an unnerving consistency across people who have never met and whose lives look nothing alike. Read them slowly. What matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours, word for word, with no translation required.

The vigilance set, which keeps a body braced for something that has not happened:

  • "I'm always preparing for the moment when things fall apart."
  • "I have to be vigilant for any signs of rejection or abandonment."
  • "I tend to overthink every little thing in relationships, looking for signs they're pulling away."
  • "It's safer to keep people at a distance."

The worth set, which decides in advance what any piece of feedback is going to mean:

  • "I often feel like I'm not good enough, no matter how hard I try."
  • "My worth is tied to how much others need me."
  • "The moment I'm not useful anymore, they'll find someone else."
  • "I need to be perfect to be loved."
  • "I often feel lonely, even when I'm with someone."

The concealment set, which governs what is allowed to be visible in a room:

  • "I have to hide my true feelings; otherwise, I'll be too much for them."
  • "I sometimes feel like I'm just too much for anyone to handle."
  • "Being vulnerable is too risky; it's easier to keep a facade."
  • "It's difficult for me to ask for help because I fear it'll push people away."

Two more are worth quoting in full, because they show the language lever doing something more precise than general gloom. The first is a rule about which feelings are permitted in which rooms:

"I am so in pain, but if I let my children see that it will make them so upset, so I can't be upset."

The second is a state being defended on purpose, by somebody who has worked out exactly what it does for them:

"I don't want to stop being angry, because if I don't get angry then I keep getting taken advantage of, and then no one will pay attention to me and I will make no progress at all."

Both of those come back later on this page, because they describe the two hardest cases: the state that is being hidden, and the state that is being chosen.

Is That a State Change, or Is It Something Else?

Five fairly different moves get called the same thing, and only one of them changes what a situation means to you.

The move What it changes What it leaves running How you can tell within the hour
Thinking positive, reframing, affirmations The internal sentence Posture, breath, and everything attention is scanning for The sentence needs defending, and it fades the moment you stop holding it
Suppression, holding it together The face and the voice The entire physiology underneath, at full volume You look calm and you are more tired afterward than the situation explains
Distraction: scrolling, a show, a drink Attention, temporarily Body and internal language, completely untouched The state is waiting, unchanged, the second the distraction stops
Waiting it out Nothing, deliberately Everything, until the state clears by itself It does pass, and every meaning it assigned on the way through stays assigned
A deliberate three-lever change Body, attention and language together Very little The same facts read differently, and you can say what changed about them

Suppression deserves the extra attention, because from outside it is indistinguishable from a genuine shift and the person doing it often cannot tell either. A steady face and a level voice laid over a body that is still fully switched on works as a mask. It is measurably more effortful to hold than the state it covers, and the bill turns up hours later as a fatigue that sleep does not touch. Sustained for long enough, the same move flattens everything else along with it. What that flatness turns into has its own page.

The honest test for whether you changed a state or hid one has nothing to do with how you look from the outside. Check whether the same facts now mean something different to you. If the stalled reply, the flat text, the number that came in under still mean precisely what they meant twenty minutes ago, whatever you did was cosmetic.

Where Does This Show Up When You Would Not Call It a Mood Problem?

In six places, and most people recognise themselves in one or two of them before they see the rest.

Walking into a hard conversation already braced, jaw tight, breath shallow The drive over was spent rehearsing the worst version, so the body arrived configured for a fight nobody has started "I'm always preparing for the moment when things fall apart."
Treating every difficult feeling as a problem to be managed Suppression filed as discipline, holding the surface steady while the charge underneath drains hours that never get accounted for "I have to hide my true feelings; otherwise, I'll be too much for them."

Take the version most people recognise fastest. Somebody spends the twenty-minute drive to a difficult meeting running the worst version of it in full: what the other person will say, what they will say back, the tone, the face, the silence afterward. They arrive with a tight jaw and shallow breath and read that tension as diagnostic, as proof the conversation really is dangerous. The tension was built by the rehearsal. Body, attention and language all ran the fight before anybody had said a word, and the meeting inherited a state that was fully assembled before it started. What changed it, when it changed, was two minutes of deliberately different posture and breathing before the door. The move was physical rather than intellectual, and it altered which version of the conversation felt available once the meeting actually began.

A second version is harder to see, because it looks like competence. Somebody has spent years treating every difficult feeling in their household as a problem to be managed rather than a signal to be read, and describes things at home as fine. The tension is real, constant, and never named, which is exactly why it never resolves. It drains hours that never feel like they belonged to the person living them. What cracked it open was a single question: how long does one hour feel in that specific room, compared with an hour anywhere else. They worked out the answer themselves, and a cost that had been chronic enough to become invisible turned into a specific number that could no longer be filed as nothing.

A third shows up in people who are, by every external measure, doing extremely well. Every target hit, close to nothing felt on hitting it, and the next target approached already flat. The achievement was being used as the mechanism for state, so each win buys a shorter window than the last and the response on offer is always a bigger target. Walking through the actual morning after the next win, in detail, tends to settle it: nothing arrives in that scene either. A state has to be produced directly, on purpose, independent of what any outcome does or fails to deliver. What the wins were quietly being asked to supply is worth naming separately.

Why Does the Good State You Build Fade by Lunchtime?

Because a state clears on its own regardless of how it was produced, and almost nobody does anything with it while it is up.

That is the step most methods skip entirely. The combined three-lever move is fast, seconds to a couple of minutes for a genuine shift, and it is repeatable on demand once you have run it a few times. What is slow is making anything stick, and the moment that decides it is the peak.

While a deliberately built state is up, it is assigning meanings just as reliably as the low one was. That is your window, and the target for it is the specific thing the old state kept ruling on: the stalled reply, the conversation you have been dreading, the number that came in under, the thing your father said in April. Assign the new meaning right there, out loud or on paper, while the state is high. Skip that and the state passes, nothing was stored, and the old interpretation reloads by default for the simple reason that it is still the only one on file.

Expect the first several attempts to land as nothing much. They are repetitions. An automatic sequence degrades through repeated failure to complete, rather than through one memorable interruption, which means the tenth ordinary attempt does more work than the first dramatic one. The mechanics of interrupting an automatic sequence are worth reading in full.

There is a related trap for anyone who has been to something intense. The state at a big event is genuinely new, and nothing about it gets rehearsed inside the actual rooms, voices and mornings that cue the old one. Three weeks later the old configuration is back on schedule, and the conclusion arrives that the change was never real in the first place. What actually happened is that a state gets stored with its retrieval cues, and an unchanged environment keeps supplying the old ones. The unglamorous work of moving something from the week into the year has a name and a method.

How Do You Change Your State in the Next Two Minutes?

By moving all three levers inside the same minute, deliberately, before you feel like it.

  1. Change the body first, and change it more than feels reasonable. Stand up. Walk. Lengthen your exhale until it is clearly longer than your inhale. Unclench your jaw and your hands. The body is the fastest lever because operating it requires no thinking your way anywhere first, and it works while your mind is still arguing with you.
  2. Move attention onto something specific and current. Telling yourself to think about something else is an instruction nobody can follow. Pick a target instead: the actual next action for the next ten minutes, one object in the room you have never looked at properly, the question you would ask if this were somebody else's situation. Attention moves toward a target far more easily than away from one.
  3. Change the sentence, and say it out loud if the room allows it. Catch the exact words running underneath, which usually takes one honest second, then replace them with something you would actually defend to a friend. Saying it aloud matters more than it sounds, because speech recruits breath and posture, which quietly gives you two levers for the price of one.
  4. Do all three inside sixty seconds. Order matters less than overlap. Three levers moved in the same minute hold each other up. Three moved across an afternoon behave like three separate one-lever attempts and fail the same way.
  5. Use the top of it. While the state is up, say what the triggering thing now means, in one sentence. This is the storage step, it takes about eight seconds, and it is the one that gets skipped almost every time.
  6. Run it before you need it rather than after. Two minutes in the car park before a hard conversation is worth twenty minutes of recovery afterward. Nearly everyone runs this in the wrong direction, arriving braced and repairing later.
  7. Practise it in the room where you actually lose it. A shift rehearsed only in calm conditions has never been tested. Do it at your own desk at nine in the morning, in the kitchen at six in the evening, in the car outside your parents' house, in the five minutes before the weekly call you dread.
  8. Count repetitions rather than results. Ten deliberate shifts inside real situations move more than one perfect one on a good day. Overthinking in particular needs the body lever first, because attention will not redirect while the physiology keeps generating fresh things to replay.

Is any of this comfortable at first? No. It is mechanical, slightly embarrassing, and freaking repetitive, and it is exactly the kind of thing that holds when a single dramatic decision does not.

Don't judge yourself if the first few barely move anything. Running a shift while a state is at full volume and watching it shift almost nothing is a completely normal step, and it still counts as a repetition. The count is what does the work.

What If a Bad State Is the Only Thing That Has Ever Worked?

Then giving it up feels like handing back the only tool that has reliably produced anything, which is a fair reading of the situation and exactly why arguments against it slide off.

Somebody who returns deliberately to anger whenever a situation calls for standing up for themselves is usually in more control than they look. They are reaching for the one state they trust to produce attention or movement from other people, and it does produce it, in the short term, reliably enough to keep getting chosen. The cost lands somewhere else and later: on the relationship, on the body, on becoming the person everybody else manages carefully. It rarely gets weighed against the benefit, because the benefit arrives in seconds and the cost accumulates over years.

Being told to stop being angry moves nobody in this position, and it deserves not to, because it asks them to hand back their only working tool and offers nothing in exchange. What does move it is two questions, asked in that order. What is the anger actually getting you? The answers are usually specific and reasonable: being taken seriously, not being walked over, being visible at all. And then, what else could get the same result, in this particular room, with this particular person? Naming the job the state is doing turns a different state into an added tool rather than a surrender.

The same logic covers urgency, vigilance and low-grade dread. Each is a state that did real work at some point and then stayed in rotation long past its usefulness. Anger specifically, including what to do with it in the moment, is covered separately, and why a pattern keeps running long after you have understood it perfectly is worth settling alongside this.

What Does It Look Like When a Default Has Actually Moved?

Quieter than most people expect, and visible in what gets decided rather than in how anybody looks.

Every one of these is a polarity with two ends, and describing only the stuck end leaves you with an accurate account of the problem and nowhere to walk. Here is the other end, translated out of abstraction and into an ordinary Tuesday.

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. The thing I underestimated longest was how physical this is. I kept trying to think my way out of states and getting further into them, and what finally worked was cruder and far less interesting than anything I had been reading. Where it sits for me today, plainly: I catch a state before it has decided something far more often than I used to, and I still lose whole mornings to one I never noticed, and I would rather tell you that than sell a finished version of something nobody finishes.

How Long Does This Honestly Take?

Two speeds, and confusing them is where most of the discouragement comes from.

The shift itself is fast. Body, attention and language moved together produce a genuine change in under a minute, and it is available today, without preparation and without anybody's permission. Most people have felt it accidentally: one phone call that changed an entire afternoon in thirty seconds, in either direction.

The default is slow. The state you return to under load, regardless of circumstance, moves at the pace of accumulated repetitions. The honest unit is weeks to months of close to daily practice. The comparison that fits is orthodontic braces: continuous, unglamorous pressure applied over a long stretch, where no single day does anything you could point at and the sum of them moves something that felt fixed. A single dramatic push does not hold on its own, whatever it cost and however extraordinary it felt at the time.

Two things shorten the timeline. Doing the repetitions inside the actual contexts that cue the old state, because a state gets stored with its retrieval cues and an unchanged environment keeps handing them over. And storing meaning at the peak of each shift, so every repetition leaves something behind rather than clearing empty.

One thing to expect, because it surprises people and gets badly misread. A better state can pull you back toward the old one within hours, particularly after something goes well, because familiarity is cheaper to run than pleasantness in a system built to conserve effort. That pull has its own page, and it is a mechanism rather than a character flaw.

Is that frustrating? Yes, and I would rather say so than sell a faster route that does not exist. The people who actually move a default are the ones still running unremarkable repetitions months after the idea stopped being interesting to think about.

What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Try to Fix Your Mood Again?

The next time you notice your state has dropped, do nothing about it for sixty seconds and write three things down. What your body is doing right now: posture, jaw, shoulders, the length of your exhale. What your attention has been on for the last ten minutes. The exact sentence running underneath, in the words it actually uses rather than a tidied version. Then change all three, deliberately, for ninety seconds. Stand up, breathe long, pick one specific target for your attention, say a different sentence out loud. Then read back what you wrote and answer one question: does the same situation still mean what it meant two minutes ago? Whatever the answer turns out to be, you now have it from your own experience instead of from a page, and it cost you two minutes.

Questions About Changing How You Feel

What does it actually mean to change your state?

It means changing the three things producing the feeling rather than arguing with the feeling itself: how your body is being used, where your attention is pointed, and the language running underneath. All three are live every minute whether you operate them or not. Changing your state on purpose means moving them deliberately and together, before the situation demands it, and checking the result by whether the same facts now mean something different.

Which lever should I move first?

The body, in almost every case. It responds fastest, it requires no thinking your way anywhere first, and it works while your mind is still arguing with you. Once the body has moved, attention becomes possible to redirect and a different sentence becomes possible to say without it sounding absurd. Starting with the sentence, from a slumped posture and a locked focus, is the version that keeps failing on people.

Why does deciding to feel better not work?

Deciding happens entirely in language, and language is one input out of three. Your posture, your breathing and whatever your attention is locked onto stay exactly as they were, and those two keep rebuilding the old feeling underneath the decision. The decision then has to be held consciously to survive, which is tiring, and it usually loses within a few minutes of your attention wandering somewhere else.

Why do I feel worse after trying to think positive?

Because a one-lever attempt against an unchanged body and an unchanged focus tends to lose, and losing costs something. You end up carrying the original low state, plus the effort spent holding a sentence that did not survive, plus a fresh conclusion that positive thinking does not work for you. That last item is the expensive one, because it usually gets stored as a fact about your character.

Is changing your state just toxic positivity with extra steps?

No. Toxic positivity is a language-only override that requires pretending the situation is fine. Changing your state operates the body and the attention as well, and it asks nothing of you about denying what is happening. You can be in a genuinely bad situation, name it accurately, and still choose which physiology and which focus you carry while you deal with it. The facts stay. The configuration you meet them in moves.

Does changing your posture really change how you feel, or is that pseudoscience?

Posture is one of three inputs rather than the whole mechanism, which is where most of the argument comes from. Changed on its own it produces a real shift that fades quickly, so anyone testing it in isolation is right to be unimpressed. Changed alongside where attention is pointed and what you are saying to yourself, it contributes to a shift that lasts long enough to change what you decide to do next.

Can you change your state without anyone noticing?

Yes. The body lever runs quietly if you need it to: lengthen the exhale, unclench your jaw, put your weight evenly on both feet, change where your eyes rest. None of that reads as dramatic from across a table. It is less complete than the full three-lever move, and it works well enough in a meeting, a waiting room, or the two minutes before you walk into something difficult.

Why is my body tense when I know there is nothing to be afraid of?

Because the body does not check your conclusions before it runs its share of the state. It responds to what attention is doing and to what you have been rehearsing, so a morning spent running the worst version of a conversation arrives in your shoulders whether or not you believe the worst version. Knowing you are safe is a language fact. The tension is a body fact. They do not sync automatically.

Do I have to run this every single day, forever?

Every state clears on its own, so at the start yes, several times a day and deliberately. That changes as repetitions accumulate: the shift gets faster, smaller cues trigger it, and the new configuration eventually starts arriving without being asked for. What keeps the daily requirement in place longest is skipping the storage step, since a shift that leaves nothing behind has to be rebuilt from scratch every time.

Why do I end up in the same low mood no matter what changes in my life?

Because changing circumstances does not rebuild the route. Your default state is the cheapest, most practised option your system has, and a new job or a new relationship arrives on top of the same posture, the same habitual focus and the same internal sentences. Nothing about those was rehearsed differently, so the default reasserts within a few weeks and the new circumstances get read through it.

Why does my anger feel like it is protecting me?

Because it may genuinely have worked. Anger has probably got you noticed, stopped somebody taking advantage, or produced movement when nothing else did, and your system filed that result. The state is doing a real job. The difficulty is that it becomes the only state in rotation, applied to situations that needed something else, and the cost lands on the relationship and the body over years rather than in the moment.

Why did the shift I felt at a retreat or a big event wear off so fast?

Because a felt shift and an installed habit run on different timelines. The state was genuinely new, and nothing about it was rehearsed inside the specific rooms, voices and mornings that cue the old one. Back in the original context, the original cues arrive on schedule and the old configuration reassembles. That tells you where the practice needs to happen rather than whether the experience was real.

Is it bad to stay calm in front of my kids or my team when I am not calm?

Holding a steady face while the physiology underneath stays fully switched on works as a mask over the state rather than a change to it, and it costs more to hold than it looks from outside. That differs from genuinely settling yourself before you walk in, which changes what is underneath. The tell arrives ninety minutes later: a real shift leaves you tired in a normal way, a held mask leaves a fatigue that rest does not touch.

Is it manipulative to deliberately change how I feel?

No. Changing how somebody else feels without their knowledge is manipulation. Operating your own body, attention and language on purpose sits in the same category as choosing what you eat, when you sleep, or whether you walk around the block before a hard conversation. You are already running all three inputs every minute of the day. The only open question is whether you run them deliberately or by default.

Why do I sabotage myself right after I start to feel good?

Often because the good state does not match the deeply grooved default, and familiarity is cheaper to run than pleasantness. A state you have held for years takes almost no effort to produce, and a new one takes effort every single time. The pull back toward the familiar can arrive within hours of something going well, and it gets read as self-destruction when it behaves much more like gravity.

What do you do when nothing you try shifts a low state?

Check two things before concluding it cannot move. First, whether you moved more than one lever, or whether you tried a better thought against an unchanged body and an unchanged focus. Second, whether you tried it inside the actual situation that produces the state, or only in calm conditions where it was never really tested. A great deal of nothing working turns out to be one lever, tried once, in the wrong room.

Can you change your default state permanently, or does it always drift back?

It moves, at the pace of accumulated repetitions rather than in a single session. A state itself can shift in under a minute. The default you return to under load takes weeks to months of close to daily practice, closer to how orthodontic braces work than to a breakthrough. Drift back happens most under load and after gaps in practice, and it stops being total once the new configuration has enough repetitions behind it.

When should this be worked on with a professional instead of alone?

When a low state has stopped lifting at all, when hopelessness has been sitting there for months, or when there is any thought of harming yourself, however quickly it passes, that belongs with a licensed professional now. The same holds alongside an addiction, a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, or a bereavement in the last few weeks. Working the levers helps, and it does not replace care.

Can you change how you feel?

Yes, and the honest answer comes in two speeds. What you feel right now can move in under a minute, once your body, your attention and the language underneath move together instead of one at a time. What you feel by default, the state you land back in under pressure, takes weeks to months of repetition. Feelings about a specific person or event tend to follow once the state producing them has shifted.

What are 5 ways to change yourself?

Five that survive contact with an ordinary week: change what your body is doing before you try to change your mind, give attention one specific target rather than an instruction to stop thinking, catch the exact sentence running underneath and say a different one out loud, use the top of a good state to decide what something means, and practise all of it in the room where you actually lose it. Repetitions matter more than which one you start with.

What are the 7 steps in changing your life?

The number moves depending on who is selling the list, and no seven-step version survives an ordinary Tuesday. What holds up is smaller and duller. Operate your state deliberately, several times a day. Store a new meaning while the state is up rather than letting it clear empty. Run the repetitions inside the situations that produce the old state. Count repetitions rather than results, and give it months rather than a weekend.

How do you change your life when you feel stuck?

Start with the state, because stuck announces itself as a permanent fact about your circumstances while behaving like a configuration you are running. From a flat, braced state every available option looks equally pointless, so no decision made from there deserves much trust. Move the body, put attention on one concrete next action, change the sentence underneath, then look at the same situation again. The circumstances may well still need changing. The read on them gets more accurate first.

How do you change how you feel about yourself?

By changing what gets stored, rather than by arguing with the verdict directly. A state assigns meaning constantly, and years of those meanings are what a self-image is built from. Build a good state deliberately, and while it is up, say what one specific piece of evidence about you actually means, out loud or on paper. That is a single repetition. Enough of them, inside real situations, changes what your system reaches for first.

Is changing how you think the same as changing how you feel?

They overlap, and thinking is one input out of three. Change what you think while your posture, your breathing and your habitual focus stay exactly as they were, and the old feeling keeps getting rebuilt underneath the new thought until the thought gives up. Move the body and the attention alongside it, and the new thought stops needing to be defended, because the other two inputs are no longer voting against it.

Can changing your language really change your emotions?

Some, and it is the weakest of the three levers when it runs alone. The label you put on a situation and the sentence repeating below it both work as instructions, so a different sentence, run consistently, does change what you feel. Against an unchanged body and an unchanged focus it collapses first. Say it out loud where the room allows, since speech recruits breath and posture and hands you two levers at once.

What is the difference between a mood and a state?

Duration. What is running in you at this moment is a state, assembled continuously out of three inputs. Hold that configuration for a day and you would call it a mood, at which point it stops sounding like a mood and starts sounding like an accurate account of your life. Hold it for years and it becomes where you land under pressure. Same machinery at every scale, which is why the same three moves apply.

Is changing your state different from just distracting yourself?

Yes. Distraction moves attention for as long as it lasts and leaves the body and the internal language untouched, so the state is sitting there intact the moment the episode ends. A genuine change operates all three inputs, and the situation you go back to reads differently afterward. The test takes ten seconds: once the distraction stops, check whether the thing that set you off still means exactly what it meant before.

How do I stop overthinking and change my state instead?

Move the body first. Overthinking is largely an attention problem, and attention will not redirect while a braced, shallow-breathing physiology keeps producing fresh material to replay. Stand up, walk, lengthen the exhale, and only then give attention a specific target: the actual next action for the next ten minutes, or the question you would ask if this were somebody else's situation. Pointing attention at something is far easier than pulling it off something.

Why do successful people still feel empty after hitting their goals?

Because the outcome was being used as the machinery for producing a state. If winning is the only route you have ever had to a good one, each win buys a shorter window than the one before it, and the only answer on offer is a bigger target. Producing a state directly, on an ordinary morning with nothing to celebrate, is what changes how the next piece of good news lands.

How do you change how you see the world?

By changing the state you are looking from. A state narrows what gets noticed and then hands you the result as though it came straight from the world, so the view arrives feeling like a fact. Two people can walk out of one meeting having attended two different ones. New information helps, and it lands inside whatever configuration is already running, which is why reading something excellent while flat changes almost nothing.

Why can music change how you feel about a time in your life?

Because a state gets stored with whatever was around while it formed, and a song is one of the most reliable retrieval cues there is. Hearing it drops you back into an old configuration within seconds, and from inside that configuration the whole period gets read again to match. What moved is the state you are remembering from, while the period itself sat still. The same effect is usable deliberately, before an hour you know will be hard.

Can you change how you feel about someone?

Partly, and two things are worth separating. What you feel in their presence is a state, running on the same three inputs, which is why a conversation you walk into braced goes differently from the same conversation walked into settled. What you have concluded about them over years was assembled from meanings assigned inside those states, so it moves as they move. A feeling that will not shift at all is usually worth listening to rather than overriding.

Can antidepressants or hormones change how you feel about someone?

Both belong with a prescriber who knows your history, and nothing on a page can answer them for your body. Effects of medication on mood and attachment are widely discussed and worth raising directly at your next appointment. Hormonal shifts around cycles, pregnancy, postpartum and menopause change the physiology a state is partly built from. Never stop or adjust a prescription because of something you read online. This practice sits alongside medical care.

How do I stop feeling like I have no purpose?

Check the state before treating it as a verdict on your life, because flatness and purposelessness arrive together and the flatness usually came first. From a depleted configuration nothing looks worth doing, and the read feels like an honest audit. Move the body, take one concrete action for the next ten minutes, and see whether the question changes shape. If the emptiness has been unbroken for months, that belongs with a professional.
