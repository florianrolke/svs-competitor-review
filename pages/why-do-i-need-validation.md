# Why do I need other people to tell me I'm doing OK?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because at some point your own read of yourself stopped counting as evidence, and somebody else's word became the thing that settled it. Needing validation works as an outsourced verdict rather than as vanity: the internal instrument that decides whether a feeling is real or a decision was right got overruled early, so it gets checked against someone outside. That instrument can be rebuilt.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
Explanations come later on this page. Start with these four, and watch for the one that arrives already worded the way you word it.

- "I'm always seeking validation that I'm loved."
- "Without constant validation, I feel unloved and worthless."
- "I need constant signs to prove that I'm still loved."
- "My parents' approval is the only way I can feel validated."

All four hand the verdict on your own life to somebody else, and the cost shows up in decisions that never close and evenings decided by a reply that did not arrive.

[How the seminar works with the verdict you keep handing to other people](/seminar/)
</div>

You read the message three times before you send it, and then you check, within about five minutes, whether it has been read. Nothing in it was risky. You only needed to know that it landed the way you meant it, and until you know, the whole thing sits slightly open at the back of your mind.

Those five minutes are the whole pattern in miniature. Something happened, you have your own read of it, and your own read will not close the file. It needs a countersignature from somebody else.

Most people arrive at this question carrying one specific example. A project finished on Thursday that felt like nothing at all until a manager said it was good on Friday. A parent told about a promotion who said the right words in slightly the wrong tone, and the whole call went quiet inside you. A post refreshed eleven times before lunch, with the rest of the day tilting one way or the other depending on a number. This page covers where that comes from, why more reassurance keeps failing to fix it, what it gets confused with, and what the other end of it looks like on an ordinary Wednesday, which is quieter and considerably more reachable than most people expect.

## What Does Needing Validation Sound Like From the Inside?

Like a standing request for confirmation running underneath ordinary life, so old that it almost never gets said out loud, even privately.

The sentences below are the lines people actually run in their own heads, in their own phrasing rather than a tidied-up version of it. They recur with unnerving consistency across people who have never met each other and whose lives look nothing alike, which is the first clue that this is something learned rather than something anyone simply is.

- "I'm always seeking validation that I'm loved."
- "I need constant validation to feel loved."
- "I need to be loved to feel validated as a person."
- "I need continuous validation to believe in love's presence."

Read them slowly. What matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word, with no translation required.

Then notice what is missing from that list. Not one of them mentions wanting to be admired, envied, or thought impressive. Every single one is about whether something is still true: whether the love is there, whether it is there today, whether it counts. That is the particular flavour of this pattern, and it is why the pattern gets misfiled so often as ego or attention-seeking by people watching from outside.

The request almost never surfaces as a request, either. It arrives as "was that weird?" the day after an ordinary conversation. As "be honest, what did you actually think?" As a story told in more detail than the story needs, with the extra detail there to pull a reaction out of the listener. As a decision you already made, presented to a friend as though it were still open, so that their agreement can arrive and finish it.

Don't judge yourself for any of that. Every one of those moves is an intelligent solution to a problem you did not choose, which is the next thing to look at.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, before anything practical, because this boundary matters more than every technique further down.

A need for approval is one of the easiest things in a person to exploit, and some people do exploit it deliberately. If somebody in your life supplies warmth and praise when you comply and withdraws it the moment you assert anything, if you find yourself working to earn back a mood that keeps getting taken away, if the approval always arrives just often enough to keep you reaching, that is a control dynamic and it belongs with a licensed professional rather than with a self-directed practice. Bring in people who can genuinely help, including people who can help you leave if that is what the situation turns out to require.

The same holds when the origin was severe. Where self-trust was damaged by betrayal, by violence in the house, or by an adult whose reactions were genuinely unpredictable, the work of rebuilding it is usually safer with a clinician alongside you. That applies too if any of this sits next to hopelessness that has not lifted for months, an addiction, a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, a relationship with food or your appearance that is organised around other people's reactions, or any thought of harming yourself, however quickly it passes. Asking for help in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve. Everything below is written for the everyday version of this: the text reread three times, the achievement that stayed flat until somebody noticed it.

## Where Does the Outsourced Verdict Actually Come From?

From an environment where your own read of reality was not accepted as evidence, repeated often enough that you stopped submitting it.

Several different childhoods produce the same result, which is why two people with this pattern can describe families that sound nothing alike. In the first route, the deficit itself is the lesson. Warmth and attention were absent or unreliable, so worth never got established as a given and became a lifelong search instead: being seen has to be earned, and then re-earned, through achievement, usefulness, or performance. [The quieter version of that childhood has its own page](/patterns/childhood-emotional-neglect/), and it is worth reading if nothing dramatic ever happened in your house.

In the second route, your perceptions were routinely corrected. You were not that hungry, you were not really upset, you were being dramatic, that is not what happened. After enough repetitions the child's own reading of a situation stops functioning as evidence at all, and a second opinion becomes structurally necessary before anything can be treated as true. [That is emotional invalidation, and what it does over time deserves its own reading.](/patterns/emotional-invalidation/)

In the third, love was metered against performance. Nothing was ever explicitly conditional and the arithmetic was unmistakable anyway: a good result bought a week of warmth, a mediocre one bought a cooler house. A child in that arrangement never reaches an "enough", so achievement turns into a permanent bid for a verdict that never fully lands. [What that does to a person's sense of a finish line is covered separately.](/patterns/unrealistic-parental-expectations/)

In the fourth, self-trust was damaged directly. A caregiver whose mood was unpredictable, a betrayal, or an environment where reading the room wrongly had real consequences all teach the same lesson efficiently: your own judgment cannot be relied on, so check it with somebody else first. [The adult version of that lesson is worth understanding on its own terms.](/patterns/betrayal-trauma/) And in the fifth, identity was built through a relationship rather than as a separate thing, so there is no independent reference point to check anything against. [Enmeshment leaves a person genuinely unsure which preferences are theirs.](/patterns/enmeshed-family/)

The mechanism is identical across all five. The instrument that reads your own experience and reports back on it was either never calibrated or was actively overruled, and an external instrument was fitted in its place because the external one was, for a while, the only one that worked.

## What Is All the Checking Actually Protecting You From?

From having to rely on a judgment that was historically punished, dismissed, or proved wrong at your expense.

Seen from that angle the behaviour stops looking like neediness and starts looking like a reasonable procedure. If your read of yourself was overruled often enough, then checking with somebody before you commit is simply the method that did not get you hurt. It is a quality control step, installed for good reasons, still running in an adult life where the original inspector left decades ago.

There is a second job it does. Checking in constantly catches disapproval early, while it is still small and still fixable, which in a household where warmth could disappear without warning is a genuinely valuable skill. Reading a partner's short reply for tone, asking twice whether everything is fine, watching a friend's face for the flicker: all of that is an early-warning system doing exactly what it was built to do. [Hypervigilance is the same machinery pointed at a wider range of threats.](/patterns/hypervigilance/)

And there is a third job, which is the one people are least willing to say out loud. As long as the verdict lives outside you, you never have to sit with the possibility that your own answer would be worse. Asking somebody else keeps the question open and shared. Deciding for yourself makes it yours, and if the underlying belief about your worth is bleak, an internal verdict is the last one you would want to hear. [Where that bleak belief comes from is a separate subject](/patterns/self-loathing/), and it explains why simply being told to trust yourself lands as useless advice.

None of these are stupid. That is worth sitting with for a moment, because the shame attached to this pattern usually rests on the assumption that the behaviour is pathetic. It is efficient, it was correct once, and it is now costing more than it returns.

## Is It Validation-Seeking, or One of the Things It Gets Confused With?

Worth checking carefully, because five neighbouring things produce similar-looking behaviour from the outside and the right response to each one differs completely.

The most common mix-up is with people-pleasing, and the distinction is worth getting exactly right. People-pleasing is about permission and being liked: you adjust what you do so that somebody does not get upset, disappointed, or distant. Validation-seeking is about confirmation of what is real: does this feeling count, was that the right call, am I actually loved. They coexist constantly and they are genuinely separable. You can placate a colleague you need nothing at all from, which is people-pleasing without any validation in it. You can run a decision past a friend with zero interest in whether they like you afterward, which is validation-seeking with no pleasing in it.

| What you are seeing | What it might actually be | The thing that tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Constantly checking how something landed | Validation-seeking | You want the reading confirmed, and you would accept an uncomfortable answer if it were definite |
| Adjusting what you say so nobody gets upset | [People-pleasing](/patterns/what-is-people-pleasing/) | The goal is their mood staying level, not your question being answered |
| Needing to matter, be noticed, be somebody | [The need for significance](/patterns/need-for-significance/) | The currency is importance rather than confirmation. Being agreed with does not satisfy it |
| A poor opinion of your own worth | [Low self-esteem](/insights/root-cause-of-low-self-esteem/) | That is a belief you hold. Validation-seeking is the checking behaviour built on top of it |
| Your feelings being dismissed by others | [Emotional invalidation](/patterns/emotional-invalidation/) | That is what was done to you. Validation-seeking is the adult strategy that formed in response |
| Wanting your work appreciated | Ordinary human wanting | You enjoy the praise and the work still counted without it |

The bottom row deserves as much attention as the others. Wanting appreciation is not a symptom of anything. Nearly everybody works better when their effort is noticed, and a person who genuinely felt nothing about recognition would be a strange one. What separates the pattern from the ordinary wanting is whether the recognition is doing structural work: whether the thing counts without it, and whether it can be banked once received or has to be re-supplied on a schedule.

The distinction from emotional invalidation is the one that changes what you do next. Invalidation is the cause and it happened to you. Validation-seeking is the strategy that grew out of it, and it is now aimed at anybody who might supply the missing confirmation: a partner, a manager, a comment section, a stranger. That is why fixing the relationship with the original source rarely resolves it, and why the search simply relocates when one supplier goes quiet.

## Why Doesn't More Validation Ever Settle It?

Because the problem is capacity rather than supply, and pouring more supply into a missing capacity reinforces the original wiring instead of replacing it.

Watch what actually happens when the validation arrives. A compliment lands on a Tuesday morning and carries the whole day, genuinely. By Thursday it has expired and the account needs topping up again. A manager says the work was excellent, and for about an hour the work is excellent, after which the question quietly reopens: was that just something managers say. The verdict has a short shelf life because it was never yours to begin with. Anything issued from outside can be withdrawn from outside, so it never fully converts into something you own.

There is a second reason, and it is the more uncomfortable one. Every time the confirmation arrives from outside and does its job, the system learns that this is where confirmation comes from. The external instrument gets one more successful reading, and the internal one gets no practice at all. Ten years of very supportive friends can leave a person exactly as dependent as they started, sometimes more so, which is why "just find people who appreciate you" is such well-meant and unhelpful advice.

The third reason shows up when the supply is cut off. If a specific person will not give it, the search widens rather than stops. A new manager, a new audience, a new project, a new relationship takes over the role, often within weeks. That widening is the single clearest evidence that the mechanism sits inside you rather than in whichever person you are currently frustrated with. It also means the fantasy version of relief, where the one person who never approved of you finally does, would not deliver what you expect even if it happened.

What actually changes this is a track record of your own readings holding up. That is slower, it is duller, and it is the only thing that has ever worked.

## Where Does It Show Up When You Would Not Call It Neediness?

In seven arenas, and most people recognise themselves in two or three at first and find the others later.

| Where it shows up | What it looks like from outside | The sentence underneath |
|---|---|---|
| At work | Needing explicit approval before believing the work was good, rereading a sent email, asking for a second opinion on something already decided | "I need to know it was actually good, from someone who would know" |
| With a partner | Asking where things stand more often than the relationship warrants, reading a short reply for an hour, needing love said out loud on a schedule | "I need constant signs to prove that I'm still loved" |
| With friends | Measuring how much someone cares by reply speed and tone, asking "was that weird?" after an ordinary evening | "I need to check that nothing got damaged" |
| With family | Still angling for one parent's specific approval decades after leaving home, mentioning the promotion twice in case it was missed | "My parents' approval is the only way I can feel validated" |
| On social media | Posting and then tracking the response as a referendum, with the rest of the day moving with the number | "Without constant validation, I feel unloved and worthless" |
| With decisions | Collecting second opinions on a choice already made internally, changing course when one person frowns | "I can't trust my own call until somebody backs it" |
| With your own feelings | Not being sure whether you are actually upset until somebody says you have a right to be | "I need to know if this feeling is allowed" |

The last row is the one people miss in themselves for longest, and it is the deepest version of the pattern. Uncertainty about a decision is uncomfortable. Uncertainty about what you are currently feeling is disorienting, and it is common in anyone whose feelings were routinely corrected by an adult. If you regularly cannot tell whether you are angry until a friend says the situation sounds infuriating, that is the same mechanism operating one level further in. [Learning to locate a feeling in the body rather than reasoning your way to it is the practical route out of that particular version.](/insights/how-to-feel-your-emotions/)

Two further places belong on that list, quietly. Changing how you look, repeatedly and without much attachment to any particular version, is often an experiment in what gets noticed. So is throwing yourself into volunteering or into a cause with more intensity than you have for your own life. Neither is a problem in itself. Both are worth a second look if the reaction, rather than the thing itself, is what you are actually there for.

## Can You Need Validation and Still Look Fiercely Independent?

Yes, and this version is the reason a great many people never recognise themselves on a page like this one.

The same wound gets managed in two opposite ways. One is pursuit: asking, checking, achieving, posting, over-explaining, staying useful enough that the confirmation keeps arriving. The other is pre-emption: deciding in advance that you will not need it from anyone. The second group runs a different internal script entirely, and it sounds like this.

- "I shouldn't need anyone."
- "Admitting I need help makes me feel weak and vulnerable."
- "I need to be strong and keep my emotions in check."
- "Relying on external validation is a weakness."
- "I don't need anyone else, they just bring trouble."
- "No one really sticks around when you need them."
- "It seems like people always leave when you need them most."
- "I need to be cautious with everyone."

Every one of those is a solution to the same problem. If the confirmation was never reliable, one workable response is to stop requesting it and call that strength. From the inside it registers as maturity, self-sufficiency, and a healthy immunity to what other people think. It comes with real costs that are easy to attribute elsewhere: help refused on principle, praise deflected the second it arrives, an unusual need for privacy and personal space, plans made alone that would have gone better with input, and a quiet contempt for people who visibly need reassurance. That contempt is usually the loudest tell. Nobody is that irritated by a stranger's neediness unless something in them recognises it.

The two versions can even alternate in the same person, month to month or arena to arena: openly reassurance-seeking with a partner, immovably self-sufficient at work. [The distancing version of this has a fuller treatment of its own](/patterns/emotionally-unavailable/), and [the difficulty trusting anybody's read including your own is covered separately.](/patterns/trust-issues/)

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. What I underestimated longest was how much of this runs on capacity rather than supply. More reassurance never once produced a person who needed less of it, in myself or in anybody I have worked with since. Where it sits for me today is worth saying plainly: I still check, I check considerably less than I used to, and I would rather tell you that than sell a finished version of something nobody finishes.

## Why Do Praise and Criticism Both Land Harder Than They Should?

Because both are being received as verdicts on the whole of you rather than as passing information about one piece of work.

A compliment that would be pleasant to most people arrives instead as a ruling, and a ruling changes the state of things. That is why a single warm sentence can genuinely reorganise a day and why the effect fades so completely by the weekend: verdicts expire, and the case reopens. Criticism runs on the same machinery in the other direction. A note about one paragraph in one document gets processed as a statement about your competence, your standing, and possibly your value as a person, which is a wildly disproportionate response to a note about a paragraph and completely logical if the underlying question was never settled independently.

Silence is the worst of the three, and the reason is structural. A system built to catch disapproval early has to fire on weak signals, since the whole point is to notice before it becomes obvious. Silence is the weakest signal there is, so the machinery treats it as data and fills it in with the worst available reading. Nothing has actually been communicated. The absence of information gets replaced by the old expectation, which is why an unanswered message can generate three hours of internal argument out of literally nothing.

There is one honest complication here. Sometimes the read is right. Someone was cold, the silence did mean something, the criticism was in fact about more than the paragraph. The pattern does not make you wrong; it makes you unable to tell the difference, because the alarm fires identically in both cases. Rebuilding the instrument is what gives you back the ability to distinguish a real signal from a reflex, which matters far more than deciding in advance that you are always overreacting.

## What Does Self-Validation Actually Look Like on an Ordinary Wednesday?

Considerably less impressive than the word suggests, and much closer than it looks from here.

Every one of these patterns is a polarity with two ends, and describing only the painful end leaves you with an accurate account of your problem and nowhere to walk. The other end of this one is a working internal instrument: the ability to notice what you think and feel, and to treat that reading as real before checking whether anybody else agrees. Confidence and charisma have very little to do with it, and indifference to other people has even less.

| The outsourced end | The self-validating end | What it looks like on an ordinary Wednesday |
|---|---|---|
| Feelings need confirming before they count | Feelings register as real when you notice them | You are annoyed at four in the afternoon and you know it at four, without needing a friend to agree the situation was annoying |
| Decisions stay open until somebody agrees | A decision gets made and stays made | You pick the thing, and you do not run it past two more people that evening |
| Praise is load-bearing | Praise is pleasant | A compliment is enjoyed on the spot and nothing depends on the next one arriving |
| Criticism triggers a scramble | Criticism gets evaluated on its merits | You take the useful half of the feedback and leave the rest, that same afternoon |
| Achievements stay pending until witnessed | Finished work feels finished | You close the laptop on a good piece of work and feel done, without telling anyone |
| Silence gets filled with the worst reading | Ambiguity can be left ambiguous | A short reply gets no interpretation until more information arrives |
| Posting is a bid for a verdict | Posting is just posting | You share the thing and do not look again for two hours |
| Your own read is a hypothesis | Your own read is evidence | You say what you think happened before you find out what anybody else thought |

Read the right-hand column carefully, because it is deliberately unspectacular. Nobody in that column has stopped caring what people think. Self-validation still leaves you wanting closeness, enjoying praise, and asking for reassurance when you want it. What changes is that the caring stops being the mechanism that decides whether something actually happened, was good, or was true.

That is the honest version of the goal, and it is worth stating precisely because the caricature version does real damage. Plenty of people chase a fantasy of total indifference, conclude they have failed at it, and give up. [The full argument for why indifference is the wrong target is worth reading if that is where you are stuck.](/insights/how-to-stop-caring-what-people-think/)

Don't judge yourself if that column reads as slightly out of reach today. The gap took years to open and nobody crosses it in a weekend.

## How Do You Build an Internal Instrument You Can Actually Use?

By accumulating a track record of your own readings holding up, one small repetition at a time, in situations where being wrong would cost you almost nothing.

The mechanism is simple to describe and uncomfortable to run. Notice a feeling, a judgment, or a decision. Act on it without outsourcing it first. Then check afterward whether it held up. Each time it holds without external confirmation, the internal instrument becomes slightly more credible to you, and credibility is the entire currency here.

1. **Put a gap between the act and the check.** Send the message, then leave the phone face down for twenty minutes. The gap is the actual exercise, and twenty minutes is enough. What you are practising is tolerating an unresolved question, which is the specific discomfort this whole pattern was built to avoid.
2. **Say your own read first.** Before asking how a meeting went, state what you thought of it, out loud or in a note. Hearing somebody else's view first quietly overwrites yours, and after enough overwriting you genuinely stop having one.
3. **Finish one thing without announcing it.** Complete a piece of work, close it, and tell nobody for a day. This is harder than it sounds and it directly targets the habit of routing achievement through a witness before it can register.
4. **Make one small decision and leave it made for a week.** Not a life decision. A restaurant, a haircut, a route. The size does not matter, because what is being rehearsed is the experience of a choice staying closed without a countersignature.
5. **Take a compliment and add nothing.** No joke, no correction, no returned favour that evening. Just thank you. For anyone whose worth has always felt provisional, receiving is a distinct difficulty and it works on the same root.
6. **Separate the feedback from the verdict.** When criticism arrives, ask one specific question about what should change in the work. A specific answer keeps it about the work. Silence in your own head turns it into a ruling about you.
7. **Leave one ambiguous silence uninterpreted for an hour.** Set a timer if that helps. Most silences resolve themselves in that time, and the ones that do not can be asked about directly, which is a great deal cheaper than three hours of guessing.
8. **Keep score of your own readings.** When you make a call without checking it first, note it, and revisit it a week later. Most people find their unassisted reads hold up far more often than expected, and that record is the evidence the internal instrument has been missing.

Two honest notes about this list. First, none of it works as a single heroic effort; it works at the rate of repetitions completed, which is why every item is small enough to do on a Wednesday. Second, this is the validation-specific slice of a larger job. [The full rebuilding of self-trust as a faculty of judgement is its own subject and has its own page](/patterns/how-to-trust-yourself/), and it goes considerably deeper into what to do when your read is genuinely wrong. Read that one next if this list feels like the right direction.

Is any of it comfortable? No. It is small, repetitive, and specifically designed to keep you sitting in the exact discomfort you built a whole system to escape, which is why it works and why so few people stay with it.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Longer than anybody wants, and the honest answer runs at two different speeds.

Recognition comes quickly. Within a couple of weeks of paying attention you will catch yourself mid-request: the second opinion you were about to collect on something already decided, the story you were about to over-tell, the phone you were about to check. Catching it counts as progress even on the days you go ahead and do it anyway, and most people find the catching itself slightly unbearable at first. Watching yourself reach for the reassurance while knowing exactly what you are doing feels worse than not noticing. It is still a genuine step.

The reflex takes months. It was rehearsed daily for years and it fades at the pace of accumulated experience rather than understanding, which is the part that frustrates thoughtful people most. Reading this page changes very little on its own. Forty ordinary decisions made and left standing without a second opinion change quite a lot. [Why understanding a pattern so rarely shifts it is worth its own detour.](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/)

And it regresses under stress, reliably. Under pressure, exhaustion, or a genuine threat to a relationship, the old shortcut returns, because asking somebody is faster than trusting an instrument still under construction. That regression is part of the normal shape of this rather than evidence of failure, and expecting it removes most of its sting. The people who actually shift this are the ones who resumed after the regression rather than reading it as proof that nothing was working.

Is that frustrating? Yes, and I would rather say so than sell a faster route that does not exist.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Check Anyone's Reaction?

Run a verdict log for forty-eight hours. Open a note on your phone, and every time you go looking for confirmation, write three things: what you wanted confirmed, who you went to, and what you already thought before you asked. That third column is the one that does the work, and it is the one most people leave blank for the first few entries because they genuinely have to stop and dig for the answer.

Then read the list on the second night. Two things usually turn up. The first is volume, which is almost always higher than expected once the indirect versions are counted: the over-told story, the decision presented as still open, the question asked to invite a reaction rather than to get information. The second is more useful. In a large share of the entries, you already had your answer before you asked. You knew the work was good, you knew the message was fine, you knew what you wanted for dinner. What you were collecting was permission to believe your own reading. Seeing that written down in your own handwriting does more than any amount of being told you should trust yourself, and it costs you two days of noticing and nothing else.
