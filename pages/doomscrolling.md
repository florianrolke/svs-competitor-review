# Why do I keep scrolling things that make me feel worse?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

You keep scrolling because the scrolling is doing a job. Watching feels like preparing, refreshing feels like control, and a feed with no bottom never delivers the moment where you are allowed to stop. Doomscrolling names the behavior accurately and stops there. The reason your thumb keeps moving at one in the morning is personal, and it is findable.

There is a specific hour most people searching this phrase already know by heart. The room is dark, the phone is the only light in it, and you are reading about something two thousand miles away that you can do nothing about. You are not enjoying it. You noticed forty minutes ago that you were not enjoying it. Your thumb kept moving.

That hour is where this whole pattern lives, and it is the only place it can be changed. Everything below is about what happens inside it.

## What does doomscrolling feel like from the inside?

Like being informed, right up until the second you put the phone down.

The lines below are the ones people run in their own heads while it is happening, in the phrasing they use when nobody is listening. Almost none of them mention wanting to feel worse.

- "I don't even want to know this. I just can't put it down."
- "If I look away, I'm being naive."
- "I'll feel better once I understand what's actually going on."
- "One more refresh and then I'll actually go to sleep."
- "I'm not doing anything else with this hour anyway."
- "I feel sick, and I'm still holding the phone."

Read them slowly. What matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word, with no translation required.

Notice what they have in common. Every one is a reason. Nobody in that list decided to wreck their own evening. Each sentence is a small, sensible-sounding case for continuing, and that case gets made again roughly every eight seconds for an hour.

The body keeps a record the mind talks straight over. Jaw tight. Shoulders somewhere up near the ears. A shallow breath held near the top of the chest. Eyes dry and slightly sore. Then, in the ninety seconds between putting the phone face down and falling asleep, a particular flat feeling that has no name and does not seem to belong to any single story you read.

## What is doomscrolling?

Doomscrolling is the habit of continuing to scroll through distressing news or social feeds long past the point where anything is being learned, usually while feeling steadily worse with each minute.

Merriam-Webster, which now carries the word as a full dictionary entry, defines the verb doomscroll as spending excessive time scrolling through online content, especially news, that makes a person feel sad, anxious, or angry. Its sister term, doomsurfing, describes the same behavior on a larger screen. Both appeared in Merriam-Webster's Words We're Watching feature in 2020, after the phrase spread out of a handful of social media posts dating to around 2018 and into ordinary speech. Macquarie Dictionary made doomscrolling its Committee's Choice Word of the Year for 2020. Researchers followed the language: a doomscrolling scale was published by Sharma and colleagues in 2022 in Technology, Mind, and Behavior, a separate Social Media Doomscrolling Scale appeared the same year, and the studies since have linked the behavior to lower life satisfaction, poorer sleep, more negative mood, and a close relationship with intolerance of uncertainty, a well-studied trait with its own measurement scale.

**What the word genuinely captures.** Three things, precisely. That the content skews toward threat rather than toward the neutral or the good. That the scrolling continues well past its own usefulness. And that the person doing it can see all of this while it is happening, which is what separates it from ordinary distraction. Naming it is worth something real. A behavior with a word attached stops being a private failure of character and becomes a thing with a shape, a literature, and other people in it.

**What the word does not capture.** Why you, specifically, are doing it. Doomscrolling describes the what with genuine precision and says nothing whatsoever about the why. The term is a description of a surface. Two people can produce that identical surface out of entirely unrelated interiors, and the word covers both of them equally well while explaining neither one. There is no diagnosis called doomscrolling, no clinical threshold, and no test that tells you which kind you are. The name is where the question starts.

## When is this more than a habit?

When the dread stops lifting between sessions, and that line is worth drawing before anything else on this page.

Most of what gets called doomscrolling is costly rather than dangerous. It takes your sleep, your mornings, and a share of your attention, and it responds well to unglamorous changes. Some of it needs more than that. Hopelessness that has held steady for weeks rather than hours, checking that has taken over your working days as well as your evenings, a loop about your own body that no result ever settles, or any thought of ending your life, however quickly it passes through, belongs with a licensed professional starting now.

There is one more case worth naming plainly, because generic advice handles it badly. Some people are scrolling because something in their actual life is genuinely unresolved and genuinely threatening: a region under attack, a family member in danger, a diagnosis in the house, a job that may not exist next month. That watching has a real object in front of it. Somebody is trying to hold something that is genuinely happening, and what that deserves is support rather than a screen time limit.

## Why does it feel worse every minute and still not stop?

Because feeling worse and stopping are run by different systems, and only one of them is watching the clock.

Four mechanisms stack here, and every one of them is documented and named.

**Negativity bias.** Threatening information gets more attention, more weight, and more memory than the neutral or the pleasant kind. Rozin and Royzman named the effect formally in 2001, the same year Baumeister and colleagues gathered decades of findings under the title Bad Is Stronger Than Good. This runs below preference. You are not choosing the alarming headline over the ordinary one. It arrives with more force.

**A schedule that refuses to release.** Refreshing a feed pays out unpredictably. Most pulls give you nothing, and then one gives you something. B. F. Skinner mapped this arrangement decades before feeds existed and found that a variable ratio schedule, where the reward arrives after an unpredictable number of attempts, produces the highest and most persistent rate of responding of any schedule available. It is the structure of a slot machine, and it is why the gesture outlives the interest.

**No bottom.** Infinite scroll was invented in 2006 by Aza Raskin, who has since said publicly and repeatedly that he regrets it. Before it existed, a page ended, and the end of a page was a decision point. A feed with no end removes every one of those decision points, so stopping has to be generated from inside you rather than offered by the thing in your hand.

**Nothing ever closes.** Bluma Zeigarnik showed in 1927 that unfinished tasks stay active in memory in a way finished ones do not. A story you have half-followed is an unfinished task. So is an argument in a comment thread, a developing situation, a number that gets updated in the morning. A feed manufactures open loops considerably faster than any person can close them, and open loops pull.

Underneath the four sits the arrangement that trains the whole thing. Relief arrives in seconds and the cost arrives hours later. Each refresh delivers a small immediate hit of knowing, of doing something, of not being caught out. The price shows up at seven the next morning as a headache and a flat mood that never gets connected to the cause. That gap between fast relief and slow cost is what turns a behavior into a reflex, and it is the same gap that keeps every other reflex running.

There is a further effect, and it works at the level of your picture of the world. George Gerbner's cultivation research found that heavy consumption of violent media leaves people believing the world is considerably more dangerous than it measurably is. He called it mean world syndrome. A feed weighted toward catastrophe does that job faster than television ever did, and the resulting picture then makes further watching feel necessary rather than optional. The behavior manufactures its own justification overnight.

## Why is it always at night?

Because the late hour is usually the only part of the day nobody else has a claim on.

Researchers at Utrecht named the wider habit bedtime procrastination in 2014: going to bed later than you intended, with no external reason forcing it. A lay version travels under the name revenge bedtime procrastination, which came into English out of Chinese social media and describes staying up specifically to take back hours the working day consumed. Both describe the same eleven forty-five. The day is finally over, nobody wants anything, and going to sleep would end the only stretch of it that belonged to you.

Then there is what the dark takes away. All day, the thing you have been avoiding is kept out by activity. Email, driving, the school run, other people talking. Lie down in a quiet room and the same thought arrives on schedule, roughly on time, in the same words it used last night. A lit screen six inches from your face is the fastest available way to keep it out. By then the feed is doing a completely different job from the one it advertises: filling a silence that would otherwise be occupied by something specific.

The morning version is the same move in reverse. The hand goes out before the eyes are properly open, and the first information of the day is somebody else's emergency. What that buys is a few minutes without the particular quality of an unstructured mind first thing, which for a lot of people is the least defended it gets all day. Placement does most of the work here. A phone charging within arm's reach of the pillow gets picked up before any decision has been consciously made, and it stays in the hand because the first thing it shows is designed to be held.

## Why do two people doomscroll identically for completely different reasons?

Because the same fruit can spring from two different roots.

No behavior happens for no reason, and the lack of a behavior does not happen for no reason either. That holds for the hour in bed the same as for anything else. What it does not tell you is which reason belongs to you. Two people can produce a scrolling session that would look identical on a screen recording, minute for minute, thumb movement for thumb movement, and the thing driving it can have nothing in common. Just because it looks the same on the outside does not mean that it comes from the same inner place.

This is exactly where the word runs out. A label can be looked up in ninety seconds. A root has to be found, and it can only ever be found in one person at a time.

| What the scrolling is doing | Where it usually starts | How to spot it | The sentence underneath |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keeping watch | A house or a period where not seeing something coming was expensive | You scan headlines you have no intention of reading, and feel briefly settled once you have "checked" | "If I know about it early enough, it can't blindside me." |
| Keeping a thought out | A specific unfaced thing: a decision, a loss, a marriage, a number | The scrolling starts within minutes of the room going quiet, and stops the moment somebody walks in | "I'm fine as long as I don't stop." |
| Supplying stimulation | A nervous system that learned to run high, where calm reads as flat rather than restful | You scroll while watching television, and the low-stakes parts of your day are the hardest to sit through | "Anything quiet is unbearable." |
| Buying back control | A stretch where something large happened that you had no say in | You read analysis rather than news, and go looking for the piece that finally explains it | "If I understand it, I can do something about it." |
| Reclaiming the day | Years of a schedule owned by other people | It only happens after ten, and going to bed feels like conceding something | "This is the only hour that belongs to me." |

**Vigilance.** Somebody who grew up needing to know the mood of the house before opening a door does not lose that skill when the house is gone. The watching kept them safe, it was accurate, and it worked. What it never got was an off switch. A feed is the most efficient vigilance surface ever built, which is why it feels almost restful for the first ten minutes and corrosive for the following fifty. The tell is the settling. Not information, and not relief exactly. A brief sense of having checked, which lasts until the next update.

**Avoidance.** Here the content is close to irrelevant. What matters is that the screen occupies the channel that the thought would otherwise use. The tell is timing rather than subject: the scrolling begins within a few minutes of quiet arriving and ends the moment somebody enters the room. If you stop and find something waiting in the first fifteen seconds, fully formed, with a specific name attached to it, the scrolling was a lid.

**Stimulation.** For some people, calm registers as wrong rather than pleasant. A nervous system trained on unpredictability treats a quiet evening as a gap that something bad might fill, so it goes looking for input. Catastrophe supplies intensity on demand at no social cost. The tell is scrolling during things you actually chose to do, and finding the low-stakes hours of your week the hardest ones to be inside.

**Control.** Something happened that you had no vote in. Reading about it feels like doing something, and understanding feels adjacent to influence. The tell is a preference for analysis over news, and a search that keeps going after the facts are in, hunting for the account that will finally make it hold still. This one is the hardest to see from inside, because it is genuinely intelligent behavior aimed at something it cannot reach.

**Reclamation.** The day was spent on other people. The hour after ten is the only stretch nobody has booked, and sleep would end it. The tell is that the scrolling is almost entirely nocturnal and comes with a distinct edge of defiance rather than dread. This root responds to something none of the others do: a real hour, earlier in the day, that genuinely belongs to you.

Notice what happens if you get the root wrong. Someone whose scrolling is vigilance gets handed a screen time limit and feels, correctly, that something protective was taken away and nothing put in its place. Someone whose scrolling is avoidance hits the limit, puts the phone down, and finds the thought waiting in the room exactly where they left it. Someone whose root is reclamation is told to go to bed earlier, which reads as the day taking one more thing. The intervention that works on one root does almost nothing on another, and that is the real reason generic advice about this behavior has such a poor record.

## What separates staying informed from doomscrolling?

Three differences, and you can check all of them in about a minute.

| | Staying informed | Doomscrolling |
|---|---|---|
| How it ends | At a point you could have named in advance | When the battery dies, somebody speaks to you, or you fall asleep |
| What you keep | You could say three things you learned, out loud, tomorrow | You cannot name one story from the last thirty minutes |
| What it changes | Occasionally leads to something you do: a call, a donation, a vote, a conversation | Leads mostly to more of itself |

An honest word about the quantity. Fifteen deliberate minutes with a serious source, once or twice a day, is being well informed by any reasonable standard. Ninety minutes of refreshing four apps in rotation is a different activity that happens to share a surface with the first one. The rotation is the thing to watch. Each pass adds emotional weight without adding information, which is why you can finish an hour heavier and no better briefed.

The test that settles it fastest is retention. Tomorrow at lunch, try to tell somebody three things you read last night. If you cannot get to three, the hour was doing a job other than informing you, and the rest of this page is about which job.

## What if the feed you cannot leave is your own numbers?

Then the same loop is running, on instruments you built yourself.

The schedule described further up this page does not care what the numbers are about. A revenue figure, an open rate, a view count, a reply that may or may not have arrived overnight: most checks give you nothing, and then one gives you something, which is the arrangement that keeps a thumb moving long after the interest has gone. The gesture is identical to the news version. Everything around it differs, in two ways that matter.

**It runs harder, because checking your own numbers is indistinguishable from working.** Every refusal available to somebody scrolling the news is unavailable here. "This is not helping me" carries no weight against a dashboard you are accountable for. "I should be doing something useful" has nothing to push against, because the tab is legitimately yours and arguably your job. So the hour never sets off the small internal alarm that eventually ends a news scroll. The whole thing looks like diligence from outside the room and feels like diligence from inside it, which is how somebody runs this for years without once putting it on the list of things they would like to change.

What it sounds like when somebody describes it plainly:

- "I can check it every single minute, every single day. I can check my performance and everything."
- "I'm monitoring the conversations like a hawk."
- "I've got nine screens now, monitoring various different information."
- "Every time I click another [profile] and check off another thing in a spreadsheet, I think I lose a brain cell."

Notice how little information is being sought in any of those sentences. The first one names a frequency and not one question the checking would answer. The last one already contains its own verdict, delivered mid-loop by the person doing it.

**Nothing in the loop is anybody else's life.** The news version at least involves other people, which is part of what makes it feel like participation. This one is a closed circuit of your own tabs: your figures, your conversation, your unfinished work looking back at you. And the tell is unusually clean. You already know the number has not moved since the last check. You know the report updates once an hour and you are on your fortieth look at it. The checking carries on anyway, which puts it on the wrong side of the retention test above: the hour was doing a job other than informing you.

One boundary is worth drawing, because the two get confused. [Checking your phone mid-conversation](/patterns/checking-my-phone-mid-conversation/) locks itself to a live person in the room and says so; this one runs in the hour when there is nobody there to interrupt.

An honest word about the evidence. These lines come from people describing the pattern in their own words, unattributed and undated, because none of them agreed to be named on a public page. Consistent description across a handful of people is worth something, and it is worth less than a measurement. No published research yet separates checking your own numbers from doomscrolling as a behavior with its own literature, so the mechanism underneath is the documented part and this version of it is, for now, only well described.

Two things follow. Deleting an app does nothing to this version, because there is nothing to delete: the dashboard is a login you need, and the spreadsheet sits on the machine you work on. And the root underneath is still one of the five above, most often vigilance or the buying back of control, running in daylight instead of after ten.

## Why does deleting the app not fix it?

Because the app was the outlet rather than the pressure.

Picture a balloon full of water. Squeeze it in one place and it bulges on the other side. The water has gone nowhere. And unless you can see the hand doing the squeezing, that bulge looks like a brand new problem with a cause of its own.

This is what usually happens in week two. The app comes off the phone and the hour reappears somewhere else: in a browser tab, in a different app that was never the problem, in three hours of television, in the fridge, in a text thread with the one friend who is always awake. People report this constantly and read it as a personal failure of willpower. It is the balloon, and the balloon is not a character flaw.

There is a second reason the deletion does not hold, and it is worth stating flatly. Changing a behavior through force and making that change last are two separate achievements. Discipline is entirely capable of producing the change. What it cannot do by itself is sustain it, and for that something in the environment has to change alongside the behavior. The environment here includes far more than the phone: the empty evening, the charger beside the bed, the hour with nothing in it, the specific thought waiting in the quiet, and the plain fact that nothing else in your week currently does the job the scrolling was doing.

That last point is the one worth sitting with. Any plan that removes the behavior without replacing its function is asking a part of you to give up something protective and accept nothing in exchange. It works for about nine days. Then the balloon bulges somewhere you were not looking.

## Why does understanding all of this change nothing on its own?

Because an explanation and an excuse can be built out of exactly the same facts.

Finding the reason is worth doing once. Without it you tend to give a behavior far more weight than it deserves and you keep attacking the surface instead of the cause. What the reason cannot do is act on your behalf.

There is a distinction here worth carrying around: giving something an explanation, without turning that explanation into a story, so that you have an excuse. The same sentence serves either function. "I scroll because I grew up in a house where you had to see it coming" can be the first honest thing somebody has said about it in years, or it can be the line they deliver at eleven at night on the way into another two hours. The words are identical. What separates them is what comes next.

Watch for the polished version. When an account of yourself has been told often enough that it comes out smoothly, in the same order, with the same phrase in the middle, it has usually stopped doing diagnostic work and started doing protective work. A reason that is still useful tends to feel slightly raw to say out loud.

Life changes through action. That is a flatter sentence than most people want from a page like this, and it is the one that survives contact with an actual Tuesday. It took me a long time to get out of my own overthinking, and I see it constantly in people drawn to this kind of material: very intuitive, very perceptive, unusually good at understanding themselves, with the last step, the acting, never quite trained in. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. The understanding was the cheap part.

## What happens if you just cut it out?

You go through a few uncomfortable evenings, and then you find out that nothing was actually missing.

The sequence is short and it looks the same every time. Decide what you actually want. Cut the thing out. Go through the withdrawal at the start, where it genuinely feels like something is gone. Then discover, faster than you expected, that it was not.

The middle stage ends most attempts, because the feeling of absence is vivid and immediate while the discovery that nothing was lost arrives several days later. In that gap the absence gets read as evidence. I clearly needed that. This obviously cannot be sustained. I was better informed before. It always feels like something is missing in the meantime, and that feeling is a stage with a predictable length rather than a verdict on the attempt.

What the first three evenings actually feel like, concretely: a reaching hand with nowhere to send it, a strange surplus of time that reads as boredom, irritability somewhere around nine, and a specific low-grade fear of having missed something important. Somewhere in the first week ordinary life tests that fear for you. Something significant happens, you hear about it from a person, in a sentence, roughly a day late, and absolutely nothing is different for the delay. That is the moment the structure loosens, and it does more than any amount of resolve.

There is a second effect, and it is the reason to make the cut clean rather than gradual. Attention feeds this. By focusing on something you make it real again and again, and by the very means of not spending any time there anymore, you feel less of it. A pattern that is watched, thought about, argued with, monitored, and reported on daily is a pattern receiving a steady supply of the one thing it runs on. Some of what feels enormous at the end of week one is noticeably smaller by week three for no reason beyond not having been fed.

Don't judge yourself if a previous attempt collapsed on day four. Day four is where the sequence is designed to be hardest, and quitting there is the most common outcome rather than a personal defect.

## Do you need to heal something or just build a different night?

Both routes are real, and using the wrong one on your own pattern wastes months.

Sometimes the emotional response is miscalibrated. The dread that shows up in a quiet room is out of all proportion to the room, and it belongs to something considerably older than the phone. In that case going at the scrolling directly is going at a symptom, and the pressure surfaces somewhere else within a fortnight, exactly as the balloon predicts.

Sometimes the emotional response is simply a habit. The hand goes out to the nightstand because it has gone out to the nightstand nine hundred times. There is no wound underneath it worth excavating, and treating it as one sends you looking for a childhood explanation that was never there. That version responds to reconditioning: different placement, different hour, something else in the hand.

Telling them apart takes about two weeks and no special skill. Cut the behavior cleanly and watch what turns up. If the pull fades and the evenings quietly fill themselves, it was a habit, and it has been outgrown. If cutting it produces a specific feeling with an age to it, something that seems to have a childhood address, then the scrolling was holding a lid down and the work sits underneath the screen rather than on it.

Misdiagnosing this in either direction is expensive. Treating a habit as a wound produces years of interesting reflection and an unchanged evening. Treating a wound as a habit produces a very disciplined person who has swapped one lid for another.

## What does the resolved side of this actually look like?

Concrete enough that you could film it, and considerably duller than most people expect.

Every one of these patterns has two ends. Describing only the costly end leaves you with an excellent account of your problem and nowhere to walk, so here is the other end, translated into an ordinary Tuesday night.

| The costly end | The resolved end | What it looks like on an ordinary Tuesday night |
|---|---|---|
| Compulsive watching | Deliberate reading | Twenty minutes, one source, stopped because you were finished |
| Vigilance with no off switch | Vigilance you can set down | You check once, and the checking actually ends the matter for the evening |
| Quiet as a threat | Quiet as rest | Ten minutes on the sofa with nothing on, and no hand moving toward anything |
| The feed as a lid | The thought faced directly | The thing you were avoiding gets four minutes and a written sentence |
| Knowing as a substitute for doing | Information that leads somewhere | One call made, one message sent, one small thing changed |
| The hour reclaimed at midnight | An hour that is genuinely yours, earlier | Something you like, at eight, on a weeknight, without permission needed |
| Sleep as the thing that lost | Sleep as the thing you keep | Lights out at a time you chose, with the phone in another room |
| A world that feels mostly dangerous | A world you can size accurately | You can name three good things that are also true this week |
| Mornings that begin with somebody else's emergency | Mornings that begin with yours | Twenty minutes awake before anything is read |
| Guilt about the hour | Nothing to account for | The evening ends and there is no reckoning attached to it |

Read the right-hand column carefully, because it is deliberately unimpressive. Nobody in it has renounced anything or announced a digital detox. The resolved end of this pattern is mostly a person whose evening ends when they decide it does.

Don't judge yourself if that column reads as slightly out of reach tonight. The distance between the two columns was built one ordinary evening at a time, and it comes down the same way.

## How do you change this without a purge that collapses in nine days?

By working the root first and the behavior second, and by changing the room rather than your character.

1. **Name the destination, then the obstacle, then the bridge.** First you identify the way you want to go, then you identify what is going to get in the way, and then you build yourself a bridge to get across. Most attempts at this skip straight to the bridge, which is why they are so often the wrong bridge. Write one sentence describing what you want your evenings to be. Then write what will stop it. Then design for that specific obstacle.
2. **Find your root before you touch the behavior.** Use the table above. Sit with the five and notice which sentence makes you slightly uncomfortable rather than which one sounds most reasonable. The uncomfortable one is usually yours.
3. **Change the geography.** The charger goes in the kitchen. That single move removes a decision you would otherwise have to make at eleven at night while tired, which is the worst possible moment to be making it. Buy a cheap alarm clock so the phone has no excuse to be in the room.
4. **Give the watching a container.** Fifteen minutes, one source, before six in the evening. A container respects the part of you that needs to know things and takes the job away from a feed that has no ending built into it.
5. **Put something in the hand.** The reflex is partly physical. A paper book on the pillow, a notebook, a crossword. No feed, and a visible end point.
6. **Let the quiet arrive on purpose, for four minutes.** Sit in a room with nothing on and let whatever shows up show up. Whatever arrives in the first fifteen seconds is worth writing down in one line. For the avoidance root this is the entire intervention, and it is the one people skip.
7. **Expect the withdrawal and put a date on it.** Write down the day you start and the day two weeks out. Mark day four in advance as the hard one. A stage with a name attached behaves very differently from a stage that shows up unannounced.
8. **Stop feeding it.** After the first week, stop tracking, stop discussing it, stop checking how you are doing with it. Attention is the supply. Cutting the supply is a real move rather than an absence of one.

Is any of this dramatic? No. It is unglamorous and freaking slow, and that is exactly why it holds when a weekend of resolve does not.

Does the pull vanish once the room is set up properly? Honestly, no. It comes back in hard weeks, on the same schedule and in a form you will recognize immediately. What grows with practice is the gap between the pull and the hand, and that gap is where an evening gets decided.

## How long does this take, honestly?

Longer than a weekend, and the truthful answer runs at two speeds.

Recognition arrives fast. Most people can catch themselves mid-scroll within a week of deliberate attention, and catching it counts even on the nights when nothing changes afterward. Watching yourself continue while knowing exactly what the continuing is for feels terrible, and it is genuine progress.

The reflex takes longer. Somewhere between one and three months of different evenings, in most cases, before the hand stops going out on its own. If a root sits underneath the habit, that runs on its own clock and does not respond to repetition alone, which is the part that catches people out. Twenty disciplined evenings do a great deal for a habit and very little for a lid.

Is that frustrating? Yes, and I would rather say it plainly than sell a faster route that does not exist. The people who actually change this are the ones still running dull repetitions in month three, long after the insight stopped being interesting.

## What is worth doing tonight, before you change anything else?

One thing, and it takes about sixty seconds. Tonight, when you notice the scrolling has gone past its use, do not put the phone down yet. Put it face down on your chest, leave it there, and finish this sentence in a note or out loud: "If I put this down right now, the thing I would have to feel is ____." Do not fix the answer. Do not act on it. Do not decide whether it is reasonable. Write the word that arrives. Most people already know what belongs in that blank, and have arranged an hour a night around never writing it down.

Do that for three nights and you will have something better than a screen time report. You will have your root, in your own handwriting, which is the piece no app and no article can hand you.
