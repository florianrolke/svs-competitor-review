    It becomes informative when the calls you keep avoiding are specifically the ones

- a: Because ending things by explanation would require naming a discomfort the whole
    strategy exists to keep unnamed. Disappearing removes the conversation entirely,
    and it works within minutes. From inside it rarely feels cruel. It feels like
    relief, followed by a low background guilt that gets managed by not thinking about
    it. The people most likely to be ghosted are often the ones who got closest, which
    is the part that surprises everybody.
  q: Why do I ghost people I actually like?
- a: Because ending the relationship removes the ongoing demand to manage closeness
    in real time, so the deactivating system gets to stand down. What arrives is lightness,
    clarity, a sense of finally being able to think straight. That relief is the system
    settling rather than evidence the relationship meant little. The loss usually
    catches up later, sometimes weeks later, and the delay convinces plenty of people
    they never cared at all.

    same sentence can be easy at a bus stop and impossible at your own kitchen table.

    a caregiver once failed to provide. The friendships are real. They simply never

# What is avoidant attachment?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Avoidant attachment is a learned strategy for turning down the signal that asks somebody to come closer. It forms where reaching out reliably produced nothing, so a nervous system stopped broadcasting the need rather than stopped having it. In adult life it shows up as withdrawal that arrives right after things go well, and it loosens through small repeated experiences of needing somebody and being met.

Somebody says I love you for the first time and your stomach drops instead of warming. You say something back, and what you say is kind and true, and underneath it a quiet piece of arithmetic has already started about how much of you is now visible and how quickly that could be used.

Nothing in that moment reads as fear from the inside. It reads as having a slightly cooler head than the person across from you, which is the main reason this pattern can run for thirty years without anybody, including you, thinking to examine it.

This page is the other half of a pair. [Anxious attachment](/patterns/anxious-attachment-style/) and avoidant attachment are two answers to one question, and the question is whether a need for closeness, expressed out loud, will actually be met. One answer is to turn the signal up until something responds. The other is to turn it down and stop sending it. They look like opposite personalities from outside and they grow from the same soil, which is why the two of them keep ending up in the same kitchen.

## What Does Avoidant Attachment Sound Like From the Inside?

It sounds like a set of quiet, sensible-seeming rules about how much of yourself it is safe to hand anybody, rules so old that they stopped registering as thoughts a long time ago.

The sentences below are the lines people actually run in their own heads, kept in their own phrasing rather than smoothed into a description. They recur with an unnerving consistency across people who have never met and who would describe their lives as entirely different, which is the first clue that this is something learned rather than something anybody is.

- "If I open up about my issues, people might see me as weak."
- "I don't want to burden others with my problems."
- "I'm afraid of being vulnerable and showing others that I need help."
- "I don't want to face this alone, but I'm too proud to ask for help."
- "I can't face this alone, but I'm too afraid to ask for help."
- "I'm afraid of losing what little control I have."
- "I prefer to keep things light and avoid heavy issues."
- "I don't want to disturb the peace by bringing up issues."
- "I'm preserving my energy by not engaging with this problem."
- "It's better to maintain a facade than to expose the issue."

Read them slowly. What matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word, with no translation required.

Notice how reasonable they sound. Almost every one of them presents as consideration, efficiency, or a mature preference for calm. Two of them are outright arguments for good manners. Nobody goes looking for a cure for being considerate, which is precisely how this survives.

Notice also the pair sitting fourth and fifth in the list, because they are the ones that give the game away. *I can't face this alone, but I'm too afraid to ask for help.* The need is right there, fully formed and clearly felt. What has been cut is the route from the need to a sentence anybody else could hear.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, before any of the practice further down, because this boundary matters more than every technique below it put together.

If distance is being used against you in a relationship, warmth restored the moment you back down and withdrawn the moment you assert something, that is a control dynamic rather than a capacity problem, and it belongs with a licensed professional. Bring in people who can genuinely help, including people who can help you leave if that is what the situation calls for.

The same holds from the other side. If closeness reliably brings back memories of being hurt, or if you are carrying an addiction, a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, a hopelessness that has not lifted in months, or any thought of harming yourself, however fast it passes, that belongs with a clinician rather than with a self-directed practice. Everything here is built for the everyday version: the lease that never gets signed, the call you did not return, the fortnight of being unusually busy at work.

One more boundary. Attachment language describes a tendency, and no page or quiz can assess anybody. Nothing here is a diagnosis and nothing here is a type you now belong to.

## Where Does Avoidant Attachment Actually Come From?

From an environment where reaching out produced nothing often enough that reaching out stopped being worth the cost.

The formation happens without ceremony and usually without a memorable scene. Attachment behavior starts as a signaling system. A small child cries, reaches, follows, and the signal exists for one purpose, which is to bring an adult close enough to settle a nervous system that cannot yet settle itself. The system runs on feedback. Signal goes out, response comes back, the child learns that the signal works and can relax between rounds.

Where that feedback fails repeatedly, something has to give. Either nothing comes back at all, or what comes back treats the need itself as an imposition: a sigh, a flat look, an adult who is technically present and emotionally somewhere else. The child is not being harmed in any way a social worker would record. Their emotional presence simply goes unregistered, week after ordinary week. [The fuller account of how an absence does this much damage has its own page](/patterns/childhood-emotional-neglect/), and it explains why so many people in this pattern describe their childhood as fine and are telling the truth as they know it.

Then comes the substitution that does the lasting work. Where warmth cannot be requested, it gets earned instead. Achievement, usefulness, being no trouble, being the capable one, being the child nobody had to worry about. Every one of those is rewarded generously by adults, teachers, and later by employers, which means the strategy gets reinforced by the whole world for decades. A person can arrive at forty with an impeccable record of competence, considerable affection from other people, and no working route for asking anybody for anything.

Not every case traces back to a parent. A household reorganised around one adult's illness where everybody quietly agreed to need less. A stretch of years where showing a feeling at school was expensive. A first serious relationship where being open once got used against you in a way you have never really described to anyone. The mechanism holds across all of them. Expressing the need turned out to be the costly part, and a person intelligent enough to notice adjusted accordingly.

## How Does Deactivation Actually Work?

By suppressing the output of the attachment system while leaving the system itself running underneath.

This is the part that generic advice about vulnerability never gets to, and it is the whole mechanism, so it is worth being precise about.

A nervous system facing repeated failed bids for closeness has two structurally different options available. The first is to turn the signal up: escalate, stay alert to every sign of somebody leaving, keep broadcasting louder until something responds. That is hyperactivation, and it is what [the anxious pattern](/patterns/anxious-attachment-style/) is built out of. The second option is to turn the signal down. Stop broadcasting, because broadcasting has been tested, trial after trial, and shown not to produce reliable comfort, and sometimes to produce something worse.

That second option is avoidant attachment, and the technical word for it is deactivation. The attachment system stays intact. The need for closeness stays intact. What gets suppressed is the output, and the suppression is aimed at the signal because the signal is what the evidence identified as the dangerous part.

Three consequences follow, and each one explains something that otherwise looks like a character flaw.

**It runs before words.** Once deactivation is the trained default, it operates automatically and pre-verbally. There is no internal debate about whether to ask somebody for help, no moment where you weigh it up and decline. The asking gets filtered out upstream, before it ever becomes a conscious option. What reaches awareness is a mild, reasonable-sounding conclusion, arriving fully formed: *if I don't think about it, maybe it will go away.*

**Closeness gets handled the way any unresolved problem gets handled.** Inside a system built on avoidance, the governing fear about any difficult thing is containment. Open it and it will not stay contained to one exchange. Let somebody in emotionally and the same logic applies with force: this will not stop at one honest conversation, it opens a door that cannot be closed again, and there is no telling what follows it through. People describe the fear almost identically, as a worry that dealing with one thing means facing everything else too. So the door stays shut, and the reason it stays shut has very little to do with whoever happens to be standing at it.

**The moves are pre-emptive rather than reactive.** The sudden distance, the flaw that appeared overnight, the two weeks of unusual pressure at work, the withdrawal that arrives exactly when a relationship would otherwise deepen. None of these are usually about the current partner. They are the attachment system defending against a need it decided long ago could not be safely expressed. Which is why the timing is so strange to everybody watching. The pulling away follows the good week, the first trip together, the moment somebody said something true.

That last point is the single most useful thing on this page. Deactivation tracks intimacy, not conflict. If you want to find it in your own history, do not look at the fights. Look at the fortnight after each of the good ones.

## Why Does Just Open Up Fail So Reliably?

Because it treats a trained suppression as though it were a missing skill.

The standard advice is familiar to anybody who has looked this up before: open up more, communicate your needs, let people in, practice vulnerability. All of it assumes the person does not know how, and would do it if they had a better script or a bit more courage.

Deactivation sits below where that advice can reach. The numbing runs first and the conscious choice comes afterward, if it comes at all. By the time somebody is aware enough to apply the advice, the need has usually already been filtered out of the picture, so there is nothing available to communicate. Ask a person in this pattern what they need in the middle of a difficult evening and the honest answer is often a genuine blank, which then gets interpreted by everybody present as stonewalling.

There is a second problem, and it is worse. Telling somebody to open up asks them to override the exact protective response that a real, earlier bid for closeness taught them to build. From inside the system, the advice does not read as growth. It reads as an instruction to make the mistake that hurt last time, again, deliberately, at a moment when nothing about the current situation has been established as safer.

And there is a third. Because deactivation includes minimizing the emotional stakes of relationships in general, a large number of people in this pattern do not experience themselves as being in any pain. They feel fine. Mildly disengaged, perhaps, a bit flat, a bit tired of being told they are distant. So advice about communicating more arrives as generic relationship chatter addressed to somebody else entirely, and gets filed accordingly.

Consider what this looks like in practice. Someone praised their whole life for being so independent, never any trouble, ends up seeking help not because a relationship is failing but because they cannot understand why they felt almost nothing at their own wedding. The numbness there is the same system that made them no trouble at seven, still competently doing its job. Nothing shifted quickly. What helped was lowering the target from feeling present at a wedding to noticing one moment in a day where they were not performing being fine.

## Is This Avoidant Attachment or Genuine Independence?

Worth checking carefully, because several very different situations produce identical-looking weeks, and the right response to each one differs completely.

How much solitude somebody wants tells you very little on its own, since plenty of securely attached people want a great deal of time alone and always have. What tells you something is what happens to that preference under pressure. Does the pulling away scale specifically with how close somebody is getting? Does it show up right when the relationship starts requiring something real, reliance, disclosure, a shared plan with a date on it? And does it reverse once things cool back to a safer distance? Withdrawal that tracks intimacy is a defense doing its job. Withdrawal that stays flat across every season of a relationship is a temperament.

| What you are seeing | What it might actually be | The thing that tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Handles everything alone, rarely asks for anything | Genuine capability, or a trained refusal | What it costs internally on the rare occasion you do ask. Proportionate discomfort points one way; disproportionate shame and an urgent need to repay by Friday points the other |
| Wants a lot of time alone | A real preference, or deactivation | Whether the need for space spikes in the days after closeness increased, or stays level all year |
| "I just need space right now" | A boundary, or a deactivating pullback | A boundary is stated in advance and held consistently. A pullback is triggered, and the same person wants closeness a fortnight later |
| Sudden certainty that a partner is wrong for them | Real discernment, or flaw focusing | Timing. Discernment is stable and proportionate. Flaw focus intensifies at commitment milestones and fades when the demand drops |
| "I don't think I feel much about them" | Genuine disinterest, or a suppressed signal | Disinterest is stable from week two. Suppression gets stronger as the relationship improves |
| Pulls close, then flees, then pulls close again | Dismissive avoidance, or the fearful version | Dismissive avoidance stays regulated by deactivating consistently. The fearful version oscillates inside a single week |
| "I'm just not an attachment person" | An absence of bonding, or deactivation | There is almost always one or two exceptions, a grandparent, an old friend, a childhood dog, where real attachment obviously did form |

The first row is the one that hides longest, because both versions produce an identical record of competence and get praised identically by everybody watching. Competence itself tells you nothing here. Only the internal cost of the exception does.

Sit with this table and pick the row matching the last three months, rather than the row matching the worst evening. Most people reading a page like this have already run the argument in their head forty times without ever pinning it to a timeline.

## Where Does It Hide When You Would Not Call It Avoidance?

In seven ordinary arenas, and most people recognize themselves in only two or three of them at first.

| Where it shows up | What it looks like from outside | The sentence underneath |
|---|---|---|
| With a partner | Four months in and they are still "someone I've been hanging out with"; the lease unsigned, the drawer unclaimed | "Solving this problem might require me to be vulnerable, and I can't do that." |
| When you need something | Everybody's first call in a crisis, and you have never once called anybody during your own | "I don't want to burden others with my problems." |
| With commitment generally | Jobs, hobbies, flats and cities held loosely, an exit route quietly preserved in each one | "I'm afraid of losing what little control I have." |
| With conflict | Opinions edited down, disagreement swerved, an odd flexibility about what you actually think | "I don't want to disturb the peace by bringing up issues." |
| With your own feeling | The bare minimum felt and expressed, stoicism worn as a value, flatness read as maturity | "I'm preserving my energy by not engaging with this problem." |
| With warmth arriving toward you | Compliments met with a joke, a correction, or a returned favor within the hour | "It's better to maintain a facade than to expose the issue." |
| When something gets difficult | A conversation that ends by disappearing rather than by ending, sometimes with somebody you liked | "I prefer to keep things light and avoid heavy issues." |

The second row deserves its own paragraph, because it is the one people find hardest to see in themselves. Being needed is safe. Needing is not. That asymmetry gets experienced as generosity, and it functions as a one-way valve installed a long time ago. Somebody can be calm, capable, and genuinely excellent in an emergency for twenty years while never once making the reverse call, and never find it strange until a friend asks the blunt version of the question: when was the last time you asked somebody for something and let them actually give it to you? The absence of an answer is the thing worth working on, rather than the crisis management skill.

The third row is worth watching too, in its physical forms. Frequent redecorating, moving flats, changing cities, restarting from a clean slate. A fresh environment offers the feeling of a new beginning at no emotional cost, which makes it an extremely efficient substitute for the beginning that would actually change something.

## Why Do Avoidant and Anxious Partners Find Each Other So Reliably?

Because each strategy provokes precisely the behavior the other one was built to defend against, and because familiarity gets mistaken for recognition on both sides.

Watch the loop run once. Somebody in the avoidant pattern goes slightly quiet after a close weekend. Their partner, whose alarm is tuned to exactly that, moves toward them and asks whether everything is all right. The extra closeness registers as escalation, so the quiet deepens, which raises the other person's alarm further, which produces another round of reaching. Twenty minutes later two protective systems built in two different houses are running at full volume in one kitchen, and both people are convinced they are dealing with the other person's problem.

Nobody in that loop is behaving badly. The pursuit is a genuine attempt to repair, and the retreat is a genuine attempt to keep the relationship survivable. What makes it so stubborn is that each move is completely rational given the evidence its owner grew up with, and completely inflammatory given the evidence the other one did.

Read the two pages side by side if you can. [The anxious version of this exact mechanism is set out in full here](/patterns/anxious-attachment-style/), from the other chair, and a surprising number of people find they are the pursuer in one relationship and the distancer in another. The same person often runs an anxious pattern with a parent and an avoidant one with a partner, or ran anxious in their twenties and moved to avoidant after a specific ending that they can name to the month. [Why the same dynamic keeps reappearing across different partners is worth settling separately.](/insights/why-do-i-attract-the-same-people/)

The useful part is that either person can slow the loop unilaterally. One extra hour of staying present from the distancer, or one round of pursuit withheld by the pursuer, changes the signal the other system is reacting to. Expect the first attempt to feel worse rather than better, on both sides.

Here is what it looks like when both halves are in the same body. Two people who each describe themselves as low maintenance date for a year. Every time one of them starts to raise something that matters, moving in, a future, a hard feeling, the other becomes suddenly very busy at work for a fortnight. Deactivating strategies rarely announce themselves as rejection. They arrive as legitimate, well-timed external pressure, a deadline, a family obligation, a headache, which happens with suspicious reliability to appear exactly when the relationship asks for more. What changed things for that couple was naming it as a pattern rather than as this particular week's excuse, which let both of them see the timing instead of arguing about the deadline.

## What Is the Difference Between Dismissive and Fearful Avoidance?

Consistency. Dismissive avoidance stays regulated by deactivating reliably, every time. The fearful version oscillates between the anxious and avoidant strategies inside the same relationship, sometimes inside the same week.

Somebody in the fearful pattern wants closeness genuinely, moves toward it, gets it, and then flees from the thing they just asked for. Both get called avoidant in ordinary conversation, and the fearful version sits closer to a blend of the two strategies than to pure deactivation.

The reason this distinction earns its place here is what people conclude about themselves without it. The oscillation gets read as a personality defect. *I'm just inconsistent. I don't know what I want. I ruin things.* Seeing it as a second, distinct pattern with two engines running at once, rather than as evidence of a broken character, changes what a person does next. It also explains the experience of wanting connection and panicking the moment it arrives, which is two accurate systems reporting at the same time rather than one confused one.

Two more words attach to that same oscillating pattern, and one of them is on far heavier rotation than the other. **Fearful avoidant attachment** is the term from the four-category model already described above, and it is used accurately most of the time. **Disorganized attachment** is the one people actually search for, along with disorganised attachment, the same word in British spelling. So is **anxious avoidant**, a fourth name doing double duty, since the infant research used it for the plain avoidant type this page has been describing all along while popular writing now attaches it to the oscillating one. In everyday use the four get treated as synonyms. They did not arrive from the same place, and where the second one came from decides how much weight it can carry.

Disorganized began as a description of infants. The separation-and-reunion procedure that produced the original three classifications had trained observers watching through one-way glass while a caregiver left a room and later came back. Most of what they saw sorted into three organized strategies, the avoidant one among them, which is where the pattern on this page starts. A fourth group did not sort. Going back through the recordings years afterward, coders found children who froze partway across the floor, or moved toward a parent with the head turned away, or produced an approach and a retreat inside the same second. The word picked for that group named the absence of any single organized strategy across a few observed minutes. It described what a small child did in a room on one afternoon. Nobody at the time was handing it to adults.

The popular adult usage takes that word and puts it on something built quite differently. One instrument is a trained coder rating observed behavior from a recording. The other is a person answering questions about themselves on a form. The two were developed for separate purposes and checked in separate ways, and where researchers have given the same people both a coder-rated measure from the developmental tradition and a self-report questionnaire, the agreement between them has come out close to nothing. That result is missing from nearly every popular retelling of this vocabulary. Both measures still describe something real inside their own territory. The narrower and more useful conclusion is that a word carried across from one of them to the other arrives carrying a claim the evidence underneath it does not cover.

| What differs | The infant classification | The adult label |
|---|---|---|
| Who assigns it | A trained coder, from a recording | You, on a form about yourself |
| What gets rated | A few observed minutes after one separation | Years of remembered feeling across many relationships |
| What the word marks | No single organized strategy visible in that episode | Wanting closeness and fearing it at roughly equal strength |
| How it gets checked | Whether two coders watching the same recording agree | Whether your own answers hold together across items |
| What it was built to do | Describe infant behavior inside one procedure | Describe adult self-report in questionnaire research |

There is a cleaner way to hold all four names at once, and it removes most of the damage the boxes do. Adult attachment questionnaires are generally built on two continuous dimensions rather than on four types: roughly, how much anxiety somebody carries about where they stand with people, and how much they avoid depending on anyone. Secure sits low on both. The pattern this page describes sits low on the first and high on the second. The anxious or preoccupied pattern sits high on the first and low on the second. Fearful sits high on both, which is the two engines described a moment ago, drawn as a location instead of as a contradiction. That is a described model, assembled by several research groups across decades rather than produced by one researcher, which is why no name is attached to it here and why there is no single study to send you to. What it gives you is a position on two sliders instead of membership in a box, and sliders move.

Now the limits, stated plainly, because almost nothing written about these four words states them. Attachment style is measured as a tendency, never as a fixed property, and it drifts in three directions at once. It drifts between relationships, so the same person can score one way about a partner and another way about a parent, which is the effect described further up where somebody runs anxious with a mother and avoidant with a partner. It drifts across years. Where the same people have been followed from infancy into adulthood, an early classification carries real predictive weight and later experience keeps overwriting it, which is a considerably softer claim than the one in circulation, that a childhood category accounts for an adult relationship. And it drifts with the instrument, since two questionnaires asking slightly different questions will place the same person in slightly different spots. Reanalyses of the original infant data have gone further and suggested the underlying variation may be continuous rather than made of discrete types at all, which would leave all four of these names as convenient regions on a map rather than things a person has. None of that makes the description useless, and this page would not exist if it did. It does mean that a category collected once, from one form, on one afternoon, is a snapshot rather than a verdict, and that what is circulating under these words is a research vocabulary rather than a clinical category, however confident the account you met it in sounded.

Which leaves the division this page has been making since the top. The four names describe what somebody does with closeness and how much of each strategy is currently showing. None of them says why yours in particular is set where it is. Two people can land in the identical corner out of an early absence and a later ending with nothing in common between them, and the description will read as accurate to both while pointing at completely different work. [The end of the same two dimensions where both of them run low has its own page.](/patterns/secure-attachment/) The word names the position. The reason sits back in the section on where this comes from, and the reason is the part that has to move before the position does.

## Does Somebody in This Pattern Actually Not Care?

They usually care a great deal, and the caring arrives at the room heavily filtered.

Deactivation suppresses expression far more efficiently than it suppresses feeling. Adults who report feeling nothing at all during a separation have been measured showing elevated physiological stress in the same moment, which is worth knowing because it settles an argument that otherwise runs forever. What looks like indifference from outside is a genuinely blocked signal rather than a performance of coolness.

The person in this pattern is often the one most convinced they do not care, since the suppression happens before anything reaches awareness. So insisting that they must feel something rarely lands as true to them, and pressing the point tends to make them more certain of their own coldness, not less. If you love somebody in this pattern, this is the most useful thing to hold. Their report of their own inner state is honest and incomplete at the same time, and no amount of argument will change the report. Only accumulated experience does.

There is a version of this that hurts more than the rest, and it deserves saying plainly. Love you cannot detect over years functions, day to day, a lot like love that is absent. That is a real cost, borne by real people, and none of the mechanism above cancels it. [What the flatness feels like from the inside is covered separately here.](/patterns/emotional-numbness/)

## What Does the Secure End Actually Look Like?

Concrete enough that you could film it, and considerably less impressive than most people expect.

Every one of these patterns is a polarity with two ends, and describing only the wounded end leaves a reader with an accurate account of their problem and nowhere to walk. So here is the other end, translated out of abstraction and into an ordinary Tuesday.

| The deactivated end | The available end | What it looks like on an ordinary Tuesday |
|---|---|---|
| Emotional guardedness | Emotional openness | The "how was your week" question gets the real answer, the first time it is asked |
| Intimacy avoidance | Willingness to be close | The trip in August gets booked in March, in your own calendar, with their name on it |
| Fear of emotional investment | Willingness to invest | You say out loud that you would be sad if this ended |
| Reluctance to ask for help | Asking, and letting it be given | You ask somebody to drive you to the appointment and you let them |
| Suppressing emotions | Expressing them | The remark that stung gets mentioned on the day of the remark |
| Emotional minimalism | A full range | You notice you are sad at four in the afternoon, roughly when it starts, rather than at midnight |
| Withdrawal after closeness | Staying | You are still in the room twenty minutes after something honest was said |
| Skepticism of compliments | Accepting them | Praise arrives, you say thank you, and you add nothing |
| Non-commitment | Commitment | One thing carries your name on it: a lease, a title, a date somebody else is counting on |
| Preference for ambiguity | Willingness to define | You ask what this actually is, and you stay for the answer |
| Cynicism about love | Trust in it | A kind message gets taken at face value on the first read |
| Expectation of disappointment | Openness to being met | You let yourself look forward to Saturday |
| Resignation to loneliness | Expecting company | You assume you will be included, without checking first |

Read the right-hand column carefully, because it is deliberately unremarkable. Nobody in it is confessing anything. There are no overdue conversations and no speeches. The available end of this pattern is mostly a person who can let one fact about their life become evidence that somebody matters to them.

Don't judge yourself if that column reads as slightly out of reach today. The distance between the two columns took a childhood and then two decades of daily rehearsal to build, and nobody crosses it in a good fortnight.

## What Actually Loosens Deactivation?

Repeated, low-stakes experiences of expressing a need and having it land safely, given in doses small enough that the deactivating system does not fire.

That sentence carries all the weight, so read the second half of it again. Doses small enough that the system does not fire. Every attempt to do this in one large brave conversation runs into the same wall, because a large conversation is exactly the level of exposure the strategy was trained to prevent. The repetitions have to be small enough to be boring.

1. **Learn to detect the body before you try to name the feeling.** Deactivation numbs the signal before it reaches conscious feeling, so the first task is smaller than knowing what you feel. It is noticing that something happened at all. The chest tightening as you read a message. The hands cooling. The sudden, urgent interest in a task you did not care about ten seconds ago. Two ten-second checks a day is a serious practice. [There is a separate page on how to actually feel a feeling rather than think about it.](/insights/how-to-feel-your-emotions/)
2. **Treat the deactivating move as information rather than instruction.** The flaw that appeared overnight, the urge to cancel, the fortnight of work pressure. When one fires, write it down with the date, then write what happened in the forty-eight hours before it. You are looking for a pattern in the timing, and the pattern is usually visible after about six entries.
3. **Ask for one small thing a week, and let it be given.** Genuinely small. A lift to the station, help carrying something, somebody's opinion on a decision you could have made alone. The favor is beside the point. What is being practiced is the complete loop, from noticing a need to voicing it to receiving something, at a dose low enough to survive.
4. **Then do not repay it.** No returned favor that evening, no self-deprecating remark to level the exchange, no thank-you card. The immediate rebalancing is the same reflex working on the incoming side, and skipping it is harder than the asking for a lot of people.
5. **Stay for the twenty minutes.** After something honest has been said in a room, set yourself the smallest possible task: remain, seated, without producing a solution and without checking your phone. Discovering experientially that the twenty minutes end and everybody survives them is what actually loosens the root. [The mechanics of that exit and how to catch it are set out here.](/patterns/emotionally-unavailable/)
6. **Say one true sentence a day, on the day.** Five words is enough. "That meeting embarrassed me." The target is frequency rather than depth, because the route being rebuilt runs from noticing to saying, and routes get built by traffic.
7. **Land one fact.** Pick one piece of external evidence that a relationship exists and let it stand: a title used out loud in front of a colleague, a date in a shared calendar, your name on something. Choose it small enough to be survivable and real enough to count. Notice what happens in your chest in the ninety seconds afterward, and let that be data rather than a verdict.
8. **Name the pattern once, plainly, to one person.** Without a label attached and without asking them to do anything about it. "I tend to go quiet after good weekends, and I am working on it" opens a conversation. Handing somebody a category closes one.
9. **Lower the bar from breakthrough to noticing.** Instead of aiming to feel present at the big occasion, aim to notice one moment in a day where you were performing being fine. That target is reachable on a bad week, which is the only reason it works.

Consider what actual movement looks like. Somebody four years into a relationship still has not fully unpacked, keeps their name off the lease, and describes the whole thing to extended family as casual long after it obviously is not. Progress did not begin with a conversation about commitment. It began with noticing, out loud and in the moment, the specific tightness in the chest that arrived just before each not yet, and treating that tightness as information instead of as an instruction.

The other lever is a relationship. Deactivation loosens fastest around somebody who behaves securely and consistently, a therapist, a steady friend, a partner who does not escalate when you go quiet, functioning as a practice relationship. Treat that as a reason to notice the steady people already in your life and run the repetitions there first, where the stakes are lowest, rather than as an instruction to go hunting for a better partner.

Is any of this comfortable? No. It is small and freaking repetitive, and it works far better than a single overdue conversation ever has. Backslides under stress are normal and expected. A big life event, an illness, a milestone in the relationship itself, and the old strategy comes back online at full strength for a while. That happening is ordinary rather than evidence the work is failing.

## What Happens to Your Relationships While You Practice This?

They usually get briefly stranger before they get more honest, which is worth knowing in advance.

Some of the distance was load-bearing for both people. A partner who spent three years learning not to ask you anything directly may not immediately notice that you have started answering, and may test it a few times in ways that feel clumsy. A friend used to a certain register may find a true sentence from you unsettling. Saying out loud what you are practicing handles most of that.

If you are with somebody who moves toward you when you move away, expect something counterintuitive. Staying present for one extra hour frequently produces less pursuit from them, sometimes within days, because the thing they were reaching for is no longer receding. That is disorienting, and it is also useful information about what the loop was actually made of.

The honest limitation belongs here too. A small number of arrangements were genuinely built on your unavailability, and those get renegotiated or they end. Friendships usually improve first, since the stakes are lower and the repetitions are easier to run. The parent relationship moves slowest, which is normal, because it has been running the longest. Start smaller there than anywhere else.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Longer than a week, and the honest answer runs at two different speeds.

Recognition arrives quickly. Most people can catch a deactivating move mid-flight within a few weeks of deliberate attention: seeing the flaw assemble itself, watching the busy fortnight get scheduled, feeling the chest tighten a second before the not yet leaves your mouth. Catching it counts even on the days you obey it anyway.

The reflex takes far longer, months to years rather than weeks, because it was rehearsed daily for decades and it fades at the pace of accumulated experience rather than understanding. Reading this page changes remarkably little on its own. Fifty small needs voiced and met, in rooms where almost nothing was at stake, change a great deal. Expect a step pattern with long flat stretches and a bad month after any real ending.

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. The thing I underestimated longest was how much of this is capacity rather than character. People who look self-sufficient are usually people who never got fifty low-stakes repetitions at needing somebody and having it go fine. Where it sits for me today is worth saying plainly: the reflex to handle it alone has quietened a lot over the years, and it has not gone to zero, and I would rather tell you that than sell a finished version of something nobody finishes.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From attachment research that has been public for decades. Five strands carry most of the weight on this page, and every one of them can be checked without going through me.

John Bowlby, a British psychiatrist, set out the idea that closeness runs as a behavioral system with its own signals, across three volumes published between 1969 and 1980. Mary Ainsworth made it observable, running the Strange Situation procedure in Baltimore through the 1960s and 70s and reporting it in *Patterns of Attachment* in 1978, and the avoidant classification described on this page starts there. Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver carried the vocabulary into adult romantic relationships in a 1987 paper, which is the reason anybody discusses attachment styles in couples at all, and Kim Bartholomew and Leonard Horowitz split the avoidant end into its dismissive and fearful versions in a four-category model published in 1991. The words deactivating and hyperactivating, used throughout this page, are laid out in Mario Mikulincer and Phillip Shaver's *Attachment in Adulthood*. The finding that somebody can report feeling nothing during a separation while their body says otherwise comes from Dozier and Kobak, whose 1992 study measured skin conductance climbing during attachment interviews in exactly the people who described the least distress.

The rest of it is mine and carries no research weight at all: the five years and five thousand hours described above, with no clinical qualification behind them. None of the researchers named has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Decide Anything About Anybody?

Run a pullback log for two weeks. Open a note on your phone. Every time you notice a move away from somebody, an urge to cancel, a flaw that arrived overnight, a reply you could have sent immediately and did not, a sudden certainty that you need space, write two lines. Line one is the date and the move itself, in five words. Line two is what happened in the forty-eight hours before it.

Then read the second column only, straight through, ignoring the first. Most people find the same thing and find it slightly unsettling: a good dinner, a compliment that landed, a plan for next year, an evening where somebody was genuinely kind. A record like that will not stop the next pullback. It does build the one piece of evidence this pattern has never had, which is a written account of withdrawal arriving on the good days. Your memory will not assemble that record on its own, because it was trained to file the withdrawal as a reasonable response to something that must have been wrong.
