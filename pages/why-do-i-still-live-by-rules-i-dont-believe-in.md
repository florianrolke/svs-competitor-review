# Why am I still following rules I stopped believing in?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because the rule was never running on the belief. A rule is a pass condition somebody attached to a value, and obeying it keeps proving to an internal audience that the value still matters. You can reject the reasoning entirely and the condition keeps firing. Rules are the most arbitrary layer of the whole structure, which also makes them the fastest thing to edit.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
Read these as sentences you might have said, rather than as descriptions of somebody else.

- "I have to be perfect to be loved."
- "I do not want to help people. I have to."
- "I feel like my life is bogged down with things I still have to do."
- "I know exactly what this pattern is and where it comes from, and I did it again last night."

A condition you can already argue against, still running your evening, is a specific and editable thing rather than a flaw in your character.

[How a seminar week works on a rule you no longer believe in](https://florianrolke.com/the-soulsearching-experience/)
</div>

You are standing in the kitchen at nine in the evening, wiping a counter that was already clean, because sitting down before everything is finished still feels like something you have not earned. The real list ran out an hour ago. You could take this rule apart in thirty seconds if a friend described it to you about their own life, and you are still standing there with the cloth in your hand.

Most people arrive at a page like this having already done the part that was supposed to work. You traced it back. You can name the house it was installed in, you can describe the exact tone of voice it arrived in, and you can say out loud, with complete sincerity, that you no longer believe a word of the reasoning behind it. Then Tuesday comes around and you obey it again, and the gap between what you believe and what you actually do starts to look like proof that something is wrong with you.

That gap is information. Repeatedly failing to do something you have genuinely decided to do is worth investigating rather than punishing, and an investigation here turns up the same finding almost every time: the rule was never obeyed on the strength of the belief in the first place, so removing the belief was always going to leave it running. [The general version of why knowing the answer changes nothing has its own page](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/). This one is about the specific structure that keeps a condition alive after its reasoning has died.

One plain line before any of it. If what you are carrying is heavier than this, if a low mood has not lifted for months, if there is an addiction in the picture, or if there is any thought of harming yourself however quickly it passes, that belongs with a licensed professional ahead of anything written here. And if a difficulty of this kind has been persistent, physically exhausting, or present for as long as you can remember, it is worth raising with a doctor or another qualified professional; this page is about the pattern rather than about any medical question.

## What Does This Look Like on an Ordinary Week?

Almost entirely unremarkable, which is exactly how something can run for thirty years without ever getting named.

- Saying yes to something you had no actual room for, and noticing afterward that no never even arrived as an option you considered.
- Standing in the kitchen finding one more thing to do before you are allowed to sit down, when the real list ran out an hour ago.
- Catching yourself mid-explanation, defending a decision out loud to nobody, in the exact tone you would use with a parent who is not in the room.
- Realizing the rule you are currently obeying is one you would talk a friend out of in five minutes flat if they described it to you about their own life.
- Noticing a specific, low dread before a call with a parent, tied to a subject where you already know in advance that you will agree to whatever they want.
- Being able to say, instantly, the precise thing that would make today acceptable, and realizing you would not have chosen any of it if nobody were grading you.

Every item on that list is a condition executing exactly as designed, on time, without asking your permission first. That is worth sitting with for a moment, because the usual reading of the same list is character. Weak. Inconsistent. Says one thing and does another. A structural reading gets you somewhere; a character reading gets you another decade of trying harder at the wrong thing.

## What Is the Difference Between a Value and a Rule?

A value is a feeling you want to feel. A rule is the specific condition that has to be met before you are allowed to feel it.

Values are things like safety, worth, being loved, belonging, being someone people can count on. They are available in principle at any hour of any day, and nothing about a value specifies a threshold. Rules supply the threshold, and they supply it in a form that sounds like a fact. Not I want to feel enough, but I am only enough if I finish everything on the list, if I never say no, if nobody ever catches me resting.

That distinction sounds academic until you notice which of the two you have been trying to change. Most people who want to stop living this way go after the value, which is the largest and most frightening edit available. They try to care less about being loved, or less about being reliable, or less about mattering to anyone. It rarely works, and it should not work, because the value is usually fine. The pass condition bolted onto it is the arbitrary part.

| The value underneath | The rule attached to it | What the rule actually demands |
|---|---|---|
| Being someone people can rely on | I am reliable only if I finish everything | Nobody ever sees you stop |
| Being loved | I have to be perfect to be loved | No visible flaw, at any point, ever |
| Being safe inside a family | I hold it together or it falls apart | You are never the one who needs holding |
| Being worth something | I count only when I am producing | Rest has to be earned before it is allowed |
| Being a good person | I am good only if nobody is disappointed | Every no gets paid for somewhere else |
| Belonging | I belong only while I am useful | Being wanted for nothing in particular is unavailable |

Read the middle column, then read the left one. Almost nobody would defend a single entry in the middle if asked about it directly and calmly. Almost everybody would defend the left. That asymmetry is the whole opening, and it is the reason this layer is worth going after first.

Rules are also the cheapest thing in the structure to change, for an unglamorous reason. Nobody has an actual emotional investment in the exact threshold. The investment sits entirely in what the threshold was recruited to prove. Move the proof somewhere else and the number becomes negotiable in a way that values and identity never are.

There is one more thing about rules worth knowing before anything else. A rule can be entirely self-enforced, run silently, in your own internal voice, using your own vocabulary, and still have been assembled from somebody else's terms. Self-enforcement tells you how thoroughly the thing was absorbed rather than who originally wrote it.

## Why Does the Loyalty Outlast the Reasoning?

Because obeying the rule is currently doing a job that has nothing to do with whether the rule makes sense.

When the condition was installed, a belief usually came with it. An explanation for why the condition mattered. My parent will love me less if I do not. My family comes apart if I stop holding it together. If I am not the easy one, there will be nothing left for me here. Years later the adult mind can pick that sentence up, turn it over, examine it properly, and put it down for good. The belief genuinely goes.

The rule keeps running because the loyalty attached to it was always doing a separate job. Continuing to obey is how you keep proving, to an audience installed at the same moment as the rule, that the relationship or the value or the identity behind it still matters to you. The proof is the point. Dropping the rule feels, structurally, less like updating a policy and more like walking out on somebody.

| What you did | What it touched | What it left running |
|---|---|---|
| Worked out where the rule came from | The history | The job the rule is doing this week |
| Decided the reasoning behind it was wrong | The content | The loyalty, which was never running on the content |
| Told a friend you no longer believe any of it | Your stated position | The internal audience the rule reports to |
| Read something that named the pattern precisely | Your vocabulary for it | The behavior on the next ordinary evening |
| Had one genuinely good conversation about it | The clarity | The need the rule is still the cheapest way to meet |
| Resolved, sincerely, to stop | Your intention | The relief that follows obeying, which reinstalls it every time |

Look down the third column. None of those entries is unusual or shameful. They describe a system with two layers that do not automatically update each other, which is a mundane engineering fact rather than a personal failing. Content changed. Function did not. From the inside that reads as hypocrisy, and it is a great deal more ordinary than hypocrisy.

The relief in the last row deserves its own line, because it is the part that keeps the whole thing alive. Every time you obey the rule, something settles. The audit goes quiet. That quiet is genuine and it arrives fast, which teaches the sequence to run again next time, exactly the way any behavior that reliably produces relief gets taught. Arguing with a structure that pays out every single day, in real relief, is a losing proposition, and it has nothing to do with how good your arguments are.

## What Does This Sound Like From the Inside?

Like obligation, in language that has quietly outrun the feeling underneath it.

The sentences below are how people actually phrase this to themselves, at that level rather than smoothed into a description of a mood. What matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word, with no translation required.

- "I don't like what I'm doing day to day. I should be doing something else with my life. I just have these images of what I should be doing that I'm not."
- "I have to do all these things. I really don't want to do that. I'm stuck doing them day in and day out. I hate it. That's how I feel sometimes."
- "I have to hide what I actually feel, or I will be too much for them."
- "I do not think I can change this. I have looked at it from every angle already."

Two things about those are worth noticing. The first is the density of have to and should in language describing a life the speaker chose, in circumstances nobody is forcing on them. Flatly compulsory grammar attached to ordinary tasks is one of the most reliable tells there is, and it is worth testing rather than believing. Ask what you would choose if no condition were attached and nobody were keeping score. Some of it survives that test and turns out to be a real want wearing the costume of obligation. The rest turns out to be compliance with an old agreement nobody has renegotiated.

The second is that last sentence, the one about having looked at it from every angle already. That sentence is extremely common in people who have done a great deal of honest work on themselves, and it is almost always describing the wrong layer. Looking at it from every angle usually means examining the reasoning from every angle, which is exhaustive work performed on the one part of the structure that was never load-bearing.

## Why Doesn't Arguing With the Rule Work?

Because argument aims at the content, and the rule was hired for its function.

Three pieces of standard advice show up here constantly, and each one misses in a slightly different way.

1. **Just stop caring what they think.** This treats the rule as a preference you could set down if you chose to. It also asks you to remove a need without offering anything to cover it, which is why the resolve holds for about eleven days. Nobody stops caring on instruction, and the attempt mostly adds a second layer of failure to be judged against.
2. **Decide to let it go.** Letting go removes the condition and leaves the job it was doing completely unassigned. Certainty, significance, safety, being loved: whatever the rule was supplying still has to come from somewhere, and when nothing else is supplying it, the old condition slides back in quietly, usually during a stressful week, usually without any announcement.
3. **Find the limiting belief and replace it with a better one.** The most sophisticated of the three and still aimed at the tabletop. Direct contradiction attacks what the rule says, which triggers a defense, and defending it makes you rehearse your reasons for keeping it. A rule argued with in this way frequently comes out of the conversation stronger than it went in.

| The standard advice | What it actually supplies | What it leaves untouched |
|---|---|---|
| Stop caring what they think | A decision, sincerely made | The need that the caring was covering |
| Just let it go | Permission | The job the rule was hired to perform |
| Replace the limiting belief | A better proposition | The loyalty, which was never holding a proposition |
| Set a boundary and stick to it | A script for the outside world | The internal audit that runs with nobody present |
| Have more self-discipline | Force applied to one side | The collision, or the moving bar, that force cannot fix |
| Confront the person who set it | A scene, and often a rupture | The condition itself, which does not require their cooperation |

The deeper issue with all six is that they treat this as a question of ability. Change here runs on motivation instead. You are not lacking the capacity to leave a counter unwiped at nine in the evening. What is missing is anything you value more than what the rule currently pays you, and until that gets named and made concrete, the capacity is irrelevant.

That reframing matters practically. Everything worth doing here starts with what the rule is worth to you today, in real terms, and what a cheaper substitute would have to cover. [Insight arriving on its own and changing nothing is the general version of this problem](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/), and [the reason a genuinely convincing realization keeps failing to convert into behavior has its own page too](/patterns/why-cant-i-change/).

## Is Your Rule Unmeetable, or Are Two of Them Colliding?

Two shapes produce most of the visible trouble, they generate identical exhaustion, and the fix that helps one does nothing whatsoever for the other.

An unmeetable rule sets a condition the value can never register against. The bar sits slightly beyond wherever you are, and it moves when you reach it, because its job is proving something rather than being cleared. Effort goes in continuously and the feeling the effort was for never arrives. People in this shape can describe the finish line in precise detail and have never once crossed it.

A conflicting pair sets two conditions that cannot both be satisfied on the same day. Be endlessly available and be endlessly productive. Be humble and be exceptional. Be fully present at home and never let anyone down at work. Every choice satisfies one and violates the other, so a loss gets paid regardless of what you decide, and the payment happens quietly, all day, with nothing visible to point at.

| | An unmeetable rule | Two rules colliding |
|---|---|---|
| What it sounds like from inside | I did everything and it still was not enough | I cannot win whichever way I go |
| Where the effort goes | Into clearing a bar that moves | Into holding two bars that cancel each other |
| What the tiredness feels like | Flat and chronic, with a sense of never arriving | Sharp, with guilt attached to every decision |
| The reliable tell | You can describe the finish line exactly and have never crossed it | You can name two things you are supposed to be and they contradict |
| What actually helps | Rewriting the threshold to something that can be met | Naming the collision out loud, then editing one side |
| What does nothing | Working harder at the same bar | Better time management |

Both shapes get read the same way from outside and, more damagingly, from inside: as generic tiredness, or as a personal shortage of discipline. Someone holding two colliding rules will buy a planner. Someone holding an unmeetable rule will get up earlier. Neither intervention touches the structure, and both add another failed attempt to the pile of evidence about their character.

Consider someone holding two inherited standards at once, one that says be endlessly available to family and one that says never let anyone down at work. Both were absorbed young, both feel like basic decency, and no single day exists in which both can be honored. What they described was exhaustion, and what they blamed was poor time management, which is what gets blamed when a collision stays invisible. Writing both conditions down side by side, in their own words, made the structure visible for the first time in twenty years. Editing one of them so that satisfying it no longer automatically broke the other did more in a fortnight than a decade of getting up earlier.

## Where Did the Rule Come From, and Does Naming That Make Anyone Wrong?

From a household where approval, attention, or safety was scarce or unpredictable, and no, naming that makes nobody wrong.

Here is the mechanism, said plainly. A child watches which behaviors bring warmth and which bring cold, or silence, or trouble. From that evidence the child works out a condition: this exact thing is what it costs to be safe here, or loved here, or left alone here. Given what a child can actually know, that conclusion was correct. It was good inference from limited data, and it was probably useful at the time.

What decides which rules get installed is scarcity rather than the amount of love in the house. That single fact explains a great deal that otherwise looks unfair. The parent whose approval was hardest to win usually sets the standard that survives, even in homes with plenty of affection in them, and even when the more generous parent was right there the whole time. Scarcity is what makes a condition memorable. Abundance rarely leaves a rule behind, because a child has no reason to work out the price of something that was simply available.

Now the release, which is the one move on this page that has to be handled carefully, because it is the difference between a page you can act on and a page that ends in a grievance.

The standard you inherited was very likely not working well for the person who handed it down either. Whoever installed it was mostly running conditions they had absorbed themselves, from their own scarce source, and paying the same kind of price for them. That observation can be made honestly, in full, without arriving anywhere near the conclusion that they were wrong to love you the way they did. It removes the loyalty holding the rule in place, since loyalty to a standard that was failing its original holder is a much stranger thing to keep paying for, and it removes nothing else.

The two intuitive alternatives both fail, reliably. Keep defending the rule out of loyalty and nothing changes. Attack the parent behind it and the loyalty structure defends them, on their behalf, taking the possibility of change down with it. The third option is the only one that frees you without asking you to make anyone wrong.

None of this is an instruction about what to do to or about them. Nothing on this page requires a conversation, a confrontation, an apology, an announcement, or a decision about contact. [Whether to change the relationship itself is a separate question with its own page](/patterns/should-i-go-no-contact-with-a-parent/). [What it does to a person when the expectations were too high in the first place is covered elsewhere](/patterns/unrealistic-parental-expectations/), and [so is the version where the control is still active in the present rather than installed in the past](/patterns/controlling-parents/). This page keeps the object of examination on the condition, in your own head, today.

## Is This a Discipline Problem?

Rarely, and treating it as one is a large part of why the last five attempts failed.

You already know what you should do. You have known for years. The repeated failure to do it is information about the structure you are working inside rather than a verdict on your character, and reading it the second way costs you the actual finding. Discipline aimed at an unmeetable rule raises the bar. Discipline aimed at a collision applies force to one side and increases the cost on the other. In both cases the effort is real, the result is worse, and the conclusion drawn afterward is that you are the problem.

There is a specific version of this that catches thoughtful people. Someone who has done a great deal of work on themselves will look at the same failure and conclude, with real sadness, that they have looked at this from every angle and cannot change it. What they have usually examined exhaustively is the reasoning, which was never holding the thing up. The angle that has not been tried is the boring one: what does obeying this actually pay me, right now, and what would cover the same payment for less.

[Whether a persistent failure to follow through is a discipline question at all is worth settling separately](/patterns/is-this-a-discipline-problem-or-something-else/), and [the reason motivation reliably arrives and then leaves has its own page as well](/patterns/why-does-motivation-always-run-out/).

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because six different things produce the same uncomfortable Tuesday and only one of them is what this page decodes.

| What it feels like | What it might actually be | The thing that tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| I keep doing something I do not believe in | An inherited condition still doing a job | You can state it as a sentence and name who first held you to that exact line |
| I believe something untrue about the world | A limiting belief | It is a claim about how things are, and it moves when its evidence gets examined |
| I feel a spike of guilt every time I stop | Rest filed as something that has to be earned | The guilt fires on stopping specifically, whatever else the day contained |
| I cannot say no to anybody | People pleasing, which needs an audience | It goes quiet when nobody is watching or would ever find out |
| My worth rises and falls with what I produced | Worth made conditional on output | A day with nothing produced in it changes your estimate of yourself |
| Somebody is still setting my terms right now | Active control in the present | Consequences arrive from an actual person, in real time, when you deviate |

Three of those have better pages than this one. [A proposition about how the world works moves through a different route entirely](/patterns/limiting-beliefs/), because evidence is what holds it up and evidence is what changes it. [Guilt that fires the moment you sit down has its own mechanism](/patterns/why-do-i-feel-guilty-when-i-rest/). [Worth that gets recalculated daily against output belongs here instead](/patterns/conditional-self-worth/), and [the version organized around other people's reactions in the moment is set out separately](/patterns/how-to-stop-people-pleasing/).

There is a seventh possibility that deserves saying, because pages like this tend to pathologize everything they touch. Some of your conditions are simply preferences you would keep. The test is cheap: imagine dropping it and check whether anything in your chest moves. A preference releases without charge. A rule releases with a small jolt of guilt or dread, and that jolt is the loyalty reporting in.

## What Actually Moves a Rule You Have Stopped Believing In?

Writing it down as a literal sentence, naming the job it was hired for, covering that job more cheaply, then rehearsing the replacement against the exact situations where the old one fires.

Nothing below is a validated protocol, and anybody offering you one for this is overselling something. What follows is worked out from how the structure holds, and it is built so that a single person can run all of it alone, in private, with nobody else's participation, agreement, or knowledge.

1. **Write the rule out as the literal sentence it is.** The exact if and then. Today counts as acceptable if I have finished everything on the list. I am allowed to rest once nobody could accuse me of slacking. I am a good daughter if I never say no to her. A condition that has run long enough stops feeling like a condition and starts feeling like a description of reality, and reality does not get edited. A sentence on a page has an author and a date, and anything with an author and a date is available for revision.
2. **Find who first held you to exactly that line.** Not roughly that line. Exactly it, with the same threshold in it. Ask at what age, and what happened when you missed. The specificity is the point, because a general sense of inherited pressure gives you nothing to work with, while one concrete threshold with a face attached is workable within an afternoon.
3. **Name the job the rule was hired for.** What was it protecting, providing, or proving, and to whom. Usually one of a small set: staying safe, staying loved, staying significant, staying connected, keeping things certain. [The needs that sit underneath most of these are laid out in full separately](/patterns/six-human-needs/). A rule that has been doing real work for twenty years will not be handed over on the strength of an argument, and it will be handed over once something visibly covers the same job for less.
4. **Check which shape you are holding.** Unmeetable, or two colliding. If you cannot tell, take a day that drained you for no visible reason and write down what you were supposed to be during it. Two contradictory entries mean a collision. One entry you have never once satisfied means a moving bar.
5. **Apply the release before you touch the threshold.** Say it deliberately: this standard was very likely not serving the person who handed it down either, and scarcity rather than affection is what decided which conditions got installed. Say it without heat and without a verdict about anybody. This step exists to take the loyalty off the rule while leaving the love exactly where it is, and skipping it is the most common reason the rest of the sequence bounces.
6. **Keep the value and rewrite only the condition.** I am someone people can rely on, and the proof is that I do what I said I would do, rather than that I never stop. The value survives untouched. Write the new condition as something you would genuinely be willing to live by on a bad week, since a replacement you cannot meet is another unmeetable rule wearing a fresh coat.
7. **Rehearse it against the situations where the old one actually fires.** The family phone call. The empty evening. The request that would once have received an automatic yes. An edit that only exists as a good idea reverts the first time a real trigger arrives, and the rehearsal is what converts it into something your hands know. Pick the smallest live instance you can find and start there.
8. **Expect to catch it late, then earlier, then in time to choose.** The first several times you will notice an hour afterward, standing in a kitchen you have already finished cleaning. Then twenty minutes afterward. Then during. Then, eventually, early enough that a decision exists. Each of those transitions takes a stack of real instances rather than one good afternoon of thinking.

Is any of that comfortable? No. Sitting down at nine in the evening with something visibly unfinished, feeling the spike arrive, and staying seated anyway is freaking tedious compared to the version where one enormous realization settles everything, and it is the version that actually holds.

## What Does the Other End of This Look Like?

Considerably quieter than the phrase breaking free suggests, and made almost entirely of small edits nobody else would notice.

| Inside the rule | The other end of it | What it looks like on an ordinary Tuesday |
|---|---|---|
| The condition runs unnamed | The condition is a sentence you can say | You can state in one line what today has to contain to count |
| Obeying automatically | Noticing it fire, then choosing | The yes arrives and you hear it as a yes you could decline |
| Guilt taken as a verdict | Guilt taken as loyalty reporting in | You sit down at nine, feel the spike, and stay sitting |
| The value proved daily | The value simply held | You are reliable without needing anyone to watch you being reliable |
| Two standards colliding in silence | The collision named, one side edited | You can say which two things you were trying to be that day |
| Explaining yourself to an empty room | Deciding without an internal audit | A decision gets made and no defense gets drafted |
| Rest earned in advance | Rest taken | An evening with nothing accomplished in it, and no bill arrives afterward |
| The inherited standard defended | The inherited standard understood | You describe where it came from without heat and without agreeing to it |

Nothing in the middle column is impressive, which is the point. What changes here is the number of hours in a week that belong to a threshold nobody would defend out loud.

Consider someone who spent two decades explicitly rejecting a parent's beliefs about work and worth, and who still could not leave the office before the last task was finished, describing it as just how they are wired. The belief was genuinely gone, examined and dropped years earlier. The rule kept running because obeying it was proving something separate: that being someone who can be counted on still mattered. What moved it was separating those two things on purpose, keeping the value while retiring the condition that demanded it be demonstrated every single day. The overwork itself was never the object worth working on.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Weeks to catch it reliably, months for a replacement to hold, and it never resolves in a single realization.

The two speeds are worth separating, because confusing them is what makes people quit in week two. Recognition is fast. Most people can name one specific condition within a day of looking properly, and can catch it firing within two or three weeks of paying deliberate attention. Catching it counts even on the evenings you go ahead and obey it anyway. Watching yourself wipe an already clean counter, knowing precisely what you are doing and why, feels like failure and is genuinely a step forward.

The replacement takes longer, because a condition that has run for thirty years has thirty years of rehearsal behind it and the new one has none. It holds only once it has been tested against the actual situations where the old one used to fire, which means waiting for real triggers rather than manufacturing practice ones. That is slow by nature. A family phone call happens when it happens.

The honest promise is narrow. The rule gets easier to catch and easier to edit each time it fires. It does not disappear on the first attempt, or the fifth. Expect it to come back at full strength during a hard week, an illness, a bereavement, a stretch of genuine pressure, and treat that as the old sequence still being installed rather than as evidence that none of it worked.

Is that frustrating? Yes. Reading a page like this changes very little on its own. Twenty ordinary evenings where you notice the condition fire and decline it once are worth more than every clear explanation of it you will ever read, including this one.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From clinical and family-systems work that has been in print for between forty and seventy-five years, and every strand of it can be checked without going through me.

The closest established name for this exact mechanism is introjection, set out by Fritz Perls, Laura Perls, and Paul Goodman in *Gestalt Therapy: Excitement and Growth in the Human Personality* in 1951. Their argument is that rules, judgments, and standards can be taken in whole from parents or other authority figures and never properly digested, so they go on operating as though the person had reached those conclusions themselves. Karen Horney had described the felt experience of it a year earlier, in *Neurosis and Human Growth* in 1950, under the phrase the tyranny of the should: a rigid internal set of shoulds making up an idealized self-image that a person feels compelled to live up to whether or not it matches their actual values or temperament. David Burns gave the same territory a widely known and checkable vocabulary in *Feeling Good: The New Mood Therapy* in 1980, popularizing Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy, where should statements appear as one of ten named cognitive distortions producing automatic guilt regardless of whether the underlying rule was ever reasonable. And Murray Bowen's family systems theory, developed from the 1960s onward, supplies the account of why an adult keeps running a household's rules long after leaving the household, under the heading of differentiation of self, the capacity to hold your own beliefs and reactions separately from the ones absorbed from your family of origin.

Those four disagree with each other about plenty and none of them was written with this page in mind. What they share is the finding that matters here: an absorbed standard can keep operating at full strength after the person holding it has stopped endorsing it, and the fix in every account involves examining the standard as an object rather than trying harder to comply with it.

The rest of this is mine and carries no research weight at all. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind. None of the writers named above has any connection to this page or to the seminar, and where their published work and my own experience disagree, theirs is the one worth going and checking.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Change Anything?

Write the sentence. Open a note and finish this line honestly, in your own words, with a real threshold in it: today counts as acceptable if. Do not tidy it, do not make it reasonable, and do not write the version you would say out loud to somebody. Write the one that is actually operating, the one you could have recited instantly at any point this week if anybody had asked what today needed to contain.

Then add three lines underneath it. First, who first held you to exactly that threshold, and at what age. Second, what job that condition was doing for you back then, in one plain phrase: kept me safe, kept me loved, kept me out of trouble, kept the house calm. Third, what you would say to a friend who read you that same sentence about their own life, in the tone you would actually use with them. Most people find the third line arrives fast, fluently, and completely differently from anything they have ever said to themselves. That gap, on a screen, in your own handwriting, does something no amount of arguing with yourself has ever managed. It costs you ten minutes and nothing else, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever go anywhere or tell anybody you did it.
