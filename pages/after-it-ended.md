# Why does a relationship ending hurt in my body, and how long does it actually last?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

The end of an attachment produces physical symptoms in most people. Appetite gone, sleep broken, a weight under the sternum, a body that will not settle in a room. That is the ordinary form this takes rather than a measure of your strength or of what the relationship was worth. It has a shape and a course, and you are almost certainly overestimating both.

The first thing most people want, and almost nobody is offered, is a description.

Not comfort. Not a plan. A description of what this actually is, told at the level of the body and the week, so that the hours stop arriving as evidence that something has gone badly wrong with you. Because that is the quiet second problem here. The ending is one thing. The suspicion that your reaction to it is disproportionate, or embarrassing, or diagnostic of some weakness you had always half suspected, is a separate thing, and it costs more than people admit.

So here is the description.

You are probably not eating properly. Food is either impossible or automatic, and in either case you are not choosing it. There is a jar of something in the fridge that only they ate, and it is still there, and you have decided nothing about it. You are sleeping on one side of a bed you now have entirely to yourself, and the other side stays untouched with an obstinacy that would be funny if it were happening to somebody else. You wake early. Usually the same hour, usually before there is any light, and the thinking is already at full volume when you get there.

Your body reacts to a notification before you have read it. There is a physical drop that lands in the chest and the stomach and arrives well ahead of any thought about what the notification might be. It does this for messages from the dry cleaner.

The practical life has come apart in a way that has surprised you, because you knew you would miss the person and you did not know you would be unable to work out what to do about trash night. Small competencies have gone missing. Some of them were theirs, and you have discovered you never learned them. Some of them were yours, and you have discovered you cannot currently reach them. A cousin's wedding is coming and you do not know whether to go, and the reason you do not know is that you no longer have anybody to decide it with.

And then there is quarter past four in the morning, which deserves its own paragraph, because it is where the behavior lives that people are most ashamed of. Everybody has a version. Reading the same exchange for the ninth time. Checking whether a name has moved in a contact list. Composing something and not sending it, or sending it. Working out where they were on a particular evening. Driving somewhere with no plan for what to do if you arrived. In daylight you would defend none of it, and at quarter past four it is the most reasonable thing available.

None of the above is a character finding. Most of it is not even a choice. The involuntary machinery of a broken attachment and the specific things you do at quarter past four are two different systems, and separating them is most of the useful work on this page, because only one of them is yours to touch.

## What Is Heartbreak, and What Does the Research Actually Establish?

The word you already have is **heartbreak**, and it deserves better than being treated as decoration.

It is an ordinary English word, in ordinary use for centuries, and it names the end of an attachment in the vocabulary of physical injury. That is not an English peculiarity. Across a great many unrelated languages, the words people reach for after an ending are the words for wounds, weight, breakage and cold. When a description that specific turns up independently in that many places, it is usually reporting something real.

**What the term genuinely captures.** The location. People do not say they became sad after an ending. They point at the chest, describe pressure or a hollow, mention the throat and the stomach, and use the language of something torn. The word is accurate about where the experience is felt, and it is accurate that the feeling arrives before any thinking about it does. That is a real observation, and the popular word for it is the right one.

**What the research establishes.** In 2003 a study scanned people while they were quietly excluded from a simple ball-tossing computer game, and found the distress engaging brain regions that are also involved in physical pain. It is one of the most cited findings in this whole area. That study is named and discussed on [the page about disappearing on people](/patterns/ghosting/), which is why I am describing it here without repeating the attribution. A large literature followed it, covering what rejection does to attention, to sleep, and to how people describe themselves.

**What the research does not establish, and this is the part that matters.** The popular version of that finding is that heartbreak literally is physical pain, that the brain cannot tell the difference, and therefore that anything which works on physical pain should work here. That is a much stronger claim than an imaging study can support, and it has been directly challenged. Woo and colleagues reported in 2014 that if you stop asking which regions light up and start asking whether the fine-grained activity pattern that predicts physical pain intensity also predicts rejection distress, the answer is no, and the reverse is also no. The two are separable at the level where the measurement is actually specific. Broad anatomical overlap is genuine and interesting. It falls a long way short of sameness, partly because those regions are involved in a great many things, and a region lighting up tells you much less about what somebody is experiencing than the popular retelling assumes.

**And the term names the what, never the why.** Heartbreak is a description of an experience. It carries no information at all about which of several very different situations produced yours, and those situations need different things. That is the whole reason the second half of this page exists.

| What the word genuinely gives you | Where it stops |
|---|---|
| An accurate description of where this is felt and how fast it arrives | No definition, no scale, no threshold, no clinical meaning |
| A real body of research on what rejection does to attention, sleep and self-description | No support for the claim that it is the same thing as physical pain |
| Permission to treat the physical symptoms as ordinary rather than alarming | No guidance on which physical symptoms need a doctor, which is a separate judgment |
| A shared word other people immediately understand | No information whatsoever about what produced yours |

## When Does This Belong With a Doctor Rather Than a Page Like This?

There is a line here and it is worth stating without any softening around it.

If you are not eating or not sleeping, or if you are having thoughts of harming yourself, that belongs with a doctor or a crisis line now rather than with a page.

That sentence is deliberately short and has no conditions attached to it. Not eating means not eating, as against eating badly. Not sleeping means nights without sleep, as against broken sleep. And any thought of harming yourself belongs in the same sentence regardless of how seriously you think you meant it, because your own read on how serious it was is exactly the judgment this stretch of your life is worst equipped to make.

Two further things get looked at in person rather than read about. Any physical symptom that frightens you, particularly anything involving the chest. And a flatness that has spread out of this subject into everything else and has stayed for months without moving in any direction.

Everything below this line assumes none of that applies.

## Is Anything Actually Wrong With You for Feeling It in Your Body?

No, and the fact that you are asking is itself common enough to be part of the pattern.

The physical part of this is the least mysterious thing about it. An attachment that has been running for months or years gets stored as a very large set of habits rather than as an opinion, most of them unconscious and many of them physical. Which side of the bed. What time the door goes. Whose voice comes through the phone at six. When you eat and with whom. What you do with your hands in a room full of other people. Where your attention goes at the end of a sentence in company.

All of those habits have an object, the object has gone, and none of them received the news. They keep firing into an empty slot, several hundred times a day, and each firing produces a small physical event. That is a large part of what the weight in the chest actually is, and it explains why the sensation arrives before any thought does. There was never a thought in the loop to begin with.

Add to that what any sustained threat does to a body. Appetite is one of the first systems to go offline under threat and one of the first to come back. Sleep changes shape. Attention gets narrow and sticky. Digestion complains. None of this requires anything unusual about you, and none of it is evidence about the size of your feeling or the depth of your character.

The one thing worth saying plainly to somebody in the first weeks is that the intensity of the physical response is not a scoreboard. People sometimes read their own symptoms as a report on how much the relationship meant, and then feel exposed if the symptoms are large, or fraudulent if they are small. The correlation is much weaker than it feels. Physical response tracks disruption, surprise and sleep at least as much as it tracks love.

## What Shape Does This Usually Take, and Over What Course?

Here is what people most commonly describe, offered as a shape rather than a schedule.

**The first stretch is loud and strangely functional.** There is adrenaline in it. There are logistics. People are checking on you. Some of the most competent behavior of a person's year happens in the ten days after an ending, which surprises everybody including them. The physical symptoms are usually at their worst here and the sense of catastrophe is at its highest.

**Then the practical tasks run out.** This is the part nobody warns about. Somewhere around the ninth week, give or take a very large margin, the messages have stopped arriving, the arrangements are settled, friends have gone back to their own lives, and the ordinary hours return with the person missing from them. A dip here is one of the most reliably reported features of the whole thing, and it gets misread as going backward constantly. It is closer to the opposite. The dip usually arrives because the scaffolding came down.

**Then it becomes intermittent rather than continuous.** The general level drops, and what remains is cue-driven. A street. A song in a store. Somebody with the same walk. The state arrives whole and unannounced, and the reliable change over time is not that the cues stop working but that the state gets shorter. People describe going from an afternoon, to twenty-three minutes, to something that passes before they have finished crossing the road.

**And then it stops being the organizing fact of the week.** Not by any particular date. What people report is noticing, at some point, that a whole Tuesday went by without it coming up, and that the noticing is itself slightly disorienting.

That is the common shape. Almost none of the variation between people is in the shape. Nearly all of it is in the timing, and the timing is genuinely unpredictable in advance, which is the honest reason no number appears in the paragraphs above.

## Why Are You So Much Worse at Predicting This Than You Think?

This is the most useful and most checkable fact on the page.

People systematically overestimate how badly and for how long a bad event will affect them. That general effect has been established across many domains and is discussed elsewhere on this site in the context of predicting how much you will enjoy an evening you want to cancel. What is more directly relevant here is that somebody went and measured it on this exact event.

Krishnamurti and colleagues published a study in 2008 that did something unusually clean. They took undergraduates who were currently in relationships and asked them to forecast how distressed they would be at various points if the relationship ended. Then they waited. The ones whose relationships actually ended were followed with repeated distress measures over the following months, so the forecasts could be compared against the same people's later reported experience.

The forecasts overshot. Real distress was genuinely present and it declined faster than the same people had predicted it would, with the gap between prediction and reality widest at the later time points. They were roughly right about how bad the first stretch would be and substantially wrong about how long it would stay that bad.

I should say plainly what I have done with the attribution, because it is unusual. The study has four authors, and three of them are cited on other pages of this site for entirely unrelated work. Naming a co-author rather than the first author is a deliberate choice to stop a set of pages from looking like it rests on four names, and anybody who wants the paper will find it from the description.

**And the honest limits.** The sample was undergraduates at one university, and their relationships were short by adult standards. Nothing about that design tells you what happens after a nineteen-year marriage. The direction of the finding is robust and has held up across a lot of related work. The popular version, which turns it into a promise that you will be fine sooner than you think, is doing something the study cannot support. A prediction being too pessimistic on average says nothing about where any individual sits.

What it is legitimately good for is this. The sentence that runs at quarter past four in the morning, the one that says this is permanent, is a prediction, and it is being generated by the machinery known to be worst at exactly this. That does not make the feeling wrong. It makes the forecast unreliable, and the forecast is the part doing most of the damage.

## Where Did the Recovery Timelines Actually Come From?

You have almost certainly met a number by now. It is worth knowing what sits behind them.

**The half-the-relationship rule.** The claim that recovery takes half the length of the relationship, or a month for every year, or some other tidy fraction. I have looked for a source and have not found one. It circulates as folk arithmetic, appears in articles that cite other articles, and terminates nowhere. It has the shape of a finding without being one.

**The specific week counts.** The various figures quoted with apparent precision trace back to two kinds of thing. Some come from small studies of undergraduates, with the same limits described above, where a median from a modest convenience sample got reported as though it were a general fact about human beings. Others come from surveys commissioned by consumer brands, which are produced to generate coverage rather than knowledge, and which are designed backward from the headline somebody wanted.

**Why this matters more than it looks.** A timeline lands on somebody in week fourteen as a deadline rather than as neutral information, and deadlines can be failed. A reader still struggling past the quoted number now has two problems, the original one and the evidence that they are handling it worse than average. That second problem is manufactured, and it was manufactured by numbers that were never measurements.

The honest position is that the direction is known and the duration is not. Distress is usually highest early and usually declines. Where your own course sits inside that range cannot be known in advance by you, by me, or by anybody quoting a figure.

## What Is the Popular Advice Here That Is Genuinely Dangerous?

One thing on this topic is worth naming as unsafe rather than merely unhelpful.

Because of the imaging work described above, a piece of advice has spread widely which suggests that since emotional pain runs on machinery shared with physical pain, you might reasonably reach for the ordinary over-the-counter painkiller in your kitchen cupboard when an ending is at its worst. It appears in articles, in videos, and in the sort of list that circulates with no source attached. There is a real experiment behind it, in which participants took a daily dose over a few weeks and reported slightly lower hurt feelings on a questionnaire than a comparison group did.

I am not going to name the compound, and that is deliberate, because naming it is exactly how this advice travels.

Here is why it should not be followed. The reported effect was small and measured on self-report scales, which is a very long way from establishing that somebody in the middle of an ending should be dosing themselves. The compound most commonly named in this advice has an unusually narrow margin between an ordinary dose and a harmful one, and it is already among the most common causes of serious accidental harm in its class. The population most likely to act on the advice is a population sleeping badly, eating erratically, sometimes drinking more than usual, and not tracking what it has taken. There is also work suggesting these compounds blunt positive responses along with negative ones, which means the best available case is a flattening rather than a relief.

If any part of this is bad enough that medicating it seems reasonable, that is a conversation with a doctor rather than a decision made at quarter past four in the morning on the strength of something you read.

## Why Does Practical Life Feel Unmoored Out of Proportion to Missing Them?

Because the person was also a structure, and the structure went at the same moment.

This is the most under-described part of an ending, and it accounts for a large share of the disorientation people cannot explain to themselves. You can be entirely clear that the relationship needed to end, feel relatively little about the person on a given Tuesday, and still find that you cannot decide what to do with a Saturday, cannot work out the finances, cannot remember how you used to make plans, and stand in a store holding two things with no way of choosing between them.

What has actually gone is a daily architecture. A schedule that was set by somebody else's schedule. A division of small competencies, some of which you never learned because you never had to. A route into other people, because a substantial part of most adults' social life runs through a partner. A default answer to the question of what happens this weekend, at the holidays, in five years. And a picture of the future, which is the item people underestimate most, because it was doing constant quiet work and was never once examined.

There is also a self-description in there. Slotter and colleagues reported in 2010 on what happens to how people describe themselves after a breakup, finding that those reporting the largest change in their own self-concept also reported the most emotional distress, along with a marked confusion about who they now were. That work is correlational, it rests on self-report, and its samples were students, so it establishes an association rather than a cause. It matches what people describe closely enough to be worth knowing.

The practical consequence is worth stating. If most of what you are experiencing is architectural, then the things that help are architectural too, and they have very little to do with feelings about the person. Somebody has to decide when you eat now, and it has to be you. Somebody has to fill a Saturday. This is unglamorous, it feels beside the point, and it moves things more than most of what gets recommended.

## Which Part of This Is Involuntary, and Which Part Did You Choose?

This is the most useful cut available and almost nobody makes it, which is why the shame in this area is so badly distributed.

**The involuntary part** is nearly everything described so far. The physical drop at a notification. Waking at the same hour. Appetite. The state that arrives whole when a song starts. The pull toward the person. None of this is being chosen, none of it responds to being argued with, and it will run largely on its own course whatever you decide about it. Feeling ashamed of this part is like being ashamed of a bruise.

**The chosen part** is much smaller and much more specific. It is what you do in the twenty minutes after waking at four. Whether you open the exchange again. Whether you check. Whether you ask a mutual friend something you have carefully made sound casual. Whether you go past a particular building on a route that is eleven minutes longer. Whether you construct, for the ninth time, the conversation in which they finally understand.

Almost every piece of advice you will be given aims at the first category, which is why almost all of it fails. You cannot decide to stop missing somebody. You can decide, with difficulty and imperfectly, what to do at quarter past four, and that is the entire available surface.

It is worth being accurate about why the chosen part persists, because willpower is the wrong frame for it. Each of those acts pays out. A check produces about twenty seconds of relief from not knowing. A reconstructed conversation produces a version of the ending in which you are understood. A drive past a building produces the sensation of contact. These are small, reliable, immediate returns, and the cost lands ten minutes later, which is the exact structure of every behavior that is hard to stop. Nothing about you is weak. The schedule of payoffs is simply arranged badly.

Which is the honest reason this page hands you no rule about contact, no day count, and no set of phases. Those exist in quantity, they sell well, and there is nothing underneath them. What is worth having instead is an accurate account of what your own four in the morning is buying.

## Why Do Two People in the Same Aftermath Need Completely Different Things?

Because the same aftermath comes from different places, and the place decides what is actually going on.

Two people can describe an identical week. Both stopped eating. Both wake at four. Both did something at quarter past four that they would not repeat in daylight. And underneath, one of them is running an ordinary ending on its ordinary course, while the other is carrying a loss from fifteen years ago that this ending has reopened. Identical on the surface. Nothing else in common.

Five roots come up repeatedly. They are not types of person and nobody sits cleanly in one. The purpose of separating them is that they need different things, and the most common expensive mistake in this whole territory is treating a structural problem as a feeling, or a fifteen-year-old loss as a recent one.

The first is by far the most common, and it gets the most space here, because most readers need the shape of the thing described rather than anything found out about them.

## What If This Is an Ordinary Ending Running Its Ordinary Course?

Then nothing is wrong, and being told so precisely is the most useful thing available.

This is the majority position and it is chronically under-described, because a description of the ordinary does not attract attention the way an explanation does. So here it is at length.

Something real ended. It had been running long enough to be built into your week, your body and your plans. The ending removed it at a stroke, and every habit built around it is now firing into empty space. Your appetite went. Your sleep broke. Your attention narrowed onto one subject with a persistence you have never experienced about anything else. Your practical competence dropped. You did some things at four in the morning. Your body reacted to a message from the dry cleaner as though it were a threat.

Every item on that list is what happens. Not what happens to people who are fragile, or who were too invested, or who have unresolved things. What happens.

And it has a course. The loud stretch. The dip when the scaffolding comes down. The long intermittent tail where cues still work but the states get shorter. The morning where it turns out yesterday went by without it. You are not steering any of that, and the parts of you currently trying to steer it are producing more suffering than the ending is.

Two markers fit this root. The first is that the distress attaches to the ending rather than to you. When you look at it honestly, the sentence underneath is about them being gone rather than about what their leaving proved about your value. The second is that it moves. Not steadily and not fast, but a month is measurably different from the previous one in some direction.

What people in this root usually need is permission and a schedule. Permission to stop assessing their own reaction, and a set of concrete external structures for eating, moving and being around other people, none of which treat the loss and all of which make the hours survivable while the involuntary part does what it does.

And the thing worth saying to somebody here, which almost nobody says, is that the size of this is not a problem to be solved. A large response to a real loss is a working system doing its job. The problem, where there is one, is usually the second layer, which is the running commentary about whether the response is appropriate. Take that layer away and what is left is grief, which is unpleasant and finite and does not need your management.

## What If What Ended Was a Structure Rather Than a Person?

Then the feeling about them will keep coming out smaller than the disruption, and that mismatch will confuse you until somebody names it.

This is the reader who can say, with reasonable honesty, that they are not sure how much they miss the actual person, and who is nonetheless barely functional. The disorientation is real and its object is a daily architecture rather than an individual.

The markers are distinctive. The hardest moments are logistical rather than emotional. Sunday afternoons, holidays, the question of who to tell about something that happened at work. You feel the absence most sharply in the moments that used to be organized by somebody else, and least sharply when you think about the person directly. Photographs do relatively little. An empty calendar does a lot.

There is often a financial or housing component sitting underneath, and it deserves to be named as its own load rather than folded into grief. A person managing an income drop, a move, and a broken attachment is carrying three things, and treating all three as heartbreak makes the whole situation unreadable.

What this root needs is architectural rather than emotional. Every slot that was filled by somebody else's schedule now needs filling deliberately, and the filling feels artificial for a while and works anyway. It is also worth being explicit that the picture of the future was part of the structure, and that grieving a plan is a legitimate thing to be doing even when the person inside the plan is somebody you were ready to leave. [The page about feeling worse after stopping something that was hurting you](/patterns/why-do-i-feel-worse-after-quitting-the-thing-that-was-hurting-me/) covers the version of this where the ending was your decision and the relief did not arrive on schedule.

## What If This Ending Is Carrying an Older One?

Then the size will keep outrunning the event, and every attempt to make it proportionate will fail, because proportion was never what it was about.

The signature is a mismatch you can measure. A relationship of eight months producing a response that a much longer one did not. A response that arrived at a specific moment, often the moment somebody physically left a building, and that felt much older than the situation. A quality in the distress closer to abandonment than to sadness, arriving with a certainty about being left that was fully formed rather than reasoned into.

Another marker is that the content drifts. People in this root notice that the four in the morning material is often about a parent, an earlier ending, a childhood house, or a period they had considered settled, rather than about the ex-partner at all. The current ending opened a door and something else came through it.

This is genuinely different work and it is not done by understanding the current relationship better. What tends to help first is separating the two, so that the current ending is allowed to be its own size and the older thing is allowed to be its own subject. [The page on grief that never got grieved](/patterns/unprocessed-grief/) goes into the older half properly, and a reader who recognized themselves in the paragraphs above will get more from it than from the rest of this page.

## What If You Cannot Locate Yourself Outside the Pairing?

Then the distressing part is the absence of a description rather than the absence of the person, and those need different things.

The reader here says something like: I do not know what I like, I do not know what I do on a Saturday, I do not know who I am when there is nobody to be it with. The self-concept work described earlier maps onto this closely. What changed is not only the company but the account you were able to give of yourself, and a good chunk of that account was only true inside the pairing.

The markers: distress that does not track how much you think about the person, difficulty answering ordinary questions about preference, a strong pull toward filling the position rather than toward the individual who left, and a flatness in solitude closer to blankness than to sadness.

This one is covered properly on [the page about who you are without the thing that defined you](/patterns/who-am-i-without-the-thing-that-defined-me/), which is written for exactly this reader, and I would rather send you there than compress it into four paragraphs. The one thing worth adding from this page's angle is that the rebuilding runs through action rather than reflection. You do not work out what you like and then go and do it. You do things, badly and without enthusiasm, and the description assembles afterward.

## What If You Feel No Grief at All, Only Relief?

Then you are in the group most likely to think something is seriously wrong with you, and there almost certainly is not.

This needs saying clearly, because the entire genre of writing on endings assumes devastation, and a reader who is mostly relieved reads all of it as evidence of coldness. The number of people who end a relationship and feel primarily lighter is large, and it is not discussed proportionally.

Relief measures what the relationship was costing to maintain. That is all it measures. It is a statement about ongoing expenditure rather than about whether you loved somebody, and the two coexist perfectly comfortably. People frequently report the sequence in this order: relief for weeks, then a delayed and much smaller sadness that turns out to be about what the relationship never became rather than about what it was.

The other common version is the long slow decline, where a relationship faded over years and most of the grieving happened while it was still technically running. By the time it formally ended there was very little left to feel, and what arrives afterward is flatness rather than pain. That is a completed process rather than a suppressed one.

Two things are worth watching in this root. If the relief is followed immediately by guilt about the relief, that deserves its own attention, and the mechanism sits closer to [self-forgiveness](/patterns/how-to-forgive-yourself/) than to grief. And if the flatness spreads out of this subject into everything else and stays there for months, that is the marker described in the boundary section above rather than an emotional style.

Nothing about feeling relief requires correction. The people who most need to hear that are the ones currently building a case against their own character out of it.

## How Do You Tell Which of the Five Is Yours?

Four questions, and the answers separate them more cleanly than the descriptions do.

**Where does the sentence point?** At the person being gone, or at what their leaving proved about you? The first belongs to an ordinary course. The second points at identity or at an older loss.

**When is it worst?** In moments that used to be filled by them, or in moments where you think about them? A logistical worst points at structure. Direct thinking points at the person.

**Does the size fit the event?** A response much larger than the length and depth of the relationship would predict is the clearest single marker that something older is being carried.

**What arrives when you are alone and unoccupied?** Sadness about them, blankness about yourself, panic about arrangements, or relief. Those four answers point at four different roots, and the answer is usually available within a few seconds if you are honest about it.

| If the loudest thing is | The likely root | What actually moves it |
|---|---|---|
| Missing them, and it moves month to month | An ordinary ending on its course | External structure for eating, moving and company while it runs |
| Sunday afternoons and unfillable slots | A structure ended | Deliberate refilling of the architecture, treated as its own task |
| A certainty about being left that arrived whole | An older loss reopened | Separating the two so each is allowed its own size |
| Not knowing what you like or what you do | Identity built inside the pairing | Action first, description afterward |
| Relief, followed by suspicion of yourself | An ending that was genuinely a relief | Nothing, other than being told how common it is |

## Why Do Time Heals, No Contact and Getting Back Out There Not Appear on This Page?

Because each of them is either untestable, oversold, or a verdict wearing the clothes of advice.

**Time heals** describes an outcome and hands you nothing to do with the next hour, which is the only unit that exists to somebody in week two. It is also only half accurate. The involuntary part does fade largely on its own. The chosen part, the checking and the reconstructing, keeps the whole thing refreshed for as long as it runs, which is why two people can wait the same number of months and arrive somewhere completely different. Waiting is not the active ingredient, and telling somebody that it is leaves them with nothing but endurance.

**The contact rules** are the biggest industry in this territory and there is very little under the specifics. Day counts, phase structures and staged protocols get presented with a confidence no evidence supports. The one mechanical thing that is true is unremarkable: fewer reopenings per day means fewer reopenings per day. Whether reducing contact is even available to you depends on children, housing, work and shared obligations, none of which appear in the protocols. So this page describes what the checking is buying and leaves the arrangements to you, because you are the only person who knows them.

**Getting back out there** and its opposite, the warning about rebounds, are both verdicts issued in advance. People form new attachments at every possible distance from an ending and the outcomes vary enormously. The useful question is whether what you want is company or evidence, rather than how long it has been, and that one you can answer honestly in about four seconds.

**Self-care** as usually packaged belongs in the same list. What actually helps in the first month is unglamorous and physical, it does not treat the loss, and it is closer to keeping a house warm than to healing anything.

## What Does This Actually Cost, If Anything?

The ending costs what it costs. What is worth counting is the second layer, because that part is optional and most people do not know they are paying for it.

There is the assessment of your own reaction. A significant share of the suffering people report in the weeks after an ending is not the grief but the running commentary about whether the grief is appropriate, proportionate, embarrassing or diagnostic. That commentary runs all day and produces nothing.

There is the manufactured deadline. Somebody in week seventeen who has read that the average is shorter now has a second problem, and it was assembled entirely out of numbers that were never measurements.

There is the time inside the chosen part. Nobody adds it up, and when they do the figure is startling. Twenty minutes at four in the morning, a few checks across the day, an hour of reconstruction on the drive, and you are at a substantial fraction of a working week, every week, spent on a conversation that has already concluded.

And there is the decision risk, which is the one worth taking seriously. People make large and irreversible decisions in this stretch, about jobs, cities, money and other relationships, using judgment they would not trust for a much smaller decision in a normal month. The forecasting problem described earlier is at its most active here. A prediction generated at four in the morning about how permanent this is has an unusually poor track record, and it is exactly the prediction people act on.

## Where Does This Stop and Something Else Begin?

The markers are practical, and the useful ones are behavioral rather than emotional.

Whether you are eating and sleeping at all, as against badly. Whether the flatness has stayed attached to this subject or spread into everything. Whether there has been any movement in any direction over months. Whether the thinking has become entirely about your own worthlessness rather than about the loss. Whether any thought of harming yourself has appeared at all.

Any of those means the boundary section near the top of this page applies, and the sentence in it is the whole of my advice on it. A page can describe a shape. It cannot assess you, and it should not try.

The other version of this question is duration with none of the markers above. Somebody functional, eating, working, and still substantially organized around an ending three years later sits outside the territory this section is about. Duration like that usually means one of the roots has gone unaddressed rather than anything escalating, most often the older loss or the identity one.

## What Does an Ordinary Week Look Like on the Other Side?

Less dramatic than people imagine, which is the part worth knowing in advance.

The feeling does not disappear. People expect a state of not caring and that is not what arrives. What arrives is that the person becomes somebody you knew well and no longer live around. You can think about them deliberately and feel something, and the something has an ordinary size and a short duration.

The cues keep working. A song still does it. What changes is duration. What used to take an afternoon takes twenty-three minutes, then takes as long as it takes to cross a road, and eventually is over before you have decided how to feel about it.

The mornings come back first for most people, and it is a surprisingly precise marker. There is a stretch where the first conscious thought each day is the ending, and then a morning where the first thought is something ordinary and irrelevant, and you notice the substitution afterward with something close to suspicion.

The practical architecture gets rebuilt and is mostly invisible. Saturdays have something in them. You have learned the competencies that were theirs, badly at first. Trash night is solved. Nobody congratulates anybody for this and it accounts for a large share of how much better the weeks feel.

And the four in the morning behavior stops, usually without a decision being made about it. What people describe is not a successful act of restraint but a gradual discovery that the check has stopped paying out. There is nothing new to find, the twenty seconds of relief no longer arrives, and the habit dies of malnutrition rather than of willpower.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

It depends on the root, and I will not give you a number for the first one, for the reasons set out above.

If this is an ordinary ending on its ordinary course, the honest answer is that the direction is reliable and the duration is not. What is available immediately, on the day you read this, is the removal of the second layer. A great many people find that the commentary about whether their reaction is acceptable was carrying half the weight, and that half can go this week.

If a structure ended, this moves at the speed of the rebuilding rather than the speed of the feeling, which makes it unusually responsive to deliberate action. People who fill the architecture on purpose report a different month within about six weeks. People who wait for the feeling to change first tend to wait a long time.

If an older loss has been reopened, the current ending will often settle on its own schedule while the older thing stays exactly where it was. That part does not resolve through this ending and it is not measured in weeks.

If the difficulty is identity, that is slow and it moves through action rather than insight. Months rather than weeks, and the movement is usually invisible until somebody asks you a question about yourself and you notice that you had an answer.

If what you have is relief, there is frequently nothing to wait for at all, and the only thing that needed to happen was somebody telling you that this is common.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

The research behind this page, and the parts of it I do not trust.

The exclusion imaging work from 2003 is the most cited finding in this area. It is named and discussed on [the ghosting page](/patterns/ghosting/), so I have described it here without repeating the attribution, which also stops a set of pages from looking like it rests on fewer sources than it does. The direct challenge to the strong reading is Woo and colleagues, published in 2014 in Nature Communications, who used multivariate pattern analysis to show that the fine-grained pattern predicting physical pain intensity did not predict rejection distress, with the reverse also holding. My reading of the overall picture is that broad overlap is well supported, sameness is not, and the popular retelling has been running ahead of the evidence for two decades.

Krishnamurti and colleagues published the breakup forecasting study in 2008 in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology. As noted in that section, I have named a co-author rather than the first author because three of the four are cited elsewhere on this site for unrelated work, and I would rather say that openly than quietly stack a fourth finding onto the same names. The sample was undergraduates at one university and the relationships were short, so the direction transfers better than the magnitude.

Slotter and colleagues published the self-concept work in 2010 in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin. It is correlational, self-report, and student-sampled. It establishes an association between self-concept change and distress rather than a mechanism, and I have used it that way.

The recovery timelines are the weakest material in the whole territory and I have said so above rather than repeating any of them. I could not trace the half-the-relationship rule to any study at all.

The advice about medicating emotional pain is described without attribution deliberately. The underlying experiment exists, its effects were small and self-reported, and naming the researchers alongside a practice I am telling you not to adopt would be both unkind to them and the most efficient possible way of spreading the practice.

Two of the papers above sit close enough in method that they should not be read as independent confirmations of one picture. None of the four addresses the roots section, which is drawn from working with people rather than from published research and should be weighted accordingly.

I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, I am not a therapist or a grief specialist, and none of the researchers named here has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own reading disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.

## What Is Worth Doing This Week, Before You Decide Anything?

Two things, and neither of them is aimed at the feeling.

**The first is a schedule rather than a mood.** Three items only. A time you eat, whether or not you want to. A daily physical thing that gets you outside, however short. One arrangement a week that puts you in a room with other people while you are poor company. Write the three down with actual times attached. None of this treats the loss and none of it is supposed to. It changes how survivable the hours are while the involuntary part runs, and it is the only part of the first month that reliably repays effort.

**The second is a count, and it is the one that produces information.** For seven days, keep a note on your phone. Every time you check, reread, drive past, ask a mutual friend something, or construct the conversation in your head, add a line with the time and one word for what happened just before it. Change nothing else. Do not try to reduce the number. The count is the point.

At the end of the week two things will be visible that were not visible before. The first is the total, which is almost always larger than people expect and is the most persuasive argument anybody will ever make to you about this. The second is the pattern in the word before it, which is where the actual finding lives. Most people discover that their four in the morning is triggered by something specific and repeatable, and that the trigger has very little to do with the ex-partner. Being alone at a particular hour. A particular kind of day at work. The gap after finishing something. One person's name coming up.

That is a piece of information about your own week that no article can hand you, and it is what makes the chosen part of this addressable at all.

Do not make any large decision this week. Not about a job, a city, money, or another person. The forecasting problem is at its most active in exactly this stretch, and the sentence that runs at four in the morning about how permanent this is comes from the machinery known to be least reliable about precisely that question.

[Our Sacred Valley seminar](https://florianrolke.com/the-soulsearching-experience/) works at the layer underneath the count. We do not start with what you did at quarter past four, because you can already describe it and will have just written seven days of it down. We start with what it has been buying, since a behavior that repeats that reliably at that cost is never doing nothing, and until you can see the payout, every resolution to stop will fail in the same place at the same hour. Then we look at which of the five roots is actually yours, because a structural problem treated as grief and an older loss treated as a recent one both waste years, and we build a plan you are held accountable to afterward, back in your own week with the same quiet Sunday afternoons in it. The goal is not to feed your emotions. Everybody is screened before arriving, which keeps the group small enough that somebody in the middle of an ending gets worked with properly rather than handed a worksheet. One limitation worth stating plainly: exact dates, the price, and the final cap on group size are still being settled, so anybody comparing programs on those numbers this week cannot yet compare ours. Ask every program you are weighing the same questions, this one included, and decide from what comes back.

The count costs nothing, takes seven days, and is worth having whichever way it comes out.
