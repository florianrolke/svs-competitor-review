faqs:
- q: "Why can't I press publish on my own work?"
  a: "Because finishing and releasing are two separate acts, and only one of them has ever been assigned to you. Making has a shape: a spec, a receiver, a point at which it is done. Releasing has none of those. Nobody is waiting for it, nothing breaks if it never happens, and you are the only person who can authorize it. An act with no assignment and no deadline does not get done late. It does not enter the queue at all."
- q: "Why can I speak to a room of strangers and still not post a video?"
  a: "Because the room releases itself and the video does not. When you walk to the front of a room, somebody booked the date, the people are already sitting down, and the thing happens whether or not you authorize it that morning. A recording asks you to be the one who decides, on a day of your choosing, with nothing forcing it. Confidence in front of people was never the missing part."
- q: "Is this stage fright?"
  a: "Usually the opposite of it. A large share of people who cannot post are noticeably good in live settings and would rather do the live version every time. What they are stalling on has no audience present at all: a file, a text field, and a button. If you would happily do the same material in front of two hundred people this afternoon, the audience is not what you are avoiding."
- q: "Why does the finished thing sit on my phone for days?"
  a: "Because the work is over and the decision has not started. A finished file feels like a completed task, so nothing in you flags it as outstanding, while the one remaining act carries all of the exposure and none of the momentum. People describe checking it repeatedly through the day, at eleven at night, and then going to bed. Nothing is being worked on. A decision is being deferred."
- q: "What is actually different about a published version compared to a live one?"
  a: "Four things, and every one of them makes the release harder than the performance. A live moment is scheduled by somebody else, it is over when it is over, it is watched by people whose reactions you can read, and it leaves no artifact. A published version is scheduled by you, permanent, watched by people you cannot see on a timetable you do not control, and it exists afterward as an object with your name attached."
- q: "Why is there no deadline on the thing I make for myself?"
  a: "Because you are the client, and clients set deadlines for other people. Every other piece of work in your week arrived with a date and a receiver, which is why it got done despite being harder and less interesting. Your own release has neither, and that missing date removes the only mechanism that has ever reliably moved you."
- q: "What is context collapse?"
  a: "The name for what happens when audiences that stay separate in life arrive in one place. Marwick and boyd described it in 2011: a person posting to a network is addressing former colleagues, family, clients and school friends simultaneously, with no way to tune the message for any one of them. The tone that works for one group reads badly to another, and no available wording satisfies all of them at once."
- q: "Who am I actually writing to when I post something?"
  a: "An audience you construct, because the real one is invisible at the moment you write. Researchers call this the imagined audience, and the version people build tends to be an unfriendly composite: the sharpest person you know, the relative who would find it embarrassing, and a stranger looking for a reason to dismiss it. You are not writing for readers. You are writing past a committee you assembled."
- q: "How many people actually see what I post?"
  a: "Far more than nothing and far fewer than the number you are braced for, and people get the estimate wrong in a direction that surprises them. Bernstein and colleagues compared what users guessed about their audience against server logs and found the guesses ran roughly four times too low. The audience you dread is imaginary in its composition rather than its size, which is the uncomfortable half of the finding."
- q: "Why does my voice sound wrong on the recording?"
  a: "Partly because you normally hear it through bone as well as air, so the recorded version is missing frequencies you have heard your whole life. That explains only some of it. Holzman and Rousey argued in 1966 that the larger part is content rather than pitch: a recording carries information about your state that you cannot edit and had no idea was audible, and hearing it is what produces the recoil."
- q: "Why do I look at what I recorded and find it bland?"
  a: "Because you have switched seats. While recording you were inside the intention, which is vivid, specific and full of everything you meant. Watching it back, you are an audience member with none of that, comparing a first take against material made by people with editors. The gap you are reacting to sits between your intention and the artifact rather than between you and other people."
- q: "Is this perfectionism?"
  a: "Sometimes, and assuming so sends a lot of people at the wrong work. A rising standard keeps a thing unfinished, revised and never quite ready. This pattern shows up differently: the thing is done, you think it is fine, and it still does not go out. If your file is finished and you are not editing it, the standard is not what is holding the release."
- q: "Why do I keep making more instead of releasing what I have?"
  a: "Because making is the part you are good at, and it produces the honest feeling of a day well spent. Research, preparation, a better version, a new outline: all of it is real work, all of it delays the one act that carries exposure, and none of it registers as avoidance. This is the hardest version to catch, because from the inside it looks exactly like diligence."
- q: "Why does just post it not work?"
  a: "Because it is an instruction to perform the act, aimed at somebody whose difficulty is authorizing it. You already know how to press the button. What is missing is a reason that outweighs the exposure at the moment of pressing, and a sentence from somebody else does not supply one. Advice that skips the authorization step gets followed twice and then stops."
- q: "Is it true that quantity beats quality?"
  a: "The famous version of that claim is a story rather than a study. It comes from a 1993 book about making art, where a ceramics teacher supposedly graded half a class on volume and half on a single perfect pot, and the volume group made the better work. There are no measurements, no controls and no data behind it. Repetition genuinely helps. That particular anecdote establishes nothing."
- q: "Why does it feel like posting is bragging?"
  a: "Usually because a rule about self-display got installed somewhere before any of this came up, and publishing your own work is the exact act the rule was written to prevent. People in this version can promote somebody else's work cheerfully and freeze on their own. The tell is that the discomfort tracks whose name is attached rather than how good the thing is."
- q: "Why can I send work to one person and not to everybody?"
  a: "Because a named receiver restores everything the public version removes. One person is a known audience, on a known timescale, whose reaction you will actually see, and the thing you send them reads as a draft rather than as a claim. That is why sending to one named person works as a first step and why a general plan to share more does not."
- q: "Does it help to schedule the post in advance?"
  a: "More than almost anything else, because it moves the authorization away from the moment of exposure. Deciding on Monday that a thing goes out Thursday puts the decision in the hands of a version of you who is not currently looking at the file. The people who unstick this usually do it by separating the release decision from the release, rather than by becoming braver at the moment of pressing."
- q: "What is the smallest version of releasing something?"
  a: "One finished thing, to one named person, with a date on it, and no request for reassurance. Not a plan to post weekly. Not a public account. Send the piece you already have to somebody specific by Thursday and say what you want back. That is a complete release with the audience shrunk to one, and it exercises the exact muscle the public version needs."
- q: "Why is a podcast invitation harder to accept than the podcast itself?"
  a: "Because saying yes is the release and the conversation is only the work. People who talk easily for an hour routinely talk themselves out of the invitation before answering, on grounds about eloquence or expertise that would not survive the first ten minutes of the actual recording. The stall lands on the authorization, which is the same place it always lands."
- q: "Why do I feel fine about the work and terrible about the post?"
  a: "Because they are claims of different sizes. The work is a thing you made. The post is a statement that you are the sort of person who makes this and wants it looked at, which is a claim about standing rather than about quality. People who are entirely happy with a document can be unable to attach their name to it publicly, and nothing about the document is what changed."
- q: "Does anybody actually remember what I posted last year?"
  a: "Almost nobody, and you can test this without asking anyone. Try to recall three specific things a person you follow closely posted twelve months ago. Most people cannot manage one. That is the retention budget your own audience has, and it is the same size as yours. The permanence that worries you is real for a search engine and thin for a human being."
- q: "Why do I delete it after posting?"
  a: "Because the exposure arrives after the act rather than before it, and deleting ends it instantly. The relief is genuine and it teaches the wrong lesson, since it registers as evidence that pressing publish leads to something you had to escape. If you are deleting, treat the first hour afterward as the part to plan for, rather than the press itself."
- q: "Is not posting ever the right decision?"
  a: "Frequently, and a page that says otherwise is selling something. Plenty of people have thought it through and decided a public record of their opinions serves them badly, which is a coherent adult position. The question worth asking is whether you decided it or whether it happened to you. A decision you can state in one sentence is a policy. A file sitting untouched for seventeen days is not."
- q: "Why does this get worse the better the work is?"
  a: "Because a better piece carries a larger claim, and the claim is the part that stalls you. Something rough can be released as a first attempt, which asks little of you. Something you are proud of asks you to stand behind it, and standing behind it is the act you cannot authorize. People routinely release their weakest work and sit on their strongest, which looks backwards until you notice what is being decided."
- q: "What if I do not think of myself as an expert in this?"
  a: "Then the honest move is to say so on the thing rather than to withhold it. A great deal of stalled work is stalled because publishing feels like claiming an authority you have not been granted. You can release something as one person's working view, stated plainly, and the claim shrinks to the size you can actually defend. Withholding it makes no such claim and also helps nobody."
- q: "Why does having a name on it change everything?"
  a: "Because attribution converts an object into a position. An unsigned piece of work can be judged on its own; a signed one is read as a statement about the person who signed it, and it stays retrievable long after the day it was made. That is why the same person can write something freely and stall for a week on attaching their name to it."
- q: "How is this different from avoiding photographs?"
  a: "Avoiding the camera concerns whether an image of you exists at all, and it stops at the shutter. This concerns what happens after a finished thing exists: whether you release it. Many people do both and the roots differ, so it is worth separating them. If you would happily be photographed for a private album and cannot post anything, the difficulty sits in the distribution rather than in the image."
- q: "How long does this take to change?"
  a: "The first release is the slow one and the rest move quickly. Most people who unstick this describe two or three weeks of deliberate, small, dated releases before the act stops feeling like a decision. The feeling about your own recorded voice and your own first drafts moves far more slowly and may never fully go. A record that exists is the goal rather than a person who enjoys posting."
- q: "What is worth doing this week?"
  a: "Take the finished thing you already have, name one person, and put a date on it. Not a plan, not a schedule, not a public account. One artifact, one named receiver, one date inside the next seven days, with a specific question attached so the reply has somewhere to go. Then look at what actually happened, which in almost every case is very little."

# Why can't I press publish on my own work?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because making a thing and releasing it are two separate acts, and only the first one was ever assigned to you. The work has a spec, a receiver and a finish. The release has no date, no client and no consequence for skipping it, and you are the only person who can authorize it. Unassigned acts do not get done late. They never enter the queue.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
People describe this in almost the same words, and the words are usually about a contrast rather than about fear.

- "I'll do that to 10,000 people and I won't be scared. I'll be like, this is exciting. Let's do it. But ask me to make a video and post it. Like, oh, my God, it's so terrible."
- "I'd rather go get my tooth drilled instead of doing a video or a post."
- "One thing that's on my mind every single day is not posting the damn thing. [...] I have it on my phone to post. And then it's like, I don't know, hours go by. And I'm 11 o'clock at night, I'm like, got to get this post done."
- "I recorded some stuff yesterday and then I watched it and I was like, this is so bland. [...] it's so cringy."

Start with the thing that makes this pattern strange, because it is the part that rules out most of the obvious explanations.

You have stood at the front of a room. Maybe it was two hundred people, maybe it was nine, and either way you did it without a script and without your hands shaking, and afterward you could not remember being nervous because you had not been. People came up at the end. You handled the difficult question. You have done some version of that a number of times and you are, by any ordinary measure, good at it.

Then somebody suggests a video.

Not a keynote. Fifty seconds, on your own phone, in your own front room, about a subject you have explained out loud a hundred times. And something happens that has no relationship at all to the room. You agree in principle. You think about it again on Thursday. You write three versions of the opening line on your phone on a Wednesday and none of them survive Thursday. Six weeks later there is no video, and if anybody asked you why, you would say you had been busy, and you would half believe it.

Or the invitation arrives. Somebody with an audience asks you onto their show. It is a good fit, the topic is yours, and you would be excellent at it, and instead of answering you begin building a case against yourself: that you are not eloquent enough on the spot, that there are already people doing this, that you are not really the person for it. By the time you reply, if you reply, the case is finished and it is airtight. The conversation itself would have taken an hour and gone well.

Or the thing is already made. This is the version that stings most, and it is the one this page is really about. The piece is written. The video is recorded and trimmed. The file is on your phone, ready, with the caption already typed underneath it. It has been sitting there for seventeen days. You have opened it twenty-six times. Every time you open it you look at it for about a minute and put the phone back in your pocket, and at eleven at night you say to yourself that you have got to get this done, and then you go to bed.

Nothing in any of that is a confidence problem, and calling it one has probably cost you a few years. You are fine with people. You are fine with the subject. What you cannot do is the last act, the small mechanical one, the one that takes a second and cannot be taken back.

## Where Does This Show Up Across an Ordinary Week?

In more places than the obvious one, which is why it has never registered as a single pattern with a shape.

The drafts folder has eleven things in it and four of them are finished. You know which four.

You have a professional headshot from a job you left, and a profile with no picture on it, and neither of those has bothered you enough to fix in four years.

You wrote something good, and rather than releasing it you sent it to one friend, and their reply was warm, and the piece is still where it was.

You have recorded the same fifty seconds nineteen times and you have listened to none of them properly, because listening to them is a separate thing you also cannot do.

You research instead. There is always one more thing worth reading before you would be qualified to say anything, and the reading is genuinely interesting, and it is genuinely work, and you have been doing it for a long time.

You will happily promote a colleague. You will write two paragraphs about why somebody else's thing is worth looking at, in public, under your own name, without a trace of the difficulty. Then you will not mention your own.

You have a whole outbox of things you were going to send.

You have said out loud, more than once, that you should be doing more of this, in the tone of somebody describing a chore rather than a fear.

You have watched people who are worse at your subject than you are build an audience out of it, and you have felt something complicated about that, and you have still not posted anything.

And somewhere there is a piece of work that would have helped somebody and did not, because it never left the machine it was made on.

None of that is dramatic. Together it amounts to a body of finished work that nobody has seen, produced by somebody who has no trouble being seen.

## Why Can You Hold a Room and Not Post Fifty Seconds of Yourself?

Because the room releases itself, and the video asks you to be the one who releases it.

This is the whole page in one line, and it is worth taking slowly, because once you see it the pattern stops being mysterious and becomes almost administrative.

Think about what actually happens when you speak in front of people. Somebody else chose the date. Somebody else booked the space and told other people to come. By the time you are standing there, the event is happening with or without your enthusiasm, and your only remaining job is to do the thing you are already good at. You did not authorize that moment. You accepted an assignment and then executed it. The two acts were bundled together and handed to you by somebody else, and the bundling is what made it easy.

Now look at the video. There is no date. Nobody booked anything. Nobody is waiting, nothing breaks if it never appears, and no one will ever ask where it went. Every part of it, including the part where it becomes public, has to be initiated by you, on a morning you select, for reasons you have to supply yourself. And the final act in that sequence carries all of the exposure of the room with none of the momentum that carried you into it.

That is the asymmetry. Live work arrives pre-authorized. Published work has to be authorized by the person it exposes, which is a conflict of interest built directly into the task.

Two consequences follow immediately, and both of them explain things people find confusing about themselves.

The first is that getting braver does nothing. Bravery is what you spend in the room, and you have plenty of it, and it is not the resource being asked for here. What is being asked for is a decision made alone, in advance, with no external pressure and no one to perform it for.

The second is that the difficulty scales with how much the thing matters to you, which is exactly backwards from how fear usually behaves. A throwaway post goes out easily. The piece you actually care about sits for seventeen days. If this were about exposure alone, both would be equally hard.

## What Is Actually Different About the Published Version?

Four properties, and each one removes something that was carrying you through the live version.

| | The live version | The published version |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides it happens | Somebody else, in advance | You, on a day you have to choose |
| When it ends | When you stop talking | Never, in principle |
| Who is watching | People whose faces you can read | An unknown set, on their own schedule |
| What is left afterward | A memory, held by people who were there | An object, retrievable, with your name on it |
| What failure costs | An awkward twenty minutes | A permanent exhibit of the awkward twenty minutes |
| What you can do if it goes badly | Adjust in the next sentence | Nothing, except delete it and be the person who deleted it |

Read the left column and notice how much of the difficulty is being handled for you. The scheduling. The ending. The feedback. The impermanence. All four are holding weight, and all four disappear at once when the thing becomes a file.

That last row deserves a moment. In a room, a bad sentence is repaired by the next sentence, and the repair is invisible because the whole thing is a stream. A published piece has no next sentence. It stands as it is, indefinitely, and the only available correction is removal, which is itself a public act. People who are outstanding at live recovery discover they have no equivalent skill here, because the skill they have is built entirely on being able to keep going.

There is a smaller property that does a surprising amount of the work: **the artifact carries your name in a way the moment does not.** In the room you are a person talking. On the page you are an author, and what you made is filed under you, permanently, next to everything else filed under you. That is a different transaction from being looked at, and the pages about being looked at do not reach it. [The sensation of an audience on you while it is happening is a separate mechanism with its own page](/patterns/feeling-watched-and-judged/), and it is worth reading if what you recognize is the room rather than the file.

## Why Does the Release Never Get Assigned?

Because you are both the client and the supplier, and clients are the ones who set dates.

Look at everything else you completed this week. The invoice went out because somebody was waiting for it. The report happened because a date existed. The difficult conversation happened because it was on a calendar somebody else could see. None of those were easier than posting a video. Several were considerably worse. They all got done anyway, because each of them arrived with two things attached: a receiver and a deadline.

Your own release has neither, and that absence removes the single mechanism that has moved you reliably your entire working life.

This is why the standard advice sounds so reasonable and does so little. Be more disciplined. Want it more. Care less what people think. Every one of those aims at your character, and your character is not the variable that differs between the report you filed on Tuesday and the video you did not post. The variable is the structure of the task.

Watch what happens when the structure changes. Somebody commissions a piece from you with a date on it and it appears on the date. Somebody books you for the podcast, fixes a time, and you turn up and are good. A client asks for the thing and you send it, on time, without a week and a half of internal negotiation. The same person, the same exposure, the same public record afterward, and none of the difficulty. What was removed was not the exposure. What was removed was the requirement that you be the one who authorizes it.

There is a second structural fact underneath this that people rarely name. **A finished thing does not feel outstanding.** Your sense of what is unfinished runs on tasks that look incomplete, and a finished file looks complete, so it stops generating any of the low-grade pressure that gets ordinary work done. The thing that would nag you if it were half-written goes quiet the moment it is whole. That is why the pile of unreleased finished work grows so much faster than the pile of unfinished work, and why nobody notices it growing.

Put those two together and you have the mechanism. The release has no date, no receiver and no felt incompleteness. It is the only act in your week with none of the three, and it is also the only one that never happens.

## What Do People Call This, and Why Is There No Name Here?

There are several partial names, none of them fits, and I would rather tell you that than hand you a term that sends you at the wrong work.

You will meet writing block, which describes a person who cannot produce. That is a different situation, and if you are reading this you have almost certainly produced. The material exists. It is sitting on your phone.

You will meet perfectionism, which is closer and still wrong for most people here. A rising standard keeps work in revision: another pass, another version, never quite ready. That is a real pattern and it has [a page of its own that treats it properly](/patterns/how-to-stop-being-a-perfectionist/). It behaves visibly differently from this one. Somebody in that pattern is still working on the thing. You are not working on the thing. You think it is fine. It still does not go out.

You will meet self-promotion reluctance, a term from workplace research about people who do not speak for their own contribution in rooms where credit is being assigned. That research is real and [the behavior it describes is covered here in its own place](/patterns/letting-people-take-credit/), attributed properly. It is about speaking up in front of people. This is about releasing an artifact when nobody is in the room at all.

And you will meet the popular vocabulary for feeling like a fraud at work, which is a genuine and distinct experience with [its own page on this site](/patterns/impostor-syndrome/). Some people who cannot press publish have it. Plenty do not, and are perfectly certain their work is good, and still cannot release it. Attaching that name to this behavior explains the cases where it applies and quietly misfiles all the others.

So there is no term for this, and rather than invent one I want to be plain about what is actually going on: the behavior is one act, and the act is the release. Everything above describes an adjacent thing. What separates two people doing the identical stalling has nothing to do with vocabulary. What separates them is what the release means to each of them, and that has to be found in the person rather than looked up.

## When Does This Belong With Somebody Qualified Rather Than a Page Like This?

In four situations, and none of them is the ordinary version this page is written for.

If the difficulty has stopped being about publishing and has spread into whether you can be seen at all, that belongs with a licensed professional rather than an article. The markers are concrete: turning down work that requires any visibility, declining meetings you would previously have taken, a working life that has narrowed to what can be done without appearing anywhere, or physical symptoms in ordinary situations.

If it arrived suddenly, or with no relationship to any change in your circumstances, that is worth a conversation with a doctor rather than a page about a pattern. Something you have done since you were nineteen is a different object from something that started in March.

If what stops you is a specific belief about what will happen to you personally if your name appears in public, and you can name who and what, that sits outside what this page describes and outside what it can help with. Some readers have genuine reasons of safety, immigration status or personal history for staying off a public record, and those are not patterns to be worked on. They are conditions to be respected, and where the fear has a specific person attached to it, the right help is a person with the relevant training rather than an article about behavior.

And if any of this sits alongside something you are already being seen for, the person seeing you is better placed than this page and should hear about it.

For everybody else, which is most people reading, the rest applies.

## Who Are You Actually Writing To When You Cannot See Who Will Read It?

An audience you built yourself, and the one people build is unusually hostile.

Here is a thing that seems obvious and is not. In a room, you know who is there. You can see the ages, the mood, the level, whether they are following. Every adjustment you make while speaking is driven by that information, and you make dozens of them without noticing. Speaking well is largely the skill of reading a specific set of faces and responding.

Publishing removes the faces and does not remove the need to address somebody. So you supply one.

Marwick and boyd described this in 2011, working from interviews with people posting to a network where the audience is unknown at the moment of writing. Two things came out of it that are worth having. The first they called **context collapse**: the audiences that stay separate in ordinary life, the clients and the family and the former colleagues and the people you knew at nineteen, all arrive in one place, and nothing you write can be tuned for one without landing wrong for another. In conversation you speak differently to each of those groups without ever thinking about it. On a public post you have to address all of them in one sentence, and there is no sentence that works.

The second is the **imagined audience**: since the real readers are invisible, people write toward a mental construction of who is out there, and that construction guides everything about the tone. Which would be fine, except for what the construction is usually made of. Ask somebody to describe who they picture and you get a remarkably consistent answer. The sharpest person in their field. A relative who would find the whole thing embarrassing. Somebody they had a difficult professional relationship with in 2019. A stranger scrolling, looking for a reason to dismiss it.

Notice that nobody in that group is the person the work is for.

That is the mechanism behind an experience most people in this pattern report and cannot explain: you write something you know is useful, you read it back through the imagined audience, and it collapses. It has not changed. The reader has. You swapped the person who would benefit from it for a committee assembled out of everybody who has ever made you feel small.

Two honest limits on that research. It was interview work rather than an experiment, on one platform, in 2011, and platforms have changed a great deal since. And the concept has been argued about since it was introduced, including whether one term is doing the work of two, since the collapse of separate audiences and the deliberate mixing of them are different things. What the work establishes is a description that people recognize immediately, rather than a measured effect with a size attached. I am using it as a good description and I would rather say so than dress it up.

## How Big Is the Audience You Are Braced For?

Considerably larger than the one that exists, and the direction of that error is not the one you expect.

Bernstein and colleagues published a study in 2013 that is unusual because it could compare a belief against a ledger. They asked people how many others they thought had seen their posts, then compared those guesses to server-side logs of how many actually had. The guesses came in at roughly a quarter of the real figure. People systematically **underestimated** their audience, by a factor of about four.

Sit with that for a second, because it cuts against the reassurance you have been offered.

The comfortable thing to say to somebody who cannot post is that nobody is looking. The measured version says otherwise. More people see it than you think. The audience is genuinely there, and the honest version of this page has to start from that rather than from a soothing line that a single piece of evidence would knock over.

So what is being overestimated, if not the size?

The composition. The audience you are braced for is made of the four people described in the previous section, all paying full attention, all forming a durable view. The audience that exists is a much larger number of people paying very little attention for a very short time and retaining almost nothing. Both facts are true at once, and holding them together is more useful than either on its own: **more people see it than you think, and each of them thinks about it for less time than you can imagine.**

You can check the second half yourself in about a minute and it is worth doing. Pick somebody whose work you genuinely follow and admire. Now try to recall three specific things they published twelve months ago. Not the general impression. Three specific items. Almost nobody manages one, and this is a person you chose and pay attention to on purpose. That is your retention budget for somebody you like. Everybody else's, applied to you, is the same size.

There is a real tension here worth naming rather than smoothing over. A well-known finding says people overestimate how much attention is on them in a room. This finding says people underestimate how many saw their post. Those point in opposite directions and both are defensible, because they measure different things: one is a felt sense of scrutiny in a physical space, the other is a headcount on a server. [The room version is treated carefully on its own page, with its own honest limits](/patterns/feeling-watched-and-judged/). What matters here is that neither one supports the sentence you have been given, which is that nobody will see it.

The limits on the Bernstein work, plainly. It was one platform, in one year, with a distribution system that has been rebuilt several times since. It measured a specific kind of post to a specific kind of network. And the survey half of it relies on people reporting a guess, which is a rough measure of what anybody actually believed. The four-times figure is a good illustration of a direction rather than a constant you should carry around.

## Why Does Your Own Voice Sound Wrong When You Play It Back?

Because you have never heard it before, and because the recording carries information you did not know you were broadcasting.

Everybody knows the first half of this. When you speak, sound reaches your ears through the air and also through the bone of your skull, and the bone-conducted path carries lower frequencies. Your everyday voice is therefore fuller and lower than the one a microphone captures. Play back a recording and you are hearing, for the first time, the version everybody else has always heard.

That explanation is correct and it is the smaller half.

Holzman and Rousey published work on this in 1966, and their conclusion is more interesting than the version that circulates. They ran an experiment in which people heard recordings of their own voices and others', and observed a reaction well beyond mild surprise at the pitch. Their argument was that the discomfort is largely about **content rather than sound**. A recording carries extra-linguistic information about your state, your hesitations, your regional origins, things you convey continuously and never get to review. Hearing it played back is the first time you encounter yourself as a source of information you did not choose to send.

That reframes the reaction usefully. If it were purely acoustic, familiarity would fix it, and for a fair number of people it does not. What people describe when pressed is rarely about pitch. It is about hearing themselves try, and hearing what trying sounds like from outside.

The honest limits. It is a 1966 study with a small sample by any modern standard, its measures were of the era, and the confrontation effect has not been the subject of a large modern replication program that I can point you at. I am using it because its explanation fits what people actually report better than the bone-conduction story does, and you should weight it as one old study with a good argument rather than as settled fact.

One boundary worth drawing here. If what you recognize is specifically about how you look rather than how you come across, that is a different pattern with a different mechanism, and [it is covered fully on the page about stepping out of photographs](/patterns/avoiding-photos/). That page concerns whether an image of you exists at all. This one starts after the file exists and asks whether it goes anywhere.

## Why Does Watching It Back Make It Look So Much Worse?

Because you change seats between making it and watching it, and the two seats have completely different information.

While you were recording, you were inside the intention. You knew what you meant, you could feel the point you were building toward, and every sentence carried the full weight of the thing you were trying to convey. That experience is vivid and detailed and entirely private.

Then you press play and you are an audience member. You have none of the intention. What you have is thirty seconds of a person in a front room with imperfect lighting, and you are comparing it, automatically and unfairly, against professionally made material you have watched thousands of hours of.

What you feel at that moment is the gap between your intention and your artifact. It gets experienced as a verdict on you, and it is a description of a first take.

Two things follow, and both are practical.

The first is that this reaction gets much weaker with distance and much stronger with immediacy. The same clip watched an hour after recording is unbearable. Watched three weeks later, it is usually fine. Nothing about the file changed. What changed is that you no longer have the intention in your head to compare it against, so you are finally watching what everybody else was always going to watch.

The second is that **watching it back is a separate act you can decline.** There is no rule that you must review a thing before releasing it, and for a lot of people the review is where the pattern actually lives. The recording gets made. The review kills it. Skipping the review, on purpose, having decided in advance that a first take is acceptable, removes the exact step that has been eating the work.

## Why Do Two People Stall on the Same Button for Completely Different Reasons?

Because the stall is the visible output, and what produces it sits underneath and is invisible from outside.

Two people have a finished piece of work on a phone. Both have looked at it several times today. Both will still have it tomorrow. Watch them and you cannot tell them apart.

For the first, the difficulty is the claim. She thinks the work is good. What she cannot do is assert, in public, that she is a person who does this, because that assertion goes further than she believes she is entitled to go.

For the second, the work being good is not in question either, and the trouble is that a public thing cannot be recalled. He has watched something of his travel somewhere he did not choose, once, and he learned something permanent from it.

Same file, same phone, same seventeen days. Opposite situations, and every piece of advice that helps one of them is noise to the other. Tell the first person that nobody will remember it and you have reassured her about something she was not worried about. Tell the second person he is entitled to take up space and you have missed the point entirely.

That is the reason a page like this cannot end with a technique. What decides the useful move is which of these is running, and that is a question about you rather than about publishing.

Five versions follow. Most people recognize themselves clearly in one and partially in a second.

## What If the Post Is Making a Claim You Never Agreed To Make?

Then the block sits on the standing rather than on the quality, and this is the most common version.

Here is how it sounds from inside, and this is close to how people actually put it: they feel unsure on a subject, not because they doubt the material, but because there are already people who do this professionally and they are not one of them. The work is fine. The problem is the sentence the publishing act writes underneath it, which reads roughly: *I am somebody whose view on this is worth your time.*

Nobody asked you to write that sentence. It comes attached to the act, and it is a larger claim than the one you were trying to make.

You can see this clearly in the asymmetry almost everybody in this root displays. You will recommend somebody else's work publicly, at length, with your name on it, and feel nothing. The exposure is identical: same platform, same audience, same permanent record. What differs is the claim. Recommending somebody else asserts that you have taste. Publishing your own asserts that you have standing, and standing is the thing you do not feel you have been granted.

The tells for this root are specific.

**You can name the people you think are entitled to speak on this.** There is a list, you are not on it, and you could produce it without pausing to think.

**Your objection is about qualification rather than quality.** Asked what is wrong with the piece, you do not talk about the piece. You talk about who you are to be saying it.

**You promote other people freely.** This is the cleanest tell in the set and the one people are most surprised by when it is pointed out.

**It got worse as you got better.** The more you knew, the more visible the edge of your knowledge became, and the smaller your entitlement to speak felt.

**And a commission removes it entirely.** If somebody asks you for the piece, you write it and send it, because they granted the standing and you are only fulfilling a request.

The move that helps here is shrinking the claim to the size you can defend, rather than acquiring confidence. A piece released as one person's working view, said plainly on the thing itself, makes a claim you can stand behind on a bad day. Most people in this root have never once tried publishing something with the claim explicitly reduced, because it did not occur to them that the claim was adjustable.

## What If You Are Protecting Something That Cannot Be Taken Back?

Then you have understood something about permanence that most people have not thought about, and the caution is doing real work.

A published thing is unlike almost anything else you produce. It gets copied, cached, quoted out of context, and found by people you will never meet, at a time you do not choose. Nothing you can do afterward makes it stop having existed. There is very little else in ordinary life with that property.

People arrive at this root two ways. Some have simply thought it through and reached a defensible position. Others learned it once, at cost: something of theirs traveled, got screenshotted, got discussed in a room they were not in, and the lesson took permanently.

The tells here are clear.

**Your first question about anything you make is where it goes**, rather than whether it is good.

**You are fine with work that stays inside a boundary.** A document for a client, a piece for a private group, a talk in a closed room. The same content, released publicly, is a different proposition entirely.

**You have deleted something after posting it**, or wanted to badly enough that you remember the evening.

**The word that fits is exposed rather than embarrassed.**

**And you can point at the incident**, if there was one, and you can date it.

This root responds to rules better than to reassurance. What people in it need is a stated policy: what you will and will not publish, decided once, in advance, in the calm. Deciding it fresh every time, at the moment of pressing, means deciding it while exposed, which is the worst possible condition for that judgment. It is also worth saying plainly that this root sometimes needs no work at all. A considered position on a permanent public record is an adult conclusion, not a symptom, and some people should simply hold it and stop feeling bad about it.

## What If a Rule About Self-Display Was Installed Long Before Any of This?

Then the release is running into a standard that was set somewhere else entirely, and the standard has never been examined out loud.

People in this root say something close to the following, and the wording is worth reading slowly: that the resistance is imbued in them, because they do not share personal things, and that they actually find it a little obnoxious when other people do. That is a **judgment** rather than a fear, held sincerely, applied first to other people and then, inevitably, to any post that would come from them.

Which produces a very specific bind. To publish, you have to become the sort of person you have quietly disapproved of. Every piece of advice about consistency and visibility asks you to violate a rule you actually believe in, which is why the advice bounces so hard, and why you cannot follow it even when you agree with all of the reasoning about why you should.

The tells for this root.

**Your objection is moral rather than nervous.** The word that comes up is closer to distasteful than to frightening.

**You apply it to others too**, and you feel it about specific people, and you would not say so out loud.

**Somebody in your family holds the same rule**, usually more strongly, usually about a slightly different thing.

**You can publish work and not yourself.** A technical piece under your name is fine. A sentence about your own experience is not.

**And you feel a small contempt for the version of yourself that would post.** That is the fingerprint, and it is the reason the block holds against every practical solution.

The useful work here is not to demolish the rule. Some of it is worth keeping, and the people who abandon it entirely usually produce material they are embarrassed by later. The work is to find its actual edges. Almost everybody in this root, examined honestly, does not object to the whole category. They object to a specific kind of display, usually the performance of intimacy for an audience, and they have been applying a rule written for that to material that has nothing to do with it. A page about your subject, written plainly, breaks no rule you actually hold. [Where a rule was inherited and never checked against the present, the general mechanism has its own treatment](/patterns/why-did-i-give-up-on-my-dreams/).

## What If You Are Trading a Public Verdict for a Private One?

Then the release is being blocked by something you already do to yourself, and the button is only where you noticed it.

Some people cannot publish because a piece of work released into the world will be assessed, and there is already an assessment running that is considerably harsher than anything a stranger would supply. Adding an outside verdict to that is intolerable, so nothing goes out, and the standing verdict continues undisturbed.

The tell that separates this from the other four is that the commentary does not need an audience. It runs about work nobody has seen. It runs about the version you deleted. It ran about the thing you did release two years ago, and it is still running, and the actual reception of that thing was fine.

This root is worth naming here and then handing over, because it is genuinely a different pattern and treating it as a publishing problem wastes years. [The running verdict you pass on yourself is covered properly on its own page](/patterns/putting-myself-down/), including where it comes from and what actually shifts it. If you recognize yourself more in that description than in the four around it, that page is the one to read, and this one will not get you far.

What I will add, because it belongs here: people in this root frequently misidentify as the first root, the one about standing. They sound similar. The separation is clean once you ask the question. In the standing version, a commission removes the difficulty entirely, because somebody else granted permission. In this version, a commission changes almost nothing, because the assessment was never about permission.

## What If the Release Was Simply Never Anybody's Job?

Then nothing is wrong, and this is by a wide margin the best news on the page.

A meaningful number of people who cannot press publish are not carrying anything at all. They are carrying an absence. No date was ever set, no one ever asked for it, the task never entered any system they use for real work, and so it never happened, in the same way that any unassigned task never happens.

This root is chronically misdiagnosed, usually by the person in it, because we have a strong cultural habit of reading a gap in behavior as a fear. Asked why they have not posted, people in this root produce a reason, because everybody produces a reason. The reason sounds thin even as they say it. Pressed, they will admit they do not actually feel very much about it at all.

The tells are quiet rather than dramatic.

**Asked why, your own answer does not convince you.**

**There is no strong feeling attached**, just a vague sense that you should have done it by now.

**You do publish, occasionally, with no difficulty**, usually when something external forced the timing.

**Deadlines fix it completely.** Not partly. Completely.

**And you have this pattern about several unassigned things**, not only about publishing: the personal project, the thing you were going to organize, the call you meant to make.

The move for this root is structural and slightly boring, which is why people skip it in favor of examining themselves. Give the release a date, a receiver and a place in whatever system already runs your working week. Most people in this root shift it in three weeks, and the self-examination they were about to spend a year on would have found nothing, because there was nothing to find.

## Which of the Five Is Yours, and What Separates Them?

The separating question is different for each, and none of them is about how brave you are.

| The root | The question that separates it | What you would notice |
|---|---|---|
| The claim is larger than you agreed to | Would a commission remove the difficulty entirely? | You promote other people freely and freeze on your own, and your objection is about qualification rather than quality |
| Permanence and loss of control | Is your first question where it goes rather than whether it is good? | You are fine inside a boundary and not outside one, and you may be able to date the incident that taught you |
| An inherited rule about self-display | Is the word closer to distasteful than to frightening? | You feel it about other people too, somebody in your family holds it more strongly, and you can publish work but not yourself |
| A verdict already running inside | Does the assessment continue when nobody has seen anything? | A commission changes almost nothing, and the criticism arrives about work no one has ever read |
| No assignment | Does a deadline fix it completely? | Very little feeling attached, a thin reason when asked, and the same pattern across several unassigned things |

Read the right-hand column rather than the left. Most people find one row lands differently from the other four. A common pairing is the first and the fourth, which fit together neatly: an inner assessment supplies the evidence that you lack standing, and the lack of standing gives the assessment more to work with.

If two rows land equally, use the commission test. Imagine somebody you respect asking you for exactly this piece, with a date, for a real audience. Roots one and five dissolve. Root two changes shape but does not dissolve, because the permanence is still there. Roots three and four do not move at all. That single question sorts the set faster than anything else here.
## Why Does Making More of It Not Move Anything?

Because production is the part you are good at, and it buys real relief while changing nothing about the act you are avoiding.

This is the most expensive version of the pattern and the hardest one to see from inside, because it does not look like avoidance from any angle. It looks like diligence, and a good deal of it genuinely is.

People describe it plainly enough once they notice it. One version of the sentence goes: I have been writing academic notes instead of writing out some of the posts that actually ask people to do something. Read that carefully. The academic writing is real work. It is difficult, it takes skill, and it takes hours. And it is being produced instead of the thing that requires the release.

The substitutions are always upstream of the release and always defensible.

You research. There is one more paper, one more competitor, one more angle, and each of them genuinely would improve the piece, and none of them is the piece.

You restructure. The outline gets better. The outline has been getting better for four months.

You build capacity. A new tool, a better setup, a proper microphone, a system for organizing what you make. All of it will help. None of it is the button.

You make something else. A second piece, then a third. The library of unreleased work grows and each addition supplies a fresh day of feeling productive, which is exactly what makes the pattern self-funding.

And you plan a launch, at which point everything will go out at once, properly, in the right order. That launch has been eight months away for three years, because the one thing it requires is the act you cannot do, performed several times in a row.

The tell that separates this from ordinary preparation is the ratio. Count the finished, unreleased things you are holding. If the number is above three, preparation stopped being preparation a while ago. A person genuinely getting ready produces one thing and releases it. A person substituting produces a body of work and releases nothing, and feels tired at the end of every week.

The uncomfortable part is that stopping the substitution feels like stopping work. For the week and a half you spend releasing what you already have, you will produce nothing new, and it will feel like a wasted stretch. It is the only stretch in this whole pattern that actually changes the outcome.

## Why Does "Just Post It" Fail Every Time You Are Told It?

Because it instructs you to perform an act, and the act was never the difficulty.

You already know how to press the button. You could do it in a second. What you cannot do is authorize it, and an instruction from somebody else does not supply authorization, because the whole structure of the problem is that authorization has to come from you.

There is a whole genre of advice built on this misunderstanding, and it is worth taking apart, because most people in this pattern have absorbed all of it and concluded from the failure that something is wrong with them.

**Post daily for ninety days.** This works beautifully for people whose only obstacle was that no schedule existed, which is the fifth root and a real group. Given to anybody in the other four, it fails within two weeks and adds a documented personal failure to whatever was already there.

**Nobody is paying attention anyway.** Offered as comfort, and disproved by a single memory of somebody who mentioned your post, which everybody has. On the best available evidence it is also false about the numbers. More people see it than you think.

**Done is better than perfect.** Aimed at a rising standard. Useless to somebody whose piece is finished and who agrees it is fine.

**Fake it until you make it.** Aimed at the standing problem, and it asks you to make the exact claim you are stalling on, only more so, and to feel nothing about it.

And then there is the story everybody repeats, which deserves its own paragraph because it is the single most cited piece of evidence in this territory and it is not evidence.

You will have heard it. A ceramics teacher divides a class in two. One half is graded on the sheer number of pots produced, the other on the quality of a single pot. At the end of term, the best pots come from the quantity group, because they learned by making while the other group sat theorizing. The moral is that volume beats deliberation, and it gets deployed at anybody who cannot press publish, usually kindly.

The story comes from a 1993 book about the psychology of making art. It is told there as an anecdote. There is no class, no measurements, no grading rubric, no control condition and no data of any kind that anybody has ever been able to point at. It has been repeated for three decades in books, talks and posts by people who believe they are citing a study.

I am not telling you this to argue that repetition does not help. Repetition helps, obviously, and for reasons that need no study. I am telling you because the advice you have been given about this behavior rests on a story somebody made up, and you have been treating your failure to follow it as evidence about you. It is worth knowing how thin the ground is under the instruction before you conclude anything from having failed at it.

What all five of those have in common is that they address the button. The thing to work on sits one step earlier, at the point where somebody has to decide, and that step is missing from every piece of advice in the genre.

## What Actually Moves This?

Restoring the two things the release is missing, and then matching one specific move to whichever root is yours.

The general repair comes first, because it helps in all five cases and it is almost embarrassingly mechanical.

**Give the release a date and a receiver.** Not a resolution to publish more. A specific artifact, a specific day inside the next week, and a specific person or place it goes to. Then put it wherever your paid work lives, in the same system, formatted the same way, so it competes for attention on equal terms with things that actually get done. Most people in this pattern have never once written a release into the system that runs their working life, which is why it has never behaved like work.

**Decide on a different day from the one you publish.** This is the single highest-yield move here and almost nobody does it. Choose on Monday, in the calm, that the piece goes out on Thursday, and set it to go automatically. The version of you who chooses on Monday is not currently holding the file, is not currently in the exposure, and is a much better judge. Somebody who cannot press a button at the moment of exposure can very often set a scheduler three days ahead of it.

**Shrink the receiver before you shrink the work.** One named person, one date, one specific question attached so the reply has somewhere to go. This is a complete release with the audience set to one, and it exercises exactly the muscle the public version needs. It also has the property that almost nothing bad can happen, which is the point.

**Plan the hour afterward rather than the second of pressing.** People who delete usually do it inside the first hour, when the exposure peaks and there is nothing to do with it. Decide in advance what that hour contains: a walk, a task, anything that occupies you and is not checking. Half the deletions in this pattern are the result of an unoccupied hour.

Then the root-specific moves, and these matter more than the general ones.

| If your root is | The move that works | The move that wastes your year |
|---|---|---|
| The claim is larger than you agreed to | State the smaller claim on the artifact itself, in one line, in your own words | Working on confidence, or waiting to feel qualified |
| Permanence and loss of control | Write the policy once, in the calm: what you will publish, what you never will | Deciding it fresh at the moment of pressing, every time |
| An inherited rule about self-display | Find the rule's actual edge, which is usually narrower than you have been applying it | Trying to stop believing something you still believe |
| A verdict already running inside | The work on that page, which is a different project | Publishing more, faster, to prove something to the assessment |
| No assignment | A date, a receiver, and a place in the system that runs your week | Examining yourself for a fear that is not there |

Two more things belong here, both of which people skip.

**Decline the review, on purpose, for a fixed number of releases.** Decide in advance that the next three things go out without being watched back. The review is where a large share of finished work dies, and it is optional. Announce the rule to yourself before you record, so the decision is already made by the time the recording exists.

**Expect the drop, and expect it to be smaller than you planned for.** The first release after a long stall is genuinely unpleasant for about an hour and then almost nothing happens, and the nothing is disorienting in its own way. People report expecting a verdict and receiving a mild silence, and then having to sit with the fact that the thing they had built into an event was, to everybody else, a Tuesday.

## What Does This Cost Over Ten Years If Nothing Changes?

More than the individual pieces, and in a shape that stays invisible until it is a long way along.

The obvious cost is the work nobody saw, and it is the smallest one on this list.

The larger cost is what the withholding confirms. Nobody grants you the standing you feel you lack. It accumulates, slowly, out of a public record of having said things and stood behind them. Withholding produces no record, the absence of a record is read by you as further evidence that you have no standing, and the evidence tightens the block. Ten years of that produces somebody who is genuinely more qualified than most people publishing in their field and who feels less entitled to speak than they did at the start.

Then there is the audience that assembled around somebody else. Whatever you know, there are people who need it, and they found somebody. That carries no moral charge and is not meant as pressure. Somebody needed it, and while the file sat on the phone they went and found somebody else.

There is a professional cost that arrives without warning, usually at a transition. You need work, or a partner, or a room to open, and the person deciding searches for you and finds a profile with no picture, a headshot from a job you left, and nothing you have said. Everything you know exists in the heads of people who have worked with you, which is a distribution system with a very short reach.

And there is a quieter one that people mention last and mind most. You become somebody who thinks about doing this. That identity is stable, it is comfortable, it requires nothing, and it has a cost that only registers when somebody asks what you have been working on and you notice that the honest answer has been the same for four years.

Set against that, the price of the first repair is a date, a name, and about an hour of discomfort on a Thursday.

## Where Does This Stop and Something Else Begin?

Four neighboring patterns get folded into this one, and each is handled properly elsewhere, which matters because the wrong name sends you at the wrong work.

**Being looked at while it is happening.** If what you recognize is the sensation of attention on you in a room, the walk across an office, the certainty that a laugh behind you had you in it, [that is a different mechanism with its own page](/patterns/feeling-watched-and-judged/), and it carries the honest treatment of how much attention is actually pointed at you. The separation is clean. That page owns the moment of being observed. This one owns the moment of deciding whether a thing you made goes out at all, which happens alone, with nobody watching anything.

**Whether an image of you exists at all.** If the difficulty arrives when a camera comes up, and it concerns the photograph existing rather than where it goes, [that is dealt with fully on its own page](/patterns/avoiding-photos/), including why your own photographs look wrong to you and right to everybody who knows you. That page stops at the shutter. This one starts after the file exists.

**The running verdict you pass on yourself.** If the assessment continues about work nobody has seen, and a commission would change nothing, [that has its own page and its own mechanism](/patterns/putting-myself-down/). It is the root most often mistaken for this one, and treating it as a publishing problem costs people years.

**Work abandoned just before it is finished.** If the thing is at ninety percent and stays there, revised or simply dropped, [that is a separate pattern with a page of its own](/patterns/abandoning-things-at-ninety-percent/). The distinction is worth being precise about, because the two look identical from outside and behave differently. That page is about work that never reaches done. This page is about work that reached done a while ago and has not moved since. If you are still editing, read that one. If you stopped editing and the file is simply sitting there, you are in the right place.

Two smaller ones, for completeness. If the trouble is a standard that keeps rising so nothing is ever ready, [the perfectionism page treats that properly](/patterns/how-to-stop-being-a-perfectionist/). And if what you recognize is a general dependence on other people's opinion, running in the days before and after rather than at the moment of release, [that argument is made on its own page](/insights/how-to-stop-caring-what-people-think/).

What this page owns, and what the others deliberately leave alone: the finished artifact, the separate act of releasing it, and the fact that the release is the only thing in your week that nobody assigns.

## What Does the Settled Version Look Like on an Ordinary Thursday?

Considerably less dramatic than people expect, and it does not involve enjoying any of it.

A thing is finished on Monday. You look at it once, decide it is acceptable, and schedule it for Thursday morning. On Thursday morning it goes out while you are doing something else. Around lunchtime you notice a small unpleasant twinge, you check it once, three people have seen it, and you go back to work.

That is the whole of it. No transformation, no new relationship with your own recorded voice, no enjoyment of being visible.

Two other things tend to be true in the settled version. The decision and the release have been permanently separated, so nobody is ever asked to authorize something while holding it. And there is a stated line about what you publish and what you keep, written once, which means the question gets answered by a rule rather than renegotiated every time.

What has not changed is the twinge. You will still get it, at forty and at sixty, and there is no reason it should go, since it is a reasonable response to putting something permanent into the world with your name on it. What changes is that the twinge stops being the thing that decides.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

It runs at three speeds and confusing them is why people conclude nothing is happening.

**The first release takes as long as it takes and the next five are quick.** The gap between deciding to send one finished thing to one named person and actually sending it is where the whole difficulty concentrates. People sit in that gap for a week and then clear the next four in seventeen days.

**The act becomes ordinary over about two months.** Not comfortable. Ordinary, in the way that anything becomes ordinary through repetition with no disaster attached. Most people describe the change as the release losing its status as an event rather than as any new feeling about themselves.

**And the standing question moves slowest of all.** If your root is the first one, expect the sense of not being entitled to speak to outlast the behavior by a long way. You will be publishing regularly and still feel it, and that is the normal shape of it rather than a sign that something has failed. The behavior changes first and the entitlement follows it, in that order, which is the opposite of the order most advice assumes.

One honest complication about pace. If your root is the fourth, the running verdict, none of these timelines apply, because the work is on a different thing and this page is not the one that will move it.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From published research anybody can check, from the limits of that research stated plainly, and from work of my own that carries no research weight at all.

Marwick and boyd published their study of context collapse and the imagined audience in *New Media & Society* in 2011, based on interviews and observation of people posting publicly to a microblogging network. That is the source for the sections on who you are writing to and on why no single wording satisfies the whole audience. It is qualitative work, on one platform, in one year, and the concept has been argued over since, including whether the collapse of separate audiences and the deliberate mixing of them ought to be treated as two ideas rather than one. It supplies a description people recognize rather than a measured effect, and I have used it as one. The second author styles her name in lower case, deliberately, which is why it appears that way here.

Bernstein and colleagues published the study comparing believed audience size against server logs at a human-computer interaction conference in 2013, finding that people's estimates of how many had seen their posts ran at roughly a quarter of the true figure. That is the source for the claim that the audience is larger than you think and thinner in its attention than you imagine. Its limits: one platform, one year, a distribution system rebuilt several times since, and a survey half that measures a reported guess rather than a belief anybody acted on.

Holzman and Rousey published their work on hearing one's own recorded voice in 1966 in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, arguing that the discomfort turns on extra-linguistic content rather than on pitch alone. That is the source for the section on playback. It is an old study with a small sample by modern standards, and I could not point you at a large contemporary replication of it. I have used it because its explanation matches what people report better than the bone-conduction account does, and you should weigh it accordingly.

## What Is Worth Doing This Week?

One finished thing, one named person, one date inside the next seven days.

Not a plan to publish weekly. Not a public account. Not a schedule you will keep for ninety days. Take the piece that is already done, the one that has been sitting there, and pick a single person who would find it useful. Write down their name and a date. Attach one specific question, so their reply has somewhere to go and so you are asking for information rather than for reassurance. Then send it on the date, whatever you think of it that morning.

Afterward, write down two lines. What you expected to happen, and what happened. Most people find those two lines are not close, and the useful part is that the gap is now a fact you obtained yourself rather than a claim somebody made at you.

If that goes well, do it twice more before you attempt anything public. Three completed releases, to three named people, is a better foundation than any amount of deciding to be braver, because each one is a small piece of evidence that the act completes and the world carries on.

And if you cannot do even that, the thing to notice is which part stopped you. Choosing the person. Setting the date. The morning itself. Whichever step you could not complete is the one carrying the pattern, and it is a much smaller and more specific thing than a general inability to press publish.
