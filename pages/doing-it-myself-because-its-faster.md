# Why do I take back work I have already handed to someone else?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because taking it back is genuinely the cheaper option for that one task. Correcting somebody else's work costs more than doing it did, and your arithmetic is right. What the arithmetic leaves out is that explaining gets paid once and redoing gets paid every time. The judgment is sound per instance and expensive across a year.
</div>

- "I asked for help and then I did it myself anyway."
- "I did not tell them it was wrong, I just fixed it."
- "I am surrounded by capable people and I am still doing everything."

You said the thing you always say, and you meant it. Take a first pass at it, no rush, rough is fine.

It arrived on Wednesday morning. You opened the file, read the first two paragraphs, and something in you went flat in a way you have felt so many times it barely registers as an event any more. The structure was wrong. Not badly wrong. Wrong in the way that would take a conversation to explain and eleven minutes to fix.

You did not open the comment box. Your hands went to the document itself.

Ninety minutes later the version that went out was yours. Two of their sentences survived, one of them because it was genuinely good and one because you forgot to touch it. You sent a short message saying thanks, this was really useful, which was true in the sense that a starting point is useful, and untrue in every sense they would have understood it.

Nobody was told anything. There was no conversation about what was wrong, because there was no time for one, and because you were not sure you could have described it precisely enough to be fair. The whole exchange took the shape of a favor going in both directions and left no trace anywhere.

That was Wednesday. Something similar happened the previous Thursday with a different person, and something similar will happen next week.

The thing worth saying first is the thing almost nobody writing about this will say.

You were probably right.

## Was Taking It Back Actually the Wrong Call?

Almost certainly not, on Wednesday, for that piece of work.

Run the numbers the way you ran them at the time. Explaining the problem properly means first working out what the problem is, which you had not done, because recognizing a miss and describing a miss are separated by several minutes of real thought. Then it means writing that up in a way that lands without deflating anybody, waiting a day, receiving version two, and quite possibly finding that version two has fixed the thing you named and introduced something else. Call that three hours of elapsed time, an hour of your attention, and an uncertain result.

Doing it yourself: ninety minutes, certain result, no conversation, no waiting.

Ninety minutes beats three hours. It is not a close call, and treating it as a close call is one of the reasons ordinary advice about this bounces off people who are good at their jobs. You are not making an error of reasoning. You are making a correct local decision, which is a much more interesting problem, because a correct local decision repeats without ever needing to be defended.

What the calculation leaves out has nothing to do with this week. Explaining is a fixed cost. You pay it once, and the person carries the standard forward into every future version. Redoing is a recurring cost. You pay it in full every single time, and it never gets cheaper, because nothing was transferred.

So the honest version of the arithmetic looks like this.

| What you compared on Wednesday | What was actually being compared |
|---|---|
| Ninety minutes now against three hours now | Ninety minutes twelve times against three hours once |
| A certain outcome against an uncertain one | A certain outcome forever against uncertainty that ends after two rounds |
| Your time against your time | Your time against a capability that would have belonged to somebody else |
| One deadline | Every deadline of the following two years |

Eighteen hours against three. The reason nobody catches this is that the eighteen hours never arrive as a bill. They arrive as ninety minutes, twelve separate times, each of which was individually the right decision, none of which felt like a pattern while it was happening.

That is the whole page in one paragraph. Everything below is about why the pattern survives the moment you see it, which it will, because seeing it is not what has been missing.

## What Do Researchers Call This, and Where Do the Names Stop?

Three separate bodies of work touch this behavior, and none of them was built to explain it. Taken together they explain more of it than any one of them was designed to.

**Perfectionistic strivings and perfectionistic concerns** is the first, and it is the one that resolves the question people find most confusing about themselves: why the same trait produces excellent work in one person and quiet ruin in another.

The two-factor split emerged from questionnaires developed independently around 1990, whose items kept sorting into two clusters rather than one. Stoeber and Otto reviewed the accumulated evidence in 2006 and set the distinction out clearly. **Strivings** are the high personal standard and the pull toward meeting it. **Concerns** are the fear of falling short, the sense of being evaluated, the doubt about whether anything is actually finished, and the reading of a mistake as information about your worth.

Separated statistically, the two behave almost oppositely. Strivings track with achievement, conscientiousness, and, in a lot of studies, with satisfaction. Concerns track with distress in nearly every direction it can be measured. Somebody running high strivings and low concerns tends to be productive and pleasant to work with. Somebody running both tends to produce equally good work and pay for it.

For this behavior the split does real work. Strivings alone will make you exacting about the finished product and reasonably relaxed about who produced it, because the standard is about the work. Concerns attach the standard to you. A version going out under your name that you would not have written stops being a quality question and becomes an exposure question, and that is the difference between noticing a weak paragraph and being unable to leave it alone.

Two honest caveats belong here, because this literature is argued about more than its popular version admits. The flattering picture of strivings mostly appears once concerns have been statistically removed, and critics have pointed out for years that in real people the two rarely come apart that cleanly, so a finding about the purified version may say little about anyone you will meet. And Curran and Hill reported in 2019 that perfectionism scores have risen across birth cohorts since 1989, with the socially prescribed variety rising most, a result quoted everywhere with far more confidence than the method supports. Comparing average scores from very different samples across decades cannot cleanly separate a cohort shift from a change in who ends up in the sample.

**Efficacy beliefs held about another person** is the second. The familiar version of this idea concerns your judgment of what you yourself can accomplish. The version that matters here points outward, at what you privately estimate somebody else is capable of, and that estimate governs how much you hand over and how long you leave it there.

Leana studied delegation directly in 1986 across insurance claims departments and reported that how much a supervisor gave away tracked their assessment of that particular subordinate's competence, along with their own workload. Delegation behaved like a judgment about a specific person rather than a general trait of the manager, which matters if you have been treating your own version as a character flaw.

The uncomfortable half of this is what your estimate does to the person it is about. Rosenthal and Jacobson published the best known demonstration in 1968: teachers told that randomly chosen pupils were about to bloom, and those pupils gaining more over the year. The study was attacked hard within three years, principally by Elashoff and Snow, who took apart the statistical handling. Meta-analytic work since finds expectancy effects real, generally modest, and largest where expectations form before you know somebody well. The honest reading is that believing in somebody has no mystical channel. It has a behavioral one. What you believe about a person determines what you give them, how long you leave it with them, and whether you interrupt, and those things are more than sufficient to produce the outcome you expected.

**The correction cost in transfer research** is the third, and it is the one that vindicates you. Szulanski surveyed 122 transfers of established working practice inside eight large firms in 1996 and asked what actually stopped good practice moving from one part of a company to another. The dominant barriers were the receiving side's capacity to absorb the know-how and something he called causal ambiguity, meaning that even the people performing a practice well often cannot say precisely which parts of what they do make it work. Motivation ranked low.

Hold that finding against your Wednesday. Your difficulty naming what was wrong with the draft describes the ordinary condition of anybody who built a standard through repetition rather than through instruction, and it is a poor candidate for a communication weakness. You know the shape of correct without holding the rule that generates it, which is why the fastest route to a correct document runs through your own hands.

| What this vocabulary genuinely gives you | Where it stops |
|---|---|
| A split between the standard itself and the fear attached to falling short of it, which explains why two exacting people have completely different lives | It tells you which cluster you score on and nothing about what attached the fear to your name in the first place |
| Evidence that delegation tracks a judgment about one specific person rather than a fixed managerial trait | It cannot tell you whether your judgment about this person is accurate or inherited from a different person entirely |
| A measured, behavioral account of how expectations about somebody's capability shape their output | The effect is modest, contested in its most famous demonstration, and cannot be used to explain away a genuine capability gap |
| Confirmation that transferring know-how is expensive for structural reasons, and that your inability to articulate the standard is normal | Naming the cost as structural does nothing to decide whether this particular cost is worth paying once |
| A description of what sustained exhaustion looks like when it has three components rather than one | It describes the state and says nothing about why you would refuse to leave it |
| A word for the difference between correcting work and taking it over | The word does not survive contact with a Wednesday deadline unless something else has changed |

Here is where all three stop, said plainly. Every one of them describes a **what**. Two people can take back the identical task on the identical afternoon, produce the identical rewrite, and send the identical thank-you message, while the thing underneath has nothing in common between them. One learned an exact standard in a place that punished misses. One got blamed at twenty-six for signing off on somebody else's numbers and has checked everything since. Nothing in any of this research separates those two, and the separation is the part that has to happen before anything moves.

## When Does This Belong With a Licensed Professional Rather Than a Page?

Before anything practical, since it decides whether the rest of this applies to you at all.

If you cannot stop working when there is genuinely nothing urgent, and an empty evening produces something closer to dread than to relief, that is worth taking to somebody qualified rather than reading about. If your body has begun sending signals you are routinely overriding, including sleep that has stopped working, that belongs with a doctor first and everything else second. If the exhaustion has flattened into something that no longer lifts on a holiday or a long weekend, the distinction between tiredness and something needing treatment is not one to make on your own.

And if a mistake by somebody else produces a reaction in you that frightens you, or frightens the people near you, that is the clearest signal on this list. None of that means the rest of the page is wrong about you. It means the order matters, and that comes first.

## Why Do Two People Take the Same Task Back for Completely Different Reasons?

Because the take-back is the visible part, and the visible part is the least informative thing about it.

Five roots follow. They produce the same Wednesday. They need entirely different things, and the most common way people waste a year on this is by treating a root they do not have. Somebody whose real problem is one specific untrustworthy colleague can spend twelve months working on their relationship with control and change nothing, because the problem was accurate and local the whole time.

Read all five. Most people find one lands harder than the rest, and a few find two.

## What If the Standard Really Is Higher and Really Does Matter?

Start here, because it is the most common and the most badly served by advice.

In a great many jobs the standard is the product. If you do surgical work, structural work, legal drafting, financial control, safety-critical engineering, or anything where a miss is discovered by somebody other than you at a moment you cannot choose, then an exacting standard is the reason you are employed. It has economic value. It was built through repetitions that cost you something. Telling you to relax it is telling you to become worse at the thing you are paid for, and you are right to ignore anybody who says it.

This root also usually has a history. People with an unusually precise standard normally developed it somewhere that required one: a first job with no supervision and real consequences, a stretch where you were the only check on the work, a period where a small miss cost you something you remember clearly. The standard was a correct adaptation to a real environment.

What changes is the room. The standard has no mechanism for noticing that the room changed, so it goes on applying its original settings to a situation that no longer resembles the one that built it. A person who learned to check everything because nobody else was checking will carry on checking everything into a team of six competent adults, and will experience that checking as diligence, because for several years it was.

The tell for this root is that your take-backs are concentrated. They cluster in one domain, usually the one you are best at, and you are surprisingly relaxed about handing over things you know less well. If you are exacting about the document and genuinely fine with somebody else booking the travel, the standard is doing what a standard does, and the question is only whether it is still calibrated to this room.

## What If You Were Blamed Once for Somebody Else's Output?

Then the grip is a scar, and arguments about efficiency will not reach it.

The scene is specific and most people who carry it can name the year. Something went out with your name on the approval line. You had not checked it in detail, because checking it in detail was not the job, and because the person who produced it had never given you a reason to. It was wrong. The consequence landed on you, in a room, in front of people whose opinion mattered, and the sentence you remember is some version of you signed it off.

Whatever the actual damage, the lesson installed itself in a single afternoon. Checking everything yourself is cheap insurance against the worst professional experience of your life.

Argyris described the general form of this in organizations: moves that protect a person from embarrassment and simultaneously block the learning that would remove the cause of the embarrassment. The take-back does exactly that. It prevents the exposure, and it also prevents the only thing that would make the exposure less likely, which is somebody else developing to the point where they can be trusted with it.

The tell for this root is disproportion in the wrong direction. You will hand over something genuinely high stakes without much difficulty and then be unable to let a low-stakes thing go, because the low-stakes thing resembles the original scene more closely. The stakes are not driving it. The resemblance is.

## What If Explaining It Genuinely Takes Longer Than Doing It?

Then you are describing the most defensible root on this list, and it deserves to be treated seriously before anything is said about its cost.

The reason explaining is expensive is that you are trying to transmit something you hold without words. You can see instantly that an opening paragraph does not work. Producing the sentence *this opening assumes the reader has already agreed with you* requires you to reverse-engineer a judgment you made in half a second, and that reverse-engineering is real cognitive work you have never been asked to do. Szulanski's causal ambiguity is exactly this, sitting in a firm rather than in a person: the practitioners of a working practice often cannot specify which parts of it are load-bearing.

So the arithmetic is right today. It is also the arithmetic of somebody paying interest forever to avoid paying principal once.

Here is the part that makes this root distinct from the others. It is fixable with a specific, boring, transferable skill, which is the ability to say what is wrong at the level of the rule rather than the instance. *Fix the intro* transfers nothing and guarantees the next draft has the same problem in a different place. *Every section should open with the thing the reader disagrees with, not the thing they already accept* transfers a rule, and a rule survives into work you never see.

The tell for this root is that you feel relief rather than frustration when you take the task back. There is no anger in it. There is the specific pleasure of a problem becoming tractable, which is what happens when a task moves from a channel you cannot control into one you can.

## What If Being the Reliable One Is What You Are Valued For?

Then handing work over subtracts from the only account you have been keeping, and no efficiency argument is going to compete with that.

This root is quieter than the others and people rarely say it out loud, including to themselves. If your standing in a room rests on being the person things can be given to, then every successful delegation reduces the visible evidence for that standing. Not by much. By a little, each time. And the fear underneath has very little to do with the work turning out badly. What sits there instead is a fear of being unnecessary, which is a different fear entirely and much harder to say.

Swann's work on self-verification describes people actively seeking out feedback that confirms an existing self-view, including views that are unflattering, because confirmation makes the world predictable in a way that flattery does not. If *reliable* is the load-bearing view, then a week in which nothing came to you produces a low-grade unease that has no obvious cause, and taking something back is the fastest way to restore the reading.

There is a second, stranger mechanism here. Mochon and colleagues reported in 2012 that people value objects they assembled themselves above identical objects they did not, and that the effect strengthens in people whose sense of competence has recently taken a hit. Applied to your Wednesday: the version you rewrote arrives with value you added by touching it, over and above whatever it gained in quality. That inflation is completely invisible from the inside. It is a large part of why comparing your version with theirs never comes out the way an outside reader would score it.

The tell for this root is that the exhaustion carries resentment and you would still not give it up. Somebody offers to take something permanently off your plate and you feel a flicker of something that is definitely not relief. That flicker is the most useful piece of information on this page, and it points here.

## What If You Simply Do Not Trust the Person in Front of You?

Then you may have an accurate assessment of one working relationship, and treating it as a general trait is how it stays unsolved for years.

This is worth saying plainly because the whole self-improvement register pushes the other way. Sometimes the person is genuinely not good at this. Sometimes they are capable and disengaged. Sometimes they are capable, engaged, and mismatched to this particular kind of work. All three are ordinary, none of them is about you, and all three are solvable in ways that have nothing to do with your relationship with control.

The diagnostic takes ten minutes and almost nobody runs it. List the last ten things you took back and write down who produced each one. If eight of the ten carry the same name, the problem is specific: one relationship, one skill gap, or one conversation that has been avoided for eighteen months. If the ten names are eight different people, the pattern belongs to you and will travel with you into your next job, your next team, and your kitchen.

Most people are genuinely unsure which they have until they see the list, and a meaningful fraction are surprised by what it shows.

## How Do You Tell Which of the Five Is Yours?

| Root | The question that separates it | The tell |
|---|---|---|
| A standard that is genuinely necessary | Which domains do you take work back in, and which do you leave alone? | It is narrow. You are exacting in your area of mastery and relaxed elsewhere |
| Blame you carried once for somebody else | Can you name the year and the room? | Disproportion. Low-stakes items are harder to release than high-stakes ones |
| The correction cost being real | What do you feel in the first second after you take it back? | Relief rather than anger. The problem became tractable and that felt good |
| Identity built on being reliable | How does a week where nobody needs you actually feel? | A flicker of something other than relief when work is taken away permanently |
| Not trusting this specific person | Of the last ten take-backs, how many names are on the list? | One name dominates, and there is a conversation you have been postponing |

The five are not mutually exclusive and two of them very often run together. The standard root and the correction-cost root are close relatives, and the identity root quietly funds all the others, since it supplies the reason to keep paying.

## At What Point in the Task Do You Take It Back?

The stage tells you something the behavior alone does not, and almost nobody looks at it.

| When you take it | What is usually driving it | What it costs the other person |
|---|---|---|
| Before they start, by adding so much detail there is nothing left to decide | Anxiety about the outcome, discharged in advance | They never form a judgment, since every judgment was pre-made |
| In the first day, via a check-in that becomes a working session | Difficulty tolerating a period with no visibility | They learn that early drafts get co-authored, and stop trying to arrive with a real one |
| At the halfway point, when a preview shows the shape is wrong | The most defensible timing, and often the correct one | Moderate, provided you say what was wrong. Severe if you only take the file |
| At the end, by rewriting the finished version silently | Standard, deadline, or an inability to name the fault | The highest cost of all. They produced a whole thing and learned nothing from its failure |
| After delivery, by quietly fixing it downstream | Protecting them, protecting yourself, or both | They believe the work was fine, so nothing improves and you now do it forever |

The last row is the one people are least aware of and it is the most expensive. Repairing something after it has gone out, without mentioning it, produces a person who is genuinely confused about why they are not given more responsibility, and a version of you who is certain they cannot handle it. Both of you have evidence. Only one of you has all of it.

## Where Does This Hide When You Would Not Call It Taking Over?

| What it looks like | What is actually happening |
|---|---|
| Asking them to send you the file rather than a summary | Reviewing the whole artifact keeps the option of editing it open |
| Sitting next to them while they do it, to help | A rewrite performed through somebody else's hands, which reads as generosity |
| Giving the task to the person who needs the least explanation, every time | The capable person gets more work and the developing person never develops |
| Rebuilding the spreadsheet because their formulas were structured differently | Structure preference presented as an error, which is the easiest disguise this wears |
| Handling the client relationship personally because it is delicate | Every relationship is delicate, which makes this a rule wearing the costume of an exception |
| Volunteering to do the boring part so they can focus on the interesting part | The boring part is usually the part that carries the standard |
| Reformatting somebody's slides at midnight so they look consistent | Consistency is real and this is also a rewrite with a defensible name |
| Doing the household version and calling it a preference | The behavior does not stay at work, and the domestic version is where it is most visible to other people |

That last row deserves a sentence of its own. Almost everybody who runs this pattern at work also runs it at home, and the home version is the one their partner has already noticed. If reading the row produced a specific memory of a specific face, that is worth more than any other line on this page.

## Why Does Rewriting Feel Easier Than Saying What Was Wrong?

Because rewriting requires no articulation and feedback requires a great deal.

You saw the problem in about a second. What you had at that moment was a judgment, not a sentence. Turning the judgment into a sentence means working out which feature of the draft triggered it, checking that the feature is really the cause rather than a correlate, and finding words that describe it without describing the person. Several minutes of concentrated thought, and the kind that is uncomfortable, because it exposes how much of your standard you cannot actually state.

The rewrite skips all of it. Your hands already know what correct looks like. You can produce correct without ever knowing why the original was incorrect, and you will feel competent throughout, because you are being competent throughout.

There is a social layer stacked on top. Sending it back means saying the thing is short, watching that land on somebody's face, and then holding uncertainty until version two arrives. Doing it yourself has no social cost at all. Most people will pay ninety minutes of their own evening to avoid two uncomfortable sentences, and will keep paying, because the ninety minutes are spent alone and the two sentences are spent in front of a person.

The one line worth carrying out of this section: the rewrite is the cheapest available repair for a fault you cannot yet describe. That makes it the honest first move and a poor tenth one.

## Why Does the Third Time Change What This Is?

Because the first take-back is a judgment about a task and the third one is a judgment about a person.

Take-back one is clean. Specific piece of work, specific shortfall, specific deadline, decision made on the evidence in front of you. Nothing about it says anything general.

Take-back two is still defensible. Different task, same person, and a small note has now been made somewhere you are not looking.

By take-back three, something has changed and nothing announced it. You are no longer deciding case by case. You are operating a conclusion, and the conclusion is that work from this person requires your hands before it is finished. From here forward the decisions do not produce the conclusion. The conclusion produces the decisions, and each one still feels like a fresh assessment, because it arrives with a fresh justification attached.

The person has noticed. They noticed around take-back two. They have not said anything, because the sentence they would have to say is unsayable at work, and because you have never once told them anything was wrong, which leaves them nothing concrete to raise.

This is where the actual choice sits, and it is a much smaller and more specific choice than *learn to delegate*. It is the choice to spend the third occasion differently: same forty minutes, spent on three exact sentences instead of a whole document. Not because your version would have been worse. Because the third occasion is the last one where the situation is still about a task.

## What Does the Other Person Actually Experience?

Something quieter and more corrosive than you would expect, and almost never a grievance.

They see the version that went out. They can tell which parts were theirs, because writing has a voice and so does a spreadsheet and so does a slide deck. They count roughly how much survived. Nobody says anything, since there is nothing to say: you were nice about it, you thanked them, and they cannot accuse you of editing.

What follows looks nothing like resentment. What follows is recalibration, and it is rational. Effort settles at the level that survives the rewrite anyway, because effort above that level is discarded. Clarifying questions stop, since the answers do not change the outcome. Drafts start arriving earlier and rougher, which reads to you as declining standards and is actually an accurate read of the system they are in.

Within about four rounds you have manufactured the exact evidence you were worried about at the start. They now produce work that needs your hands. You are correct about that. The correctness is the trap, because there is no moment in the sequence where you were wrong about anything.

## Why Does Nobody Ever Tell You They Have Stopped Trying?

Because the honest sentence would end their standing in the room.

Imagine it said out loud: *I stopped putting real effort into these because you rewrite them anyway.* True, useful, and career damage with no upside for the person saying it. There is no version of that sentence that does not sound like an excuse for poor work, which is precisely what you would be entitled to hear in it.

So the information never travels as words. It travels as behavior, and behavior is ambiguous. Less initiative reads as disengagement. Rougher drafts read as declining care. Fewer questions read as either confidence or indifference, depending on your mood that week. Every one of those readings is available, every one of them fits the observable facts, and every one of them points at the person rather than at the loop the two of you are in.

This is the specific reason the pattern can run for a decade without correction. It generates no feedback. The one person positioned to tell you is the one person who cannot afford to.

## Is the Exhaustion Telling You Anything?

Yes, though probably not what the popular explanation says.

Maslach and Jackson described sustained work exhaustion in 1981 as having three components rather than one: the depletion itself, a growing cynicism toward the work and the people in it, and a falling sense of accomplishment even where accomplishment is objectively high. That combination fits this pattern precisely. You are tired, you have developed a private low opinion of what everybody around you would do without you, and you feel less impressive to yourself every year despite doing more than anyone.

The rest matters here. A weekend replaces hours. It leaves the position untouched, which is why Monday reads exactly like the Friday before it, and why people running this pattern report that holidays help for about four days.

One popular explanation deserves to be retired. The idea that self-control drains like fuel from a tank was repeated for twenty years and taught to a generation of managers. A large preregistered replication run across many laboratories and reported by Vohs and colleagues in 2021 found effects close to zero. Whatever is happening to you on Thursday evening is better explained by the number of separate decisions routed through one person than by a reservoir running low, and the difference is practical. A reservoir suggests resting more. A routing problem suggests changing what gets routed.

## Why Would You Not Give This Up Even If You Could?

Because it is a position and not only a burden, and positions are not surrendered for being tiring.

Being the one who can carries real goods. First refusal on the interesting work. A settled place in every room you walk into. An unambiguous answer to the question of why you are needed here, which is a question most people carry unanswered for their entire careers. When something matters, it comes to you, and there is a particular satisfaction in that which nobody who has felt it describes as a burden.

The resentment is real at the same time, and both are true at once without contradiction. You can be genuinely furious about the volume and genuinely unwilling to give up the position, and the fury is often about the volume specifically, rather than about the arrangement that produces it.

This matters practically. Anybody working on this while pretending they want to be relieved of it will quit in the fourth week, because the plan was built on a false description of what they want. The workable version starts from the truth: you want the position and you want it to cost less. Those are compatible. Wanting the position and refusing to name that you want it is what makes them incompatible.

## Why Does Just Delegate More Never Work as Advice?

Because it names an outcome and skips every mechanism that produces the current one.

You know you should delegate. You have delegated. What happened next was that a specific piece of work came back in a specific condition on a specific Wednesday, and you made a defensible decision under time pressure. Advice built on the assumption that the behavior is caused by not knowing better cannot touch a behavior caused by a correct local calculation. It is not as easy as saying, well, why don't you just do it.

*Done is better than perfect* fails for a related reason. In a lot of jobs it is false, and you know it is false, so the sentence discredits everything attached to it. In work where a miss is expensive and discovered by somebody else, done and wrong is worse than late and right, and any advice that requires you to pretend otherwise will be discarded by the part of you that is good at your job.

The move that actually changes anything is narrower than either. Sort your tasks by whether a miss is recoverable. On the recoverable ones, give the whole thing away, including the part where a version you would not have written reaches somebody else and nothing catastrophic follows. On the unrecoverable ones, keep the standard and change the channel: the same forty minutes, spent on rules rather than on a rewrite.

## What Is the Difference Between Correcting and Taking Over?

Where the file sits when you are done.

Correcting means the work goes back with what is wrong named precisely enough to act on, and the next version is produced by them. Taking over means the work stays with you. Everything else about the two is identical, including how much of your time it costs, which is why they are so easy to confuse from the inside.

The quality of a correction is measurable by one test: could they have produced your version from what you told them? *Fix the intro* fails. *Tighten this up* fails. *Every section should open with what the reader has not yet accepted, and this one opens with what they already agree with* passes, because it transfers a rule that will apply to documents you never see.

Naming rules is harder than rewriting and it is the entire skill. It is also, usefully, a thing you get better at fast, since each rule you manage to state once is available forever, and most people find they have about eight rules total rather than eighty.

## What Does This Cost Across Ten Years?

A ceiling, and one that arrives earlier than anybody expects.

Your output stops at what one person can personally touch. That is a hard number, it does not move much with effort, and most people running this pattern hit it in their early thirties and then spend a decade pressing against it with longer hours. Around you sits a group of capable people who have learned to bring drafts rather than decisions, since drafts are what survive contact with you.

Nothing visibly breaks. That is what makes it hard to see and hard to argue with. The work is good. Clients are satisfied. Your reputation is excellent and entirely deserved. You simply arrive at a career where every good thing that happens has to pass through your hands first, and where a two-week absence produces a backlog rather than a functioning system continuing without you.

The domestic version follows the same curve and is usually further along. The person you live with stopped offering some time ago, on the reasonable grounds that offers were being converted into supervision. What you experience as carrying everything, they experience as having been quietly retired from a job they were willing to do.

## What Actually Changes This?

Tasks, not intentions. Everybody who works on this by deciding to delegate more is back where they started inside a week, because a general resolution has nothing to hold onto when Wednesday arrives.

Pick one piece of recoverable work. Recoverable means a miss is embarrassing and fixable, which describes far more of your week than your instincts say. Name what finished looks like before it starts, in writing, in about four lines, and agree out loud that their version is the version that ships.

Then leave it alone and let it ship. Including the parts you would have done differently. Especially those.

Do it three times with the same person, because one round teaches nobody anything and the value is entirely in the third. Round one produces something you would not have written. Round two produces something closer, because round one was followed by three sentences instead of a rewrite. Round three is where you find out whether the transfer worked, and whether the estimate you have been carrying about this person was ever accurate.

There is a second half that is easy to skip and does most of the actual work. Oftentimes it is also as simple as actually removing things from your life. Not every task on your plate needs a better owner. A real portion of what you are protecting so carefully does not need to exist at all, and the fastest reduction in the number of things routed through you comes from deleting rather than from handing over.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Two speeds, and confusing them is how people conclude it is not working.

The behavior moves in a few months if you work on named tasks with named people, because it is concrete, it has an obvious trigger, and every piece of work anybody sends you is another repetition. Most people can hand something over and leave it alone within a quarter.

The feeling moves much more slowly and may never fully go. You will notice the pull to open the file long after you have stopped opening it. That gap is normal and closes in the wrong order: the action changes first and the wanting follows, sometimes years later, sometimes not at all. Waiting to feel relaxed about it before acting differently is how people stay exactly where they are for a decade.

Expect it back under pressure. A quarter where the numbers are bad, a new person who has not built any track record with you, an absence that leaves you covering three roles. The pattern returns because the conditions that justified it returned, which is a return of the conditions rather than a loss of everything you built.

## What Does the Resolved Version Look Like?

Not a lower standard, and any description that involves lowering it should be treated with suspicion.

The standard stays. What changes is that it stops being the only mechanism holding the work up. You notice the pull, spend the same energy on three exact sentences, and let a version go out that you would have written differently. Some of what comes back is worse than yours and survives contact with the world perfectly well. Some of it is better than yours in ways you would not have thought of, which is genuinely disorienting the first few times and then becomes the most interesting part.

The other change is quieter. People start bringing you decisions rather than drafts, because drafts stopped being the thing that gets a response. Your week reorganizes around the small number of things that genuinely require you, and the number turns out to be much smaller than the version of you reading this would guess.

You will still take things back. Occasionally that will be the right call, on a real deadline, for a real reason. The difference is that it will be a decision you made once rather than a position you have been defending for eleven years.

## What Is Worth Doing the Next Time Something Comes Back Wrong?

One rule, and it is closer to a measurement than to a change.

The next time work comes back and you feel the pull to open the file, set a timer for ten minutes and leave the file closed. Write three sentences somewhere else: what is wrong, why it is wrong, and what finished would look like. Send those and nothing else. Then write down how long the three sentences actually took, next to the number you would have guessed beforehand.

Do that three times and read the six numbers together. Most people badly overestimate the cost of articulating the standard, sometimes by a factor of five, and seeing the real figure once does more than any amount of deciding to be different. A few find the estimate was accurate, which is also worth knowing, since it points at a genuine skill gap in naming rules rather than at anything about control.
