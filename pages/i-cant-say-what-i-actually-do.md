# Why can't I say what I actually do in one sentence?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because the question asks for a category and the only true thing you have is a plan. A category word works by already being known to whoever is listening, and what you are building has no such word yet. So every honest reply comes out as a description of something unfinished, and it fails on compression rather than on content.

Somebody at a barbecue asks what you do.

It is the smallest question in English. It is asked to be polite, it takes two seconds to answer, and everybody else in the garden has managed it today without noticing. You have been thinking about your answer since you parked the car.

What comes out is one of three things. The old answer, which is accurate about a life you have mostly left. A long one, with two conditions in it, that you can hear going wrong somewhere around the fourth word. Or a compressed one that sounds far more finished than anything you have actually built, and which you will replay on the way home.

Then the follow-up arrives. What kind of thing. Who do you do that for. Oh, so is that full time. And you say something, and the conversation moves on, and it is completely fine, and you carry it out to the car with you.

The private verdict that forms after enough of those is usually some version of not being good with words, or not having got it clear enough in your own head yet, or being further behind than you thought. All three are wrong in a specific way, and the specific way is worth having, because it changes what there is to do about it.

## What Is That Question Actually Asking For?

A word. One word, at a particular level of generality, that the person asking already knows.

This is worth taking seriously, because it explains how a true answer can fail. Rosch and Mervis set out in the mid nineteen-seventies that people carry categories at several levels of generality and reach for one of them by preference. Above it sits the wide level, furniture, tool, vehicle, which tells you almost nothing. Below it sits the fine level, kitchen chair, adjustable spanner, hatchback, which tells you more than anybody needed. In between sits what they called the basic level, chair, hammer, car, and that is where ordinary talk lives. It is the rung where one word carries the most information for the least effort, and it is the one people name fastest, use most, and learn earliest as children.

"What do you do" is a request for a basic-level word. Teacher. Plumber. Nurse. Accountant. The word is doing something quite mechanical: it hands the listener a slot to put you in so the conversation can continue. It carries a rough picture of your Tuesday, a rough picture of who else is in your world, and about four obvious follow-up questions.

Now look at what you have available. What you are building has no basic-level word attached to it yet, because that kind of word is something a group of people share, and you are not yet part of a group who do this under one heading. You can supply the wide level, which comes out as consulting, or helping people, or something with data, and lands as nothing at all. You can supply the fine level, which comes out as four sentences about the specific work of last Thursday, and lands as far too much. The middle rung is missing, and the middle rung is the only one the question was asking for.

Two honest limits on that. Rosch's work was done on ordinary object categories, birds and furniture and tools, and stretching it to how a person describes their working life is my extension rather than her finding. And the strong version of prototype theory has been argued over for fifty years, with competing accounts of how categories work running alongside it the whole time. What has held up well is the narrow part I am using: there is a preferred level of generality in ordinary talk, and a word from the wrong rung makes a listener work harder.

## Why Does a True Answer Land as a Failed One?

Because the exchange runs on rules that have nothing to do with truth, and a true answer can break every one of them at once.

Grice set out in the nineteen-seventies that conversation runs on a set of shared expectations rather than on accuracy alone. Say as much as is needed and no more. Be relevant to what was asked. Be orderly. Nobody teaches you any of that, and everybody notices instantly when it is broken.

Watch what your honest answer does to that list. It runs long, because the conditions have to travel with it. It is relevant to a question slightly different from the one asked, because it answers what you are building rather than what you do. And it arrives out of order, because the background usually has to come first for the thing to make any sense.

So the listener experiences something being slightly off and cannot say what. You experience the same thing from the inside and you can say exactly what, which is worse. That is why the failure registers as social rather than factual. Nothing you said was untrue. What went wrong was the shape.

The limit worth stating plainly: Grice's account is a framework from philosophy rather than a measured result, and it has been argued with continuously since, including by people who think the whole set collapses into a single principle about relevance. I am using it as a description that people recognize, and it is worth exactly that much.

There is a second-order effect that makes it worse. Because you already know the answer is going to come out shapeless, you start editing it before you open your mouth, and editing while speaking is what produces the hesitation people actually hear. The stumble reports live compression rather than confusion about your own life.

## Where Does This Show Up in an Ordinary Week?

In more places than the party, and most of them do not look like the same event.

| Where it happens | What it looks like from outside | What is going on underneath |
|---|---|---|
| A barbecue, a christening, a school gate | A slightly long answer, then a change of subject | The middle rung is missing, so the answer gets built live and comes out at the wrong level |
| The occupation box on a form | A pause, then the old answer typed in | A box takes one word and cannot be steered, negotiated or charmed |
| A landing card at a border | Whatever you wrote last time | The stakes are zero and the jolt still arrives, which tells you it is not about consequences |
| A hairdresser making conversation | Full easy sentences about the old answer | Nothing is at stake and the old answer is available, so the machinery never has to run |
| Being introduced by somebody else | Relief, then a small correction you decide not to make | Somebody supplied a category and it is nearly right, which is more than you can do |
| A name badge with a blank line under your name | Left empty, or filled in with a company | The line asks for a word and offers no room for a condition |
| A message asking what you are up to now | Drafted, left, rewritten in the evening | Written answers get far more editing and land no better, which is information |
| Somebody in your own field asking | Three words and no difficulty at all | Shared vocabulary supplies the missing rung, so nothing has to be built |

Read the last row against the first. Same person, same work, same week, and one exchange is effortless while the other is not. That contrast is the most useful fact on this page, because it puts the problem outside your head. What changes between those two rows is how much vocabulary is already shared, and that is a property of the room.

## Why Is Your Answer in the Wrong Tense?

Because the question is asked in the present and the only true thing you have sits in the future, and no amount of rewording fixes a mismatch of tense.

"What do you do" is present, habitual, and about a settled state of affairs. It expects something that was also true last month and will be true next month. What you have instead is a thing that will be true if the next stretch goes well. So the honest sentence has to be built in a tense the question did not ask for, and the moment it arrives, the listener hears a plan.

There are three tenses available to you and each one costs something different.

**The past.** The old answer. It is a fact, it sits at the basic level, it closes the exchange in two seconds and it is entirely true about a life you have largely left. The cost is quiet and it compounds. Every time you say it, the new thing becomes a little less real to you, and the people around you keep updating a picture that is already out of date.

**The future.** What you are building. This is the only fully honest answer, and it hands the listener a promise where a fact was expected. The cost lands immediately, in the follow-up question, and it lands again privately, because saying it out loud creates a small obligation that you now carry around.

**The present, compressed.** A confident single line about the thing as though it were already running. This one closes the exchange beautifully and it is the most expensive of the three, because you now have to stand in front of anybody who heard it, and the distance between the sentence and Thursday morning is yours alone to hold.

Most people in this pattern rotate through all three depending on the room, without ever deciding to. The rotating is what produces the private sense of being slippery about your own life. Choosing one on purpose, for a given kind of room, removes most of the discomfort without changing a single word of the sentence itself.

If what you recognize is that you talk about the thing warmly and often, in a tense that keeps it permanently ahead of you, and there is a finished plan sitting there waiting for somebody to say you may begin, [the page on waiting for permission](/patterns/waiting-for-permission/) is the one to read.

## When Does This Belong With Somebody Qualified Rather Than a Page Like This?

In four situations, and none of them is the ordinary version this is written for.

If the question has started shaping your calendar, that is past what an article can help with. The markers are concrete rather than moody: turning down invitations because you know the question is coming, declining an introduction you would once have taken, going to fewer things month by month, or a working life that has quietly narrowed to rooms where nobody will ask. Avoidance that costs you real opportunities belongs with a licensed professional rather than with a better sentence.

If a flatness has sat over everything for months regardless of what you do, if sleep has stopped returning, or if there is any thought of harming yourself however briefly it passes through, that goes to a licensed professional now rather than later. Everything on this page can wait indefinitely and none of it is urgent.

If the difficulty arrived suddenly, with no change in your circumstances to attach it to, that is worth a conversation with a doctor rather than an article about a pattern. Something you have done since you were twenty-two behaves differently from something that started in March.

And if there is a genuine reason you cannot describe your work to people, involving your safety, your status somewhere, or one specific person who must not know, that sits outside what this page describes and outside what it can help with. Treat it as a condition to be respected, and the right help there is somebody with the relevant training rather than a page about behavior.

For everybody else, which is nearly everyone reading, the rest applies.

## What Happens to a Phrase You Circle for Weeks?

It gets better and it never closes, and the improving is what keeps you at it.

The shape is always the same. A phrase turns up that feels close. You write it down. Over the following days you improve it, swap one word, tighten the middle, try it in the shower, and each version is genuinely better than the last, which is the trap. Progress on the wording is real and it is measured against the wrong thing. Then twelve days have gone and the phrase is still not sayable at a barbecue.

Here is the part nobody points out. A phrase that names a finished thing is a description. A phrase that names an unfinished thing is a forecast, and a forecast cannot be made accurate by editing, because the thing it is about has not happened. So the search cannot end well. It ends when you stop, or it ends when the thing finishes and supplies its own word.

That has a practical consequence worth sitting with, because it is the opposite of what everybody advises. The clarity you are trying to write your way into usually arrives from outside. It arrives the first time somebody other than you describes you to a third person and gets it roughly right. It arrives with the first result somebody paid for or thanked you for. It arrives when you have done the same thing enough times that there is a same thing. In every case the word comes after the fact, and every hour spent trying to make it come first is spent on the only sequence that has never worked.

None of which makes writing things down a waste. The question worth holding is what the writing is for. A line written now is a working note for you rather than a finished answer for other people, and treating it as the second is what turns half a month into six months.

## Why Do Two People Fail the Same Sentence for Completely Different Reasons?

Because the stumble is the visible part, and the visible part carries almost no information about what produced it.

Two people give an identically vague answer at the same event. One of them is doing something that genuinely has no shared word yet, and would answer instantly if one existed. The other has a perfectly available word, uses it easily at work, and cannot say it in front of a particular kind of person because the sentence would have to be defended.

From outside those two are indistinguishable. Every piece of advice built on the stumble gets handed to both of them, and it will be wrong for at least one. Telling the first person to be more confident does nothing, because confidence was never the missing part. Telling the second person to get clearer on their positioning does nothing either, because they are entirely clear and the difficulty is in the room.

Four situations produce this reliably enough to be worth separating. They are not stages and most people carry one dominant version with a second running underneath it. The first goes first because it is the most common and the most immediately relieving to recognize.

## What If the Thing Simply Has No Word Yet?

Then there is no sentence to find, and the failure to find one is a correct result being read as a personal shortfall.

This is the most common version and the easiest to miss, because it looks exactly like confusion from outside. The person is not confused. Ask them what they did last Tuesday and you will get forty minutes of specific, ordered, confident detail. Ask them for the word and there is nothing, because the word does not exist in the language yet.

Words for what people do are not invented by the people doing them. They arrive when enough people are doing a similar enough thing under a similar enough heading that the language finds it economical to have a single term. Until that happens, every honest description has to be assembled from the words of two neighboring categories, and it comes out as one of those plus a qualifier, which is precisely the shape that fails Grice's test for saying no more than is needed.

There is a real cost attached to sitting between categories, and it is not only in your head. Hsu published work in 2006 on films that span genres, finding that audiences rated the genre-spanners less highly and that they appealed to fewer people overall. The mechanism she describes has no hostility in it at all. An audience evaluates against the expectations of a category, and something belonging to two categories partly satisfies both sets of expectations and fully satisfies neither.

Take the limits seriously before you take the conclusion. That study is about films and the audiences that watch them, in one industry, with data on ratings and appeal rather than on conversations. Applying it to a person answering a question at a party is my extension and not her finding. What it establishes is narrower and still useful: the penalty for spanning categories is a real feature of how audiences work, rather than something you are imagining about yourself.

The tells for this version are clean. You can describe the work in detail for as long as anybody will listen. You have never once found a word that fits, and neither has anybody who has tried to help you. When somebody in the same world asks, you answer in three words with no difficulty at all. And you are not afraid of the question, only tired of it.

What this one needs has very little to do with a better sentence. Stop grading yourself on the absence of a word, and build a two-part answer instead: a near-enough category, then one concrete thing you actually did. That gives the listener a slot and a picture, which is what they were asking for in the first place.

## What If the Answer Would Commit You to Something?

Then the sentence is a promise rather than a description, and part of you is refusing to sign it, correctly.

Watch what a confident answer does. Say the compressed version out loud to seven people at a wedding and you have now told seven people what you are. Some of them will remember. One of them will ask you about it in eight months. The sentence has moved from being a thing you said into being a thing you are expected to keep being, and none of that was your intention when you were only trying to be polite.

For anybody whose thing is still uncertain, that is a real obligation being incurred at no obvious moment, and the reluctance to incur it reads as cowardice from outside while behaving like accounting from inside. Every clean answer is a small piece of borrowing against a future that has not arrived, and a person who has borrowed against a future once before and had to explain the shortfall afterward has excellent reasons for hesitating.

There is a second layer here that is worth knowing about and easy to overstate. Announcing an identity-relevant intention appears, in some research, to reduce the effort that follows it, because the announcement itself delivers part of what completing the thing was going to deliver. That work is attributed properly on [the page about researching instead of starting](/patterns/researching-instead-of-starting/), and I am not going to repeat the names here or lean on it harder than it deserves. Hold it as a mechanism to check against your own last three announcements rather than as a law.

The tells for this version are specific. The reluctance scales with how much the listener is likely to remember, so it is worst with people you will see again and mildest with strangers on a train. You can say the sentence easily in writing to somebody far away and not out loud to somebody near. And there is usually one previous occasion, which you can name inside about five seconds, where you told people something confidently and then had to explain what happened.

## What If the Answer Would Have to Be Defended?

Then the difficulty is in the room rather than in the words, and the sentence is being scored while it is being spoken.

This version behaves differently from the others in one checkable way: the answer exists. You could write it on a card. What you cannot do is say it to a particular person, or a particular type of person, because between the thought and the mouth there is a verdict being anticipated. Usually a face comes with it. A parent, a former colleague, an ex-partner, somebody successful in the old world, or a composite of everyone whose opinion has ever landed on you.

The tell that settles this one is the room test. If the identical sentence becomes easy the moment you imagine that the person in front of you will never think about you again, the difficulty sat in the anticipated response rather than in the wording, and that response is a separate object with a separate remedy.

What tends to arrive alongside it is a running commentary that gets in ahead of the listener. You say the answer and then immediately undercut it, with a small joke, a shrug, or a line about it being early days. That pre-emptive discount is doing something specific: it lowers the claim so nobody else has to. [The verdict you pass on yourself while trying to answer has its own page](/patterns/putting-myself-down/) and it goes further into where the habit comes from than there is room for here.

Two more tells. The stumble is far worse at family events than at professional ones, which is the opposite of what you would expect if the problem were about credibility. And you can hear yourself adjusting the answer to the person before you have finished the first clause, which is an ordinary thing that everybody does and which becomes expensive when the adjustment is downward every time.

## What If There Are Two True Answers and the Question Takes One?

Then you are performing a compression that genuinely loses information, and the loss is what you feel.

Some people are honestly doing two things. The work that pays and the work that is becoming. The role and the thing built at night. Two fields with nothing much in common, both real, both taking real hours. The question takes one word and you have two, so every answer you give is accurate and misleading at the same time, and you leave the conversation having said something true that produced a false impression.

That is a different problem from having no word, and it needs the opposite response. The person with no word needs a slot. The person with two needs permission to give one of them, on purpose, chosen by the room rather than decided again from scratch every time.

Be careful with a nearby page here, because the two get confused constantly. If what you have is two live options you might pursue and you cannot pick between them, that is a choice rather than a description, and [the page on not being able to decide](/patterns/not-being-able-to-decide/) owns it properly, including why more research never settles it. This is different. Nothing here needs choosing. Both things are already happening. What is needed is a sentence, and a sentence can only carry one of them at a time.

The tells: you have caught yourself giving different answers to different people in the same week and felt slightly dishonest about it. You can name both things instantly. And the discomfort arrives after the answer rather than during it, which is the reverse of the other three versions.

## What Do People Ask You Next, and What Does That Tell You?

More than any amount of introspection about the sentence, because the follow-up question is the listener reporting back what they received.

This is the cheapest diagnostic available and almost nobody uses it, because everybody is too busy replaying their own half of the exchange. Try it for two full weeks. After you answer, notice what the other person asks. There are only about five things they can ask, and each one points somewhere different.

| What they ask next | What they received | Which version this points at |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing, and the subject moves | A word they could file, or nothing to hold at all | Check whether they repeat it later; a filed answer comes back, an empty one does not |
| "What kind of thing?" | A wide-level word with no picture attached | The missing rung. There is no shared word yet |
| "So who do you do that for?" | A plan, and they are asking whether it is real | The tense mismatch, doing exactly what it does |
| "Oh, so is that like [wrong category]?" | Two categories at once, resolved by them into one | Spanning, and their guess tells you which neighbor is nearest |
| "Are you still doing [old answer] as well?" | An impression of two things running | The two true answers, honestly received |
| They tell you their own version of it | A story rather than a category | Usually fine, and worth noticing that the long answer did land with somebody |

Two notes on using that. The follow-up is information about the answer rather than a judgment of it, and reading it as a judgment is what stops people running this. And the row that surprises most people is the first one, because silence gets read as a failure and is usually the opposite: an answer that filed cleanly produces no follow-up at all, exactly like teacher does.

Run it for two weeks with a note on your phone and you will have between six and twenty rows. One row will repeat far more than the rest, and that is your version.

## Why Do People Ask This Question at All?

Because it is a routing device rather than an audit, and misreading that is where most of the private suffering comes from.

Think about what the asker is doing. They have thirty seconds of conversation to fill with somebody they have just met and no idea where to start. The question is a request for a direction to walk in. It is asked in the same spirit as asking where somebody grew up, and it is answered by most people with a word they have not thought about in years.

Almost nobody is evaluating the answer. What they are doing is looking for one hook, so that the next thing they say is about you rather than about the weather. That is why a plain answer with a picture attached works so much better than an impressive one: the picture is the hook. It is also why silence after your answer feels like a verdict and usually means the opposite, which is that they filed you and moved on to the next thing, exactly as happens after teacher.

The mismatch is worth naming precisely, because it is the whole reason this hurts more than it should. You are answering an examination question about the meaning and direction of your working life. They asked a routing question so they could keep talking. Both of you are being perfectly reasonable and only one of you is going to think about it in the car.

Which suggests a test you can run this week and nowhere else. Watch somebody else give a vague answer, and notice how much you think about it afterward. The honest result for nearly everybody is that they cannot recall it by the time they get to the drinks table, and that is the same attention the room is giving yours.

## Why Does the Advice to Write a Shorter Line Not Work?

Because all of it treats the sentence as the variable, and the sentence has never been the variable.

The standard advice in this territory is remarkably consistent. Write it shorter. Lead with the outcome. Say it out loud fifty times. Use the formula with the three blanks in it. Every one of those is a real technique that works on a real problem, and the real problem it works on is a person who knows exactly what they do and describes it badly. If that were your situation you would have solved this in an afternoon, which is roughly how long it takes.

There is a line that circulates alongside the advice, usually attributed to a famous physicist, saying that if you cannot explain something simply you do not understand it. It is worth knowing that nobody has ever found him saying it. The attribution appears decades after his death, in forms that keep changing, with no source anybody can produce, which puts it in the same family as most quotations that flatter a general point. The line is doing real damage, because it converts a missing shared word into a verdict about your understanding, and those are different objects with different remedies.

Two words are conspicuously absent from this page and their absence is deliberate rather than an oversight. There is a pair of terms that everybody reaches for the moment this comes up, both borrowed from marketing, both describing a market position rather than a person. I have kept them out on purpose. Reaching for either one moves the whole question from what is true about your week into how you should be presented, and that swap is the single fastest way to spend six months on the wrong problem. It also quietly imports an audience that has no business being in the room while you are still working out what is actually happening.

What does move, if the sentence itself does not, is the number of true present-tense facts available to you. That number grows through doing things and finishing them, not through wording. Which is a slower answer than the advice offers and it is the honest one.

## What Does the Story You Tell About Yourself Have to Do?

Two jobs at once, and it can only do the second one out loud in front of somebody.

Ibarra published a study in 1999 following professionals through periods of significant change in their working lives. What she described was people assembling and testing what she called provisional selves: watching other people, borrowing pieces, trying a version out, and keeping what survived contact. The important part for this page is that the trying happened in public. The versions were tested on other people rather than perfected privately first, and the ones that worked were kept because they worked in a room rather than because they read well.

She and a co-author extended the argument in 2010 to the story a person tells during a transition. The claim there is that a self-narrative in a period of change has to do two things at once: it has to be plausible to whoever is listening, and it has to be one the teller can actually believe. A story that satisfies only the first is a performance and it does not survive being asked a second question. A story that satisfies only the second is the forty-minute version you already have.

Take both of those as one line of evidence rather than two, because they share an author, and I would rather say that than let a reading list look longer than it is. And take the limits with them. That work is qualitative, on professionals in particular kinds of transition, with modest numbers and no measured effect anywhere in it. It supplies a shape people recognize, which is what I am using it for, and it does not establish that any of this works.

What it does supply is a correction to the private method. If a usable answer is built by testing versions on listeners, then the thirteen days you spent improving a phrase alone worked as a substitute for the work rather than as preparation for it. The first version you say to an actual person will be worse than the one in your notebook and it will teach you something the notebook cannot, which is what happens in the four seconds after you stop talking.

## Where Does This Hide When You Would Not Call It a Problem With a Sentence?

In several places that look like ordinary preferences, which is why it can run for years without ever being named.

**In being interested in other people.** You ask the first question, and the second, and the third, and people leave conversations with you feeling listened to, which is genuinely a good thing to be. It is also a complete defense against ever having to answer, and [the page on changing the subject](/patterns/changing-the-subject/) owns that move in its own right. The tell is whether you can remember the last time somebody got a full answer out of you.

**In arriving late and leaving early.** The question happens in the first ten minutes of a room and again at the door. A person who arrives while things are underway and leaves before the goodbyes has removed both occasions, and it reads as being busy.

**In the group you already know.** You go to the same three places with the same people, all of whom already know, so the question never comes up. This is comfortable, it is invisible, and it is the version with the largest cost, because a life narrowed to rooms where you are already understood is a life with fewer new people in it.

**In humor.** A deflecting joke about your own answer works every time, gets a laugh, and closes the subject faster than any sentence would. It is also the version most likely to be believed by the listener, which nobody expects.

**In work as the whole answer.** Talking about your old field with real fluency for ten minutes so that nobody notices you never answered the actual question. This one is nearly undetectable from outside and takes real effort, which is why it is exhausting.

**In writing rather than speaking.** You are excellent on a page and hopeless in a garden, so everything drifts toward written contact. That is a preference, and it is worth noticing when it becomes the only channel.

## What Does This Cost, and When Does the Bill Arrive?

Later than the behavior, in a currency that never gets connected back to it.

**The hours are the smallest part.** Two clear weeks lost to a phrase is annoying and recoverable. Repeat it four times a year for three years and the number gets large, and it still is not the main cost.

**The rooms get smaller.** This is where it actually gets expensive. Each avoided event is individually reasonable and the set of them is a shrinking life. Nobody notices a narrowing that happens one decline at a time, and the opportunities that never arrived leave no record of not arriving.

**The thing itself becomes less real.** Every time you give the old answer, the new thing spends another week existing only inside your own head. Work that has never been described to anybody out loud has a quality of provisionality about it that has nothing to do with how good it is, and it is hard to keep going on something nobody knows you are doing.

**People stop being able to help.** Almost every useful introduction in anybody's life comes from somebody who could describe them to a third person in one sentence. If nobody has that sentence, nobody can pass you on, and the absence shows up as a strange quietness rather than as a refusal.

**And the verdict grows.** After enough of these, the stumble stops being a thing that happened and becomes evidence in a case about yourself. That is the point at which the pattern stops needing any of its original causes, because the case is now doing the work on its own.

## Where Does This Stop and Something Else Begin?

Six neighboring patterns get folded into this one, and the wrong name sends you at the wrong work.

**Not knowing what you want at all.** If the blank is not about the sentence but about the wanting, so that a direct question about your own preference produces static in any format and at any length, [the page on finding yourself](/patterns/how-to-find-yourself/) owns that, including why solo trips and journals produce answers that sound right and change nothing. The two are separable in a minute. Somebody in this pattern can talk about their thing for an hour with obvious appetite. Somebody in that one cannot say what they want for lunch.

**Two live options you cannot choose between.** If both things are still only possibilities and the difficulty is picking, [the page on not being able to decide](/patterns/not-being-able-to-decide/) owns it. That page states its own scope plainly and its scope requires options. There are none in what is described here.

**The identity after something ended.** If the thing that used to answer the question has finished, the business sold, the role gone, the children grown, and what you are standing in is the absence of it, [that page](/patterns/who-am-i-without-the-thing-that-defined-me/) owns the arithmetic of what one arrangement was quietly carrying. It is the mirror image of this one. There, a real answer stopped being true. Here, one has not started being true yet.

**The verdict you pass on yourself while answering.** If the striking part is the automatic discount you attach to your own reply, [that page](/patterns/putting-myself-down/) owns it.

**Preparation standing in for beginning.** If the honest description of the last four months is research rather than work, [the page on researching instead of starting](/patterns/researching-instead-of-starting/) owns the open tabs, the courses and the reason the reading feels like progress.

**Releasing a finished thing.** If the work exists, is done, and will not go out, that is about the release rather than the description, and [that page](/patterns/why-i-cant-press-publish/) owns it.

Three more worth naming quickly. If the pattern is explaining far more than anybody asked for, in every direction, [that page](/patterns/over-explaining/) owns it and this page is a specific case of the opposite problem. If the recognizable thing is that your real life is permanently conditional on one more arrival, [the life you will start later](/patterns/the-life-i-will-start-later/) owns the deferral. And if what you are describing is a slow drift where nothing ended and you gradually stopped recognizing yourself, [that page](/patterns/i-dont-know-who-i-am-anymore/) owns it rather than this one.

## What Actually Moves This?

Four things, in this order, and the order matters more than any of them individually.

Nothing below is a validated protocol and anybody selling you one for this would be overselling it. It is worked out from how the failure happens, and it is built so that one person can start it this week without telling anybody or changing anything.

1. **Stop grading the sentence and start counting the exchanges.** Two weeks, a note on your phone, one line each time somebody asks: what you said, what they asked next, and what you did with the rest of the conversation. That is it. No improvement attempted. The counting is what turns a diffuse feeling into six rows you can read.
2. **Separate the two jobs the answer is doing.** One job is giving the listener a slot. The other is being true. Those come apart cleanly, and almost all the pain lives in trying to do both in one clause. A near-enough category, then one concrete thing you actually did last week, does both in two clauses and takes nine seconds.
3. **Choose your tense by room, once, in advance.** Decide now what you say to a stranger, what you say to somebody you will see again, and what you say to family. Three decisions, made calmly at a kitchen table, replacing a decision made under pressure forty times a year. Most of the stumble people hear is the deciding rather than the wording.
4. **Test versions on people, early and badly.** This is the part that cannot be skipped and the part everybody skips. Say the awkward version to four people this month and watch what comes back. A version that has never met a listener has not been tested, however good it looks in a notebook.

One thing that helps more than it should. Ask two people who already know you roughly what they say when they describe you to somebody else. Not what they think you should say. What they actually say. Most people find at least one of those answers is better than anything they have written, and it costs one message to find out.

And one thing to leave alone for now. Do not try to make the underlying thing describable by changing the underlying thing. Redesigning the work so that it fits a word is a real move and it is a large one, and making it while you are uncomfortable in a garden is the wrong reason and the wrong week.

## What Does the Settled Version Look Like on an Ordinary Week?

Considerably smaller than people expect, and it does not involve having found a brilliant line.

| The stuck version | The settled version | What that looks like on an ordinary Saturday |
|---|---|---|
| The answer gets built live, every time | The answer was decided in advance, by room | You hear yourself say it the same way twice and notice you did not think first |
| One sentence trying to be true and easy at once | Two clauses, one doing each job | A category, then one concrete thing you did, and the follow-up is about the concrete thing |
| The follow-up question is the bad part | The follow-up question is the useful part | Somebody asks who you do that for and you are pleased rather than caught |
| Vagueness followed by a change of subject | Vagueness noticed, and the subject left where it is | You say the short version and then simply stop talking |
| The old answer given by reflex | The old answer given on purpose, sometimes | You use it with a stranger on a train, deliberately, and feel nothing about it |
| A phrase improved privately for weeks | Versions tested on people within days | The wording changes because somebody looked confused, not because you reread it |
| Rooms avoided | Rooms attended with a decision already made | You go, the question comes, and it costs nine seconds |
| The stumble as evidence about you | The stumble as information about the room | You notice that it was easy at work and hard at the wedding, and know why |

Nothing in the middle column is impressive and that is the point. Almost nobody arrives at a beautiful line. What people arrive at is a serviceable one they are no longer frightened of, and the difference that makes to a year is out of all proportion to how ordinary it sounds.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Three different speeds, and running them together is why people give up in the third week.

The noticing moves first and it moves at the wrong end. For the first two solid weeks you catch it afterward, in the car, having already given the old answer. That feels like failure and it is the normal first stage. Within a month or so most people catch it during, which is a different experience and does not immediately change what comes out of your mouth.

A serviceable answer takes a couple of months and it takes contact with people to get there. Expect the first four attempts to be worse than the notebook version. Expect one of them to land oddly well and to have no idea why until you have said it twice more. The improvement comes from the repair rather than from the drafting.

The private verdict is the slowest of the three and it does not respond to a better answer. Somebody can have a clean sentence and still feel the old jolt for months, because the case was built over years and one good exchange does not close it. What tends to finally shift it is external and slightly undignified: hearing your own sentence come back at you from somebody who was not prompted, or having a stranger repeat it correctly to a third person while you are standing there.

And one honest thing about the underlying situation. If there genuinely is no shared word for what you are doing, that will not change on your schedule, and no amount of work on the sentence will invent one. What changes is that the absence stops being read as a fact about you.

## How Good Is the Evidence Behind Any of This?

Thinner than the confidence of most writing on the subject, and the thinness is worth mapping precisely rather than hidden.

**Nothing here was measured on this behavior.** There is no study of people failing to answer what they do at a party. Every piece of research on this page was done on something adjacent and is being carried across by me. That carrying is the argument, and you should weigh it as an argument rather than as a finding.

**The categories work is solid and it is about objects.** The preferred level of generality in ordinary naming has held up across decades of work. It was established on birds, furniture, tools and vehicles. A person is not a chair, and applying the same rung structure to how somebody describes their working life is an extension I am making on purpose and telling you about.

**The conversation work is philosophy.** Grice's expectations are a framework, not a result. They are enormously useful for describing why something felt wrong, and they predict nothing you could test in an afternoon. The field has spent fifty years arguing about whether the set should be four principles or one, which tells you how settled it is.

**The category-spanning penalty is real and narrow.** It is measured on products and audiences rather than on people and conversations, and the effect sizes belong to that setting. Take it as evidence that audiences behave this way in general, not as a measurement of what happened to you at the wedding.

**The transition work is qualitative and small.** Interviews and observation, on professionals, in particular kinds of change, with no measured outcome anywhere in it. Two papers with a shared author, which makes them one line of evidence rather than two.

**The four versions are mine and carry no research weight at all.** I have not offered a figure for how common each one is, because no such figure exists, and inventing one would be exactly the sort of thing this section is here to complain about.

**What survives all of that** is modest and checkable against your own last month. That ordinary talk runs on a preferred level of generality, and that a missing word at that level makes an exchange harder for both people. That a long true answer can break the ordinary expectations of conversation and land badly for reasons unrelated to its truth. That spanning two categories carries a real cost with audiences. And that a workable self-description gets built by testing versions on people rather than by drafting alone. Four things, none of them dramatic, and considerably more than the advice usually supplies.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From four strands of published work that anybody can check without going through me, and from a much larger amount of listening to people describe this in rooms.

On how categories sit at levels, Rosch and Mervis published the work on internal structure and basic-level categories in the mid nineteen-seventies, in *Cognitive Psychology*, establishing that one rung of generality is preferred in naming, learned first, and used fastest. The original studies used object categories. Everything I have done with it for occupational self-description is my extension of that finding.

On the expectations that make a true answer land badly, Grice set out the cooperative principle and its maxims in a paper delivered in 1967 and published in 1975 under the title *Logic and Conversation*. It is a philosophical framework rather than an experiment. It has been contested continuously since, including by researchers arguing that the maxims reduce to a single principle of relevance, and I have used it as a description people recognize rather than as a measured claim.

On the penalty for sitting between categories, Hsu published her study of genre-spanning films in *Administrative Science Quarterly* in 2006, reporting that films spanning genres received lower audience ratings and appealed to fewer people. That work is about products and market audiences. I have carried it to a person answering a question, and that carry is mine.

On how a workable self-description gets built during a change, Ibarra published the study of provisional selves in *Administrative Science Quarterly* in 1999, and extended the argument with a co-author in 2010 to the narratives people construct during major role transitions. Both are qualitative, both are on professionals, and they share an author, so they should be read as one line of evidence rather than as two independent ones.

Several things I have deliberately not done. I have not attached researchers' names to the work on how a speaker adjusts a description toward whoever is in front of them, which is directly relevant here and is already credited, with its researchers named, on [the page about agreeing when you disagree](/patterns/agreeing-when-you-disagree/). I have not attached names to the finding that a feeling of understanding collapses when somebody is made to produce the details, which is credited on [the page about being most attached to the people who hurt you](/patterns/most-attached-to-the-ones-who-hurt-me/). I have not attached names to the work on how psychological distance pushes a description upward into abstraction, which is credited on [the page about only working under pressure](/patterns/only-working-under-pressure/), or to the work on announcing an identity-relevant intention, credited on [the page about researching instead of starting](/patterns/researching-instead-of-starting/). Every one of those would have made this page look better sourced without making a single argument on it stronger, and the attribution is one click away in each case.

I also measured several unused researchers who would have fitted and left them alone, on the grounds that a fresh name is worth more to a site this size than a longer reading list.

The rest is mine and carries no research weight at all. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, I am not a therapist, and none of the researchers named above has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own reading of it disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.

One note on the first-person lines in the box near the top. They were said by real people in private conversations. All but one appear with identifying detail removed, with no name attached and no date, which is deliberate and is not going to change. The one carrying a name appears that way because that person gave recorded permission for it, and for no other reason.

## What Is One Thing to Do This Week, Before You Try to Write a Better Line?

Count the exchanges and change nothing at all.

Open a note on your phone. For the next two whole weeks, every time somebody asks what you do, write three things down afterward. What you said, as close to word for word as you can manage. What they asked next. And what you did with the rest of the conversation.

Do not improve anything while you are doing this. Do not resolve to have a better answer ready. Do not tell anybody you are running it, because announcing it turns the whole thing into something you are being watched on, which is the fastest way to stop recording honestly.

By the end you will have somewhere between six and twenty rows, and three things will be visible that no amount of thinking would have produced. The second column will repeat one question far more often than the rest, and that repetition points at which of the four versions is yours. The third column will show you how often you moved the subject, which is usually a number people find startling. And the first column will show you that you have been saying between three and five different things, none of them chosen, which is most of what the other person hears as hesitation.

If what landed hardest here was the missing word, the counting may be enough on its own and you can stop there with something useful. If what landed hardest was one of the other three, the room where it gets defended, the promise you are refusing to sign, or the two true answers you keep swapping between, then the thing worth working on sits underneath the sentence rather than inside it.

[Our Sacred Valley seminar](https://florianrolke.com/the-soulsearching-experience/) works at that layer. As Florian puts it: so if there is a certain behavior that you don't quite know what that is, nor can you explain it, that's why you come here. First we find what the stumble is actually doing for you, because something that reliable is never doing nothing. Then what stands in the way of the version where the question costs you nine seconds. Then a plan you are held accountable to afterward, back in your own week, at the same barbecues, with the same people asking. Everybody is screened before arriving, which keeps the group small enough that somebody who has spent two years giving the old answer gets worked with properly rather than handed a formula with three blanks in it.

One limitation worth stating plainly. Exact dates, the price and the final cap on group size are still being settled, so anybody comparing programs on those numbers this week cannot yet compare ours. Ask every program you are weighing the same questions, this one included, and decide from what comes back.

The fourteen-day count costs nothing and is worth having whichever way it comes out.
