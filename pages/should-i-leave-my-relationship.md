# How do I know whether to leave my relationship?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

You probably cannot know yet, and the reason is specific. A real decision needs evidence about what this relationship does when a complete, unhedged effort is aimed at what is actually missing, and almost nobody has generated it. What has been spent went into channels the other person was never running empty in. Generate the missing evidence first, then decide, and leaving afterward stays entirely legitimate.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
Some version of these has already gone through your head this month, probably at an hour you would rather not admit to.

- "I'm questioning it."
- "I want to be with my wife. I love my wife. But we don't have that many things in common."
- "I feel my husband doesn't really love me."
- "We had a fight about that about a year ago. It was a case of you will do something about this or else and we came to a compromise."
- "I thought I was so desperate that I thought my marriage was over, and I love my husband so much."

Carrying a decision this size unmade, for months, in private, has its own particular cost, and it is usually heavier than the decision itself.

It is somewhere after one in the morning and you are lying very still on your side of the bed, drafting the sentence. Not the argument, the sentence: the one you would use to tell your kids, or your mother, or the friend who has been waiting two years for you to say it, and which you have never once said out loud to anybody.

By Thursday you are making the same list again, pros on the left and cons on the right, in a fresh note on your phone because the old one felt contaminated. The columns come out almost exactly as they did in March.

Before anything else on this page: if there is violence in your home, if you are being threatened, or if you do not feel safe where you live, none of what follows applies to your situation. That belongs with a domestic violence hotline and with people who can help you leave safely, ahead of anything written here. The fuller version of that boundary sits a few paragraphs down.

This page answers the decision question only, and it hands off in three directions. If what you feel is the walls rather than the choice, [the trapped feeling has its own page and its own mechanics](/patterns/why-do-i-feel-trapped/). If the decision is already made and what you need is the next chapter, [rebuilding from the other side is covered separately](/patterns/how-to-start-over/). And if what you are struggling with is deciding anything at all, in any part of your life, [the general machinery of decisions lives here](/insights/how-to-make-a-decision/).

## Where Does This Stop Being a Decision and Start Being a Safety Question?

Right here, near the top, because this boundary matters more than everything below it put together.

Nothing on this page applies where there is violence, where there are threats, or where you are managing somebody's temper as a daily condition of living in your own home. The same holds for coercive control, which is often quieter and harder to name: your money controlled or monitored, your movements checked, decisions made about who you are allowed to see, warmth restored the moment you back down and withdrawn the moment you assert something, and a steady low-level insistence that you caused all of it. In that situation the honest advice is the opposite of everything below. Do not test the relationship. Contact a domestic violence hotline in your country, tell one person you trust in real life, and let them help you build a plan, since leaving is the most dangerous period and it goes better prepared. [The atmosphere of constant management has its own page](/patterns/walking-on-eggshells/), as does [the way responsibility gets quietly relocated onto you](/patterns/emotional-manipulation/).

The same applies from the inside. If you are carrying a depression that has not lifted for months, an addiction, a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, or any thought of harming yourself, however briefly it passes, bring in a licensed professional and put this decision on a shelf with a date on it. A depressive episode manufactures a felt certainty about hopelessness that is genuinely indistinguishable from an accurate verdict, and it aims that certainty at whatever is closest.

Everything after this section is built for the ordinary, painful, non-dangerous version: two people who both want it to work, or did once, and cannot work out why nothing they do lands.

## What Does This Actually Sound Like From the Inside?

Like two accurate reports arriving at the same time and refusing to be filed together.

The sentences below are how people say this when nobody is being careful. What matters is the small jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word, with no translation required. This first group belongs to the stuck part, the months or years before anything moves:

- "I'm split inside, one side of me is very afraid, and the other side really craves love."
- "It's not that I want a divorce, that's already done. I can't do anything about it."
- "All that time I thought it was him. He has the problem, all he cares about is work, and now I know that that's because that was one place he could go and be successful. I didn't understand that."

Notice what the third sentence is doing. It is a report from after, from somebody who had a complete and coherent account of her marriage that turned out to be built on one unexamined assumption. She had the hours right. What she had wrong was what the hours were for.

A second set comes from the far side, months later, and it is worth reading now rather than at the end:

- "I was very low on the list of priority, even though I would never admit it. But now it's completely switched around."
- "It's not that the marriage isn't gonna work, and it won't if she changes. It will work, but it's not anything she can do. It's something I have to do."

Neither of those is a happy ending you should expect. They are examples of what the question looks like once it has actually been answered, in one direction or the other, by something more reliable than a bad Tuesday.

## Why Doesn't the Answer Arrive, However Many Times You Ask?

Because you are trying to reach a verdict by thinking, on a body of evidence with a hole in the middle of it.

Everything you have available has already been examined, repeatedly. You know the history. You know what the arguments are about. You know which of your friends would say go and which would say stay. You have run the list four times and the columns keep coming out the same, which is the tell: more thinking is producing no new information because there is no new information to be had from that direction.

What is missing is one specific data point. Nobody in this relationship, including you, knows what happens when a genuinely complete, unhedged effort gets aimed at the actual deficit, held for a fixed period, and then checked with a direct question. That has almost certainly never been run. Effort has been enormous. Aim has been poor.

Being told the decision has not become available yet sounds like being told you may not have it, and it functions as the opposite. A verdict that felt permanent turns into a hold with a release condition you set yourself, on a date you choose, after which the same door stands exactly as open as it does tonight. Nobody is asking you to stay forever. The request is for six weeks of a different kind of trying, so that whatever you decide afterward is a decision rather than a guess made while exhausted.

## Why Does Real Effort Show Up as Nothing on the Other Side?

Because effort given and effort registered are separate currencies, tracked separately by each person, and almost never compared out loud.

Somebody can spend a decade of genuine, costly effort in one channel while the categories the other person experiences as empty receive none of it. That produces the strangest feature of a stuck relationship: one person is depleted and the other feels neglected, in the same week, about the same marriage, and both reports are completely accurate.

| What the effort looked like | What it genuinely supplied | What it never touched |
|---|---|---|
| Sixty-hour weeks, the mortgage covered, the family held up | Security, and a household that functions | Whether they feel significant to you in particular |
| Keeping strictly to the limit you agreed on a year ago | A rule you can both point at as honored | The need the fight was actually protecting |
| Being reliably pleasant, low-conflict, easy to live with | An absence of arguments | Whether anything between you is still growing |
| Doing whatever gets asked, promptly, without complaint | Compliance | Whether they feel chosen rather than managed |
| Long, serious conversations about the relationship | Information about the problem | Whether they felt loved on an ordinary Wednesday |
| Booking the holiday, planning the anniversary, making the effort visible | Evidence of intent | Whether they were actually met while it was happening |

Read the left column and notice how expensive it all is. None of that is laziness and none of it is fake. It is a large amount of real spending, delivered into an account that was already full, while two other accounts sat at zero the entire time.

The reason it stays invisible is that people reach for more of what they are already good at. The provider provides harder. The peacemaker gets more agreeable. The one who talks things through schedules another conversation. Each of them is doubling down in the channel where they are strong, which feels like maximum effort from inside and reads as absence from outside.

## What Would You Score, Sitting in Their Chair?

Considerably lower than you expect in two categories, and surprisingly high in one.

Here is the exercise that relocates a vague sense that we do not work into two or three specific, addressable gaps. Take the six drivers everybody runs on, [set out in full on their own page](/patterns/six-human-needs/), and score them one at a time. Not for yourself. For your partner, from inside their seat, honestly, as they would answer if nobody was listening.

| The category | The question, asked from their chair | What a zero sounds like at home |
|---|---|---|
| Certainty | How safe and settled do they feel about us, week to week? | "I never know which version of you is walking in the door." |
| Variety | How much aliveness, surprise, or play is there in this? | "We have had the same evening four thousand times." |
| Significance | How much do they feel they matter to you specifically? | "I could be anyone. You would still come home and open the laptop." |
| Love and connection | How loved do they feel, as opposed to well managed? | "You do everything right and I still feel like a logistics problem." |
| Growth | Is either of them getting bigger inside this? | "I have not learned anything about you in six years." |
| Contribution | Do they get to give something that lands? | "Nothing I do for you seems to be needed." |

Score each one out of ten and look at the shape rather than the total. The profile that comes back has a shape to it, usually high in exactly the category you have been spending in and close to zero in two others, which is the entire diagnosis in one picture.

If the scoring feels impossible because you genuinely do not know, that is information too, and there is a shortcut. Think of the last time you personally felt fully alive, or fully wanted by somebody, and hold onto how it sat in your body. Then ask when your partner last had that, from you, and whether they ever have. Most people find the honest answer is further back than they were expecting.

## What Is the Difference Between Settling a Rule and Meeting a Need?

A rule can be honored perfectly for a year while the thing that produced the fight goes completely unaddressed.

The shape is always the same. A recurring conflict reaches a crisis, somebody says a version of you will do something about this or else, and the two of you negotiate a limit: three evenings a month, a spending cap, a curfew, a phone rule, a fixed number of nights the in-laws can stay. Both people leave the conversation relieved. An agreement exists. It can be checked.

Twelve months later the same resentment is back, on time, and it reads as a new problem. It is the old one wearing a different fight, because the argument was never actually about the number of evenings. The number was standing in for something: feeling deprioritized, feeling unchosen, feeling like the least interesting thing in the house. Compliance with the rule leaves every bit of that intact, which is why somebody can hold to an agreement scrupulously and be no more loved for it.

The question that opens this back up takes one sentence. What would have been satisfied if the compromise had worked exactly as we intended? The answer that comes back is always something other than the rule: a specific, nameable thing, and once it is named it becomes something you can actually aim at instead of renegotiating the terms for another year. [Resentment that has been accumulating quietly through all of this has its own page](/patterns/resentment/), and it is worth reading if the word lands.

## Is Being This Tired the Same as Having Tried Everything?

No, and the two feel identical from inside, which is the problem.

Exhaustion from a long unresolved conflict is real, it is not imagined, and it is not evidence about whether the relationship works. What it reliably measures is how much you have spent and for how long. It says nothing whatsoever about where the spending landed, and that is the only variable that matters here.

So there are two very different findings that produce the same feeling in the body. One is I have done everything available and it still does not work, which is a conclusion. The other is I have been giving hard, in the wrong channel, for eleven years, which is a diagnosis with an obvious next step. Nobody can tell those apart by consulting how tired they feel, and almost everybody tries.

Deciding from inside that state has a further problem. A crisis-state account of a person is built entirely out of how they behave during the worst recent fight, which is the least representative sample of them available. Ask separately what they are like outside of stress, and whether you have seen that version of them in the last three months. If the answer is that you cannot remember, the question worth asking is what has been happening to both of you rather than who they turned out to be.

## Why Are the Years Already Spent Pulling So Hard on the Decision?

Because how much is invested is a separate variable from how good it is, and it moves the decision independently.

This is one of the better documented findings in relationship research. Whether somebody stays or goes tracks three things that vary independently, so a person can be genuinely unhappy and completely immovable without any personal weakness being involved.

| The variable | What it actually measures | What it tells you about leaving |
|---|---|---|
| Satisfaction | How this compares with what you expected a relationship to be | One third of the answer, and the only third most people consult |
| Investment size | Years, a house, children, in-laws, shared friends, a private vocabulary, a version of yourself that only exists here | Nothing about whether it works. Almost everything about why leaving feels physically impossible |
| Quality of alternatives | What you believe is available otherwise, including being alone | Usually the most distorted of the three, in both directions |

Naming which one is holding you takes the moral weight out of it. Feeling unable to leave a relationship you are unhappy in stops looking like cowardice and starts looking like arithmetic, which is a considerably better thing to work with.

There is a matching trap on the other side, and it catches the people who pride themselves on being decisive. The years already spent are not a reason to stay, and the wish to prove they were not wasted is not a reason to go. Both are the same error running in opposite directions: treating what has already been spent as information about what comes next, when it is only information about what came before.

## Can You Love Someone and Still Want Out?

Yes, and this is one of the most common shapes in the whole territory rather than a contradiction that needs resolving.

"I want to be with my wife. I love my wife. But we don't have that many things in common." Somebody said that in the same breath, meaning every part of it. It sounds incoherent for about a second, until you separate love into the parts it actually has. Intimacy, the closeness and the knowing. Passion, the wanting and the aliveness. Commitment, the decision to stay and keep deciding it. Those three vary independently, which means a relationship can run high on two and near zero on the third for years.

A marriage with deep intimacy and unshakeable commitment and no passion left has a name in the research literature, and people in one are describing something real when they say they love their partner. They are accurately reporting two components while missing a third. That is a specific finding with a specific target, and it is much more workable than the global verdict of we have grown apart.

So the useful question shifts. Instead of do I still love them, which produces a fog, ask which part is missing and how long it has been missing. Somebody who has lost the wanting and kept the closeness is in a different situation from somebody who has kept the wanting and lost any sense of being known. [Where one person has gone quietly unreachable, that has its own page](/patterns/emotionally-unavailable/).

## Which Marker Is Worth More Than How Bad the Last Fight Felt?

Contempt. It outperforms almost every other signal, and how often you argue is close to useless by comparison.

Decades of longitudinal research on couples converged on four communication patterns that predict breakdown: criticism aimed at the person rather than the behavior, defensiveness, stonewalling, and contempt. Of the four, contempt has consistently been identified as the strongest predictor of eventual separation. It is worth being concrete about what it looks like, since people hear the word and picture screaming. It is quieter than that. Eye-rolling. Mockery of the way they talk. Sarcasm delivered from slightly above. Talking about them to a friend in a register you would never use to their face. A small, settled sense that you are the more evolved person in the house.

One honest caveat, because this gets oversold badly. The famous prediction percentages attached to that work have been criticized for being fitted to the same couples they were derived from, which is a real methodological objection and it is why you should treat any confident number you read as loose. The behavioral marker itself has held up much better than the arithmetic around it. Watch for contempt, in both directions, including your own.

Two people can fight loudly every week, repair afterward, and be fine. Two people who have stopped fighting entirely and are quietly certain of their own superiority are usually further along. If you recognize the contempt as running in you, that is worth knowing before you decide anything, because it travels.

## Does the Pattern Belong to This Relationship, or Does It Travel?

Check the base rate, because this is the single question that separates a verdict about a couple from a verdict about a person.

The shape looks like this. Something recurs: one of you shuts down, withdraws, disappears into work, or gets suddenly unreachable at precisely the moment things start getting close. Inside one relationship, that reads as unambiguous evidence about this relationship, because it is the only one either of you can observe from the inside.

The check takes about twenty minutes of honest memory. Did the same thing happen in the relationship before this one? At roughly the same depth of closeness? And the one before that? A pattern that appears at the same point regardless of who the partner is belongs to a person, not to a match. That relocation carries no excuse with it, and it changes what you are dealing with completely, because a portable pattern is something to work on and a bad match is something to leave.

Naming it out loud is often what does the work. One person describing, plainly, that this happens at month seven of everything he has ever been in, turns a two-year argument about the relationship into a single sentence about himself. [Why the same shape keeps reappearing across different people is worth settling separately](/insights/why-do-i-attract-the-same-people/), and [the pattern of pulling back exactly when closeness arrives has its own page](/patterns/fear-of-intimacy/).

## What Exactly Would End if This Ended?

Two separable things, felt as one, which is a large part of why the decision will not resolve.

There is the relationship: whether love and connection are actually present between two people. And there is the institution: the legal status, the shared address, the family recognition, the photographs on other people's walls, your place at somebody's Christmas table, the way your name is said in your parents' town. Losing the second does not retroactively delete the first. Somebody can keep the institution intact for a decade after the relationship inside it stopped, and somebody else can lose the institution while what was real about it stays real.

This matters most where an ending carries genuine social weight: a religious family, a small community, a family business, a culture where the word for what you are about to do lands heavily. There, the two get treated as one thing, and the fear of being seen to have failed gets felt as grief about the relationship. They are separate sources of pain and they respond to completely different things.

The tell is concealment. Somebody who has functionally already decided, has perhaps already signed something, and has told nobody, is usually managing shame rather than managing a decision. "It's not that I want a divorce, that's already done. I can't do anything about it." The practical ending and the emotional one are running on separate timelines, and telling one person you trust is what lets the second one start.

## What Is a Bounded Total-Effort Trial?

A short, fixed window during which the effort is complete and aimed only at the categories that came back empty, ending on a date you set in advance with a direct conversation.

Four features make it work, and dropping any one of them turns it back into what you have already been doing.

**It is bounded.** A window you choose before you start, written down. Four weeks, six weeks, thirty days. The length matters less than the fact that it ends, because an open-ended promise to try harder is what the last three years already were.

**It is total.** No twenty percent held back as insurance against being hurt. The hedge is the single most common reason a trial returns a meaningless result, since a half-effort that fails proves nothing about a full one.

**It is aimed.** At the two lowest-scoring categories from the exercise above, not at the channel where you are already strong. That is what makes it uncomfortable, and the discomfort is the point rather than a warning sign.

**It is one-sided on purpose.** You run it whether or not they participate, and you do not announce it as a test. Saying this is your last chance converts the whole thing into an ultimatum, which is the exact move that produces a rule instead of a met need. Saying I am going to try something different for the next six weeks is enough.

Nothing about this is a validated protocol and anybody selling you one for a decision this size is overselling something. It is worked out from how the failure actually runs, and it exists to generate one missing piece of evidence.

## How Do You Run One Without It Turning Into Another Year of Waiting?

By deciding the specifics in advance, in writing, tonight, before anything about your partner changes.

1. **Set the end date and write it down.** In your calendar, with a two-word label only you understand. A trial without a date is a mood.
2. **Score the six from their chair.** Twenty minutes, on paper, honestly. Pick the two lowest.
3. **Convert each one into a weekly behavior, not an intention.** Feeling significant becomes a specific hour, phone in another room, where the only subject is them. Growth becomes one genuinely new thing done together per week. Vagueness here guarantees a null result.
4. **Stop the audit for the duration.** This is the hardest instruction and the one that decides everything. For the whole window, you stop keeping the ledger of what you are not receiving. If the giving is running on the hope of getting more back, both the withholding and the generosity are organized around the same question, and neither one moves anything.
5. **Expect the discomfort and do not read it as failure.** Real effort in an unfamiliar channel feels awkward and slightly fraudulent. More of what you are already good at never feels like that, which is exactly why it is the easier thing to reach for.
6. **Renegotiate nothing while it runs.** No new rules, no new agreements, no bargaining. The trial is a test of supply, and adding terms contaminates it.
7. **Keep a two-line log each evening.** What you gave, and which category it was aimed at. Nothing about how they responded. Tracking their response reopens the audit through the back door.
8. **Have the conversation on the date, not before.** Especially during the week that feels like it is obviously working, and especially during the week that feels like it obviously is not.

Is this easy? No, and the specific difficulty is worth naming. It requires you to give generously to somebody you are currently resentful of, for weeks, with no guarantee, while a large part of you is arguing that they should go first. That part has a case. Running the trial anyway is the only version of this that a single person can do alone, without needing their partner to agree to anything.

## What Do You Actually Say at the Checkpoint?

Something direct and specific enough that a polite answer becomes impossible.

The checkpoint conversation is harder than the crisis conversation you are having now, precisely because this one counts. A vague version of it, how are we doing, do you feel better about things, produces a vague answer and wastes the whole six weeks. Three questions, asked plainly, in this order:

1. "For the last six weeks I have been trying to do something specific. I have been trying to make you feel more like the most important thing in the house, and to get us doing something new together. Did any of that actually land?"
2. "What is still missing that I have not touched?"
3. "If nothing changes from here, what do you want to do?"

Two failure modes will try to hijack this. The first is presenting a bill: after everything I have done for you these six weeks. That turns evidence-gathering into a demand for payment and you will get compliance rather than truth. The second is accepting a kind answer. Yes, it has been better, is not an answer. What specifically was better, and is it enough, is.

Then leave a week between the conversation and the decision. Whatever you hear will land differently on the following Tuesday, and a decision this size deserves to be made twice, on two different days, before it counts.

## What Happens If the Trial Comes Back Negative?

You leave, with something you did not have before, which is an answer instead of a suspicion.

Say this plainly, since the mechanism gets misread as a technique for saving marriages. It exists to generate the evidence a leave-or-stay decision actually requires, and leaving after a real trial is exactly as legitimate an outcome as staying. Six weeks of complete, aimed effort that closes nothing tells you something no amount of thinking could: that the gap is not a supply problem you can solve from your side.

The practical difference shows up years later. People who leave from inside exhaustion tend to re-litigate the decision privately for a decade, because some part of them knows the case was never properly made. People who leave after running something like this tend to grieve it hard and then stop arguing with themselves. The relationship still ends. What changes is whether you spend the following years wondering.

A third outcome catches people off guard and it is worth preparing for. Sometimes the trial closes the gap and reveals that what was blocked was mostly on your side, which is disorienting rather than triumphant. One man's version of it: "It's not that the marriage isn't gonna work, and it won't if she changes. It will work, but it's not anything she can do. It's something I have to do." That is a harder finding than being wronged, and it is the one that actually changes a life.

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because six different situations produce almost identical sentences at one in the morning.

| The sentence you keep saying | What it might actually be | The thing that separates them |
|---|---|---|
| "We have nothing in common anymore" | One component of love missing while the other two hold | Whether you would still choose their company during a good week |
| "I have tried everything" | Enormous spending in one channel | Score the six from their chair and look at the shape of the profile |
| "I can't leave" | Investment size, doing its documented work | Whether your reasons describe the relationship or describe what unwinding it would cost |
| "I only feel free when I am away from them" | Relief from one specific starved category | Whether the relief also shows up in weeks when that category got fed at home |
| "They pull away every time we get close" | A pattern that travels with a person | Whether it happened at the same point in their previous relationships |
| "I feel nothing at all anymore" | Numbness that predates this relationship | Whether anything else in your life still registers |
| "I am staying for the children" | Sometimes true, sometimes investment size in a respectable coat | What you would say if nobody at all would judge either answer |

None of these get settled by how the relationship looks from outside, and none of them get settled by how it felt during the last fight. The information sits in what happens after a specific, deliberate change in supply, which is why the honest test cannot be run in your head.

## What Does the Other End of This Look Like?

Ordinary, mostly, and considerably less dramatic than either version you have been imagining at night.

The relationships that survive this rarely get transformed. What changes is that two or three specific things start being supplied that were not being supplied before, and the global sense of doom quietly loses its material. One woman's description of the shift, after years of assuming she came last: "I was very low on the list of priority, even though I would never admit it. But now it's completely switched around." Nothing about that sentence is euphoric. It is somebody noticing that a specific account went from empty to full.

The ones that end after a real trial look different too. The leaving is slower, sadder, and much less angry, because there is nothing left to prove and nobody left to convict. People in that position describe being able to say what happened in two sentences without needing the other person to agree with them, which is not something the exhausted version of leaving ever produces.

And the recognition itself is often the largest single moment. "All that time I thought it was him. He has the problem, all he cares about is work, and now I know that that's because that was one place he could go and be successful. I didn't understand that." Whatever she decided afterward, she decided it about her actual husband rather than about the version she had assembled over eleven years. That is what all of this is for.

Do not judge yourself if the middle of the trial feels worse than the crisis did. Giving generously while resentful is genuinely hard, and the discomfort is usually the work happening rather than the work failing.

## How Long Does This Honestly Take?

Weeks for the trial, one conversation for the checkpoint, and a week after that before the decision counts.

The scoring exercise takes an evening. The trial itself needs real, sustained days rather than one large gesture, which is why four to six weeks is the usual shape: long enough that a run of good days cannot fake it, short enough that you can actually sustain complete effort without collapsing halfway. A weekend proves nothing in either direction. A year is not a trial, it is what you have already been doing.

The checkpoint is one conversation, and it will take about forty minutes if you let it. Then a week of sitting with what you heard, because the answer that arrives on the night is not the same as the answer that is still there the following Tuesday.

A different timeline applies if the base-rate check came back positive and the pattern is portable. Working on something that has run in you since your twenties takes months to years of repetitions rather than six weeks, and it happens in a relationship or out of one. That is worth knowing before you conclude that leaving resolves it, since a portable pattern packs and comes along.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From four bodies of public work, none of them mine, all of them checkable without going through me.

Caryl Rusbult developed the Investment Model of commitment, first published in 1980 and extended over the following two decades, building on the interdependence theory of Harold Kelley and John Thibaut. It is the source of the three-variable structure used above, and the Investment Model Scale that Rusbult published with Christopher Agnew and John Martz in 1998 is the version most often used in research since. John Gottman and colleagues ran the longitudinal couples research that produced the four communication patterns described in this page, with contempt identified as the strongest single predictor of separation, though the widely quoted prediction accuracy figures from that work have drawn serious methodological criticism for being fitted to the same samples they were derived from. Robert Sternberg set out the triangular theory of love in a 1986 paper in *Psychological Review*, separating intimacy, passion, and commitment into components that vary independently. The sunk cost trap comes out of behavioral economics, described formally by Richard Thaler around 1980 and resting on the loss aversion work of Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, with Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer's 1985 experiments giving it its clearest demonstration.

The six-category scoring exercise is common currency in the personal development field and carries no peer-reviewed standing of its own. Treat it as a useful map rather than a finding.

The rest of this is mine and carries no research weight at all. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, I am not a therapist or a counselor, and none of the researchers named above has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Decide Anything?

Take one sheet of paper and turn it sideways. On the left, list everything you have actually done for this relationship in the past year, as concrete acts rather than intentions: the hours worked, the rule you kept, the holiday you booked, the argument you swallowed, the thing you gave up. Twelve to twenty lines, no editorializing. Then, next to each one, write which of the six it fed, from their side of the table rather than yours. Certainty, variety, significance, love, growth, or contribution.

Most people find the entire left column collapses into one or two columns on the right, and the two that stay empty are the two the fight has been about the whole time. That takes ten minutes and a pen, it requires nothing from your partner, and it is worth doing tonight whether or not you ever run a trial, book anything, or tell anybody you were considering this.
