- q: "Why do I feel like everyone is watching me?"
  a: "Because two separate things are happening at once and they get experienced as one. Your equipment for detecting attention is working, which is ordinary and useful. On top of that sits an estimate of how much attention is actually pointed at you, and that estimate runs high in almost everybody, by roughly a factor of two in the studies that have measured it. The sensation is real. The number attached to it is a guess, and it is a guess you can check."
- q: "What is the spotlight effect?"
  a: "The name given to a measured gap between how much attention people believe they are receiving and how much they actually receive. In the best known demonstration, students who walked into a room wearing a shirt they found embarrassing predicted that about twice as many people had noticed it as actually had. Follow-up variants found the same shape for what people say in group discussions and for how they think their performance is being tracked."
- q: "Is the spotlight effect actually true?"
  a: "The gap is real and the popular retelling of it is not. The finding is that estimates run roughly double the actual number, which is a big and useful error. The version you meet online says nobody noticed you at all, and the original numbers say something closer to a quarter of the room did. Those are different claims. The second one is the one supported, and it is also the one that survives contact with your own experience."
- q: "What is the illusion of transparency?"
  a: "The tendency to overestimate how visible your internal state is from outside. You feel your nervousness at full volume, continuously, from the inside, and you cannot get far enough away from that experience to picture how thin the outside version is. This is why people are so often told they seemed calm during something they remember as visibly coming apart, and why they assume the compliment is politeness rather than a report."
- q: "What is the difference between private and public self-consciousness?"
  a: "Private self-consciousness is attention to your own thoughts, moods and sensations. Public self-consciousness is awareness of yourself as an object other people are looking at. Researchers separated the two in the 1970s and built a questionnaire to measure them, and people differ stably on both. The second one is what this page is about. Being high on it describes a difference in where attention rests rather than a fault in the person."
- q: "Is self-consciousness a diagnosis?"
  a: "No. It is a plain description of where attention is pointed, in the same family as being distracted or being alert. There is no threshold above which a person becomes a case and no instrument that returns a verdict. What exists is a self-report questionnaire that places people on a continuum, whose internal structure researchers have argued about for four decades. A questionnaire that sorts people along a line is not the same object as a category."
- q: "Why does my body react before I have a thought about it?"
  a: "Because detecting attention is fast and cheap, and evaluating it is slow and expensive. Your visual system treats a face turned toward you as worth flagging immediately, and it does that flagging before anything you would recognize as a decision. That is why the heat in the face and the change in the walk arrive first and the explanation arrives second. The order of events matters, because only the second part is available to you."
- q: "Why do I lose track of how to walk across a room?"
  a: "Because walking runs perfectly well as an automatic sequence and badly as a monitored one. The moment attention lands on the arms, the arms become a thing to decide about, and there is no correct answer available at the speed the question is being asked. This is the same reason a familiar word looks misspelled if you stare at it. Nothing broke. A process that runs on its own got asked to run under supervision."
- q: "Why does a group laughing feel like it is about me?"
  a: "Because a laugh you cannot source is ambiguous, and ambiguous social signals get resolved toward yourself. Your own recent behavior is the most available material you have, so it gets offered first as the explanation. The certainty that follows is a feeling of certainty rather than evidence of one. In practice, the number of times a burst of laughter in a room turns out to concern any specific person in it is very low."
- q: "Why do I replay a small moment for days when nobody else remembers it?"
  a: "Because you were the only person in the room for whom that moment was about the most important subject available. Everybody else filed it against a day with a hundred other things in it. You filed it against yourself. The asymmetry of storage is the whole explanation, and it is why the exchange you are still holding at half past one had genuinely stopped existing for the other person before their bus arrived."
- q: "Why is eating alone in public so uncomfortable?"
  a: "Because there is no task to hold your attention outward, so it turns around. Eating alone gives you nothing to look at, nobody to talk to and no reason to move, which leaves attention with only one available target. The narrated feeling that follows, the sense of being a scene somebody is describing, comes from you supplying the commentary rather than from anybody in the room writing one."
- q: "Does anyone actually notice when I get something wrong?"
  a: "Some people do, which is the honest answer and the more useful one. The research says the number is far lower than you predict and higher than zero. Roughly a quarter of a room noticed the embarrassing shirt in the study everybody quotes. The reassurance that nobody saw anything is both false and easy to disprove from your own memory, which is exactly why it never holds for more than a minute."
- q: "Why does being told nobody is thinking about you make it worse?"
  a: "Because it is a claim about other people's inner lives that you have no way to verify, delivered to somebody whose entire difficulty is an unverifiable claim about other people's inner lives. It also happens to be false in the strict form it is usually given. What helps is a number you obtained yourself, from your own week, which is a different kind of thing from a better assurance."
- q: "What check can I actually run on this?"
  a: "Write your prediction down before the event: how many people you expect to notice the specific thing, and what you expect them to be able to say about it tomorrow. Then get one actual number, either by asking one person a narrow question afterward or by testing your own recall of everybody else in the room yesterday. The gap between the two figures is the measurement, and it is yours rather than mine."
- q: "How long does the check take?"
  a: "About four minutes across a week, and the results usually show up inside three attempts. Two lines written before a meeting, one line written after. The reason it works faster than most self-directed exercises is that it produces evidence rather than a resolution. You are not trying to believe something different. You are collecting numbers about a claim you already hold, which is a much smaller ask."
- q: "Why do affirmations not touch this?"
  a: "Because the belief in question is an estimate about other people, and an estimate does not move in response to a sentence about yourself. Telling yourself that you are confident leaves the guess about how many people are looking exactly where it was. The estimate moves when it meets a contradicting number, which is why the check on this page is arithmetic rather than encouragement."
- q: "Should I deliberately do embarrassing things to get over it?"
  a: "That approach gets recommended constantly here and this page does not recommend it. Manufactured embarrassment produces a story about the day you lay down in a train station and very little else, because the situation was invented and your nervous system knows it. The scenes that actually cost you are ordinary ones you did not design. Those are the ones worth measuring, and measuring them requires nothing dramatic."
- q: "Why does leaving the room not stop the feeling?"
  a: "Because for a good number of people the observer travels. If the judging voice is yours, the people in the room were the screen it was playing on, and removing the screen leaves the projector running. The tell is simple: notice whether the commentary continues in an empty flat, in the car, in the shower. Where it does, no amount of managing rooms will reach it, because the room was never the source."
- q: "Is it possible that people really are watching me?"
  a: "Yes, and any page that skips this is lying to you. Being new, being the only person of your description in the room, having a visible difference, holding a role that draws attention, or living somewhere small all produce genuine, sustained observation. In those settings the feeling is a correct reading of the situation. The useful question stops being whether it is real and becomes what it is costing you to carry it."
- q: "Why does it get worse in some rooms than others?"
  a: "Because rooms differ in how much they hand attention to you and how little they give you to do. Arriving late, walking a long open floor, standing while others sit, being introduced, eating alone and waiting with nothing in your hands all point attention inward with nothing to compete against. The same people in the same building cost you almost nothing when there is a task in front of you."
- q: "Does being watched actually change how people behave?"
  a: "Less reliably than the famous studies suggested. A widely repeated experiment found that a picture of eyes above an honesty box increased payments, and the finding traveled fast. Two meta-analyses published in 2017 pooled the accumulated studies and found no evidence that artificial surveillance cues increase generosity overall. The everyday version, where a real person is genuinely present, holds up better than the poster version does."
- q: "Why do I feel like there is a camera behind me?"
  a: "Because you are running a model of how you look from outside, and a model like that needs a viewpoint. The one people report most often sits behind and slightly above, which is roughly where a camera would go. That construction is ordinary imagination doing its job. What makes it expensive is treating the footage it produces as a record of what other people saw rather than as something you generated."
- q: "Is feeling watched the same as caring what people think?"
  a: "They sit next to each other and they are separable. Caring what people think is a stance toward opinion, and it runs in the days before and after. Feeling watched is a sensation in the body that arrives inside a specific room, often about nothing anybody has an opinion on, like the way you are holding a tray. Plenty of people carry one heavily and the other hardly at all."
- q: "Why did this get worse recently?"
  a: "Usually because something changed about how much genuine attention is pointed at you, rather than about you. A new job, a promotion into a visible role, a move to a smaller place, a change in appearance, or a period where you were actually being assessed will all raise the real number. If it arrived suddenly, alongside physical symptoms, or without any change in your circumstances, that belongs with a doctor rather than a page about a pattern."
- q: "Does the feeling ever go away completely?"
  a: "The sensation does not, and a page promising otherwise is selling something. What changes is the size of the estimate riding on top of it and how much of your behavior that estimate gets to decide. People who have worked on this still feel the room when they walk in late. They have simply stopped treating the feeling as a report on how many people looked up."
- q: "How do I tell ordinary social awareness apart from the error?"
  a: "By whether a number is attached. Ordinary awareness registers that you are among people and then releases, and it produces no specific claim. The error produces a figure: most of them, all of them, everybody at that end of the table. Anything with a quantity in it is an estimate, and an estimate can be checked. Anything without one is your equipment doing what it is supposed to do."
- q: "Why does knowing about the spotlight effect not fix it?"
  a: "Because the knowing sits in one place and the estimate sits in another. You can hold the research accurately and still feel eleven people turn as you walk in, for the same reason that knowing how a visual illusion works does not make the lines look equal. Reading changes what you can explain afterward. Only evidence you gathered yourself moves the number you carry in."
- q: "What if I was genuinely singled out once?"
  a: "Then the alertness you are carrying was earned in a setting where it was accurate, and it has outlived the setting. This is the version most often mistaken for oversensitivity, by the person carrying it more than by anybody else. What it usually needs is not a correction to the estimate but an update to which rooms it applies in, and that is slower work than the check on this page."
- q: "What does the settled version look like?"
  a: "Fairly undramatic. You still feel the room. You still notice the walk. What has changed is that the feeling stops producing a headcount, and the headcount was what used to decide whether you spoke, where you sat, and whether you went at all. People describe getting the last twenty minutes of an evening back rather than becoming someone unbothered by rooms."
- q: "What is worth doing this week?"
  a: "One prediction and one number, three times. Before an ordinary event, write down how many people you expect to notice the specific thing you are worried about. Afterward, get an actual figure, from one narrow question or from your own recall of everyone else there. Change nothing else, avoid nothing, and try no technique. Three pairs of numbers is enough to see the size of your own error."

# Why do I feel like everyone is watching me?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because two things arrive together and get felt as one. Your equipment for detecting attention is working, which is ordinary. Riding on top of it is an estimate of how much attention is pointed at you, and that estimate runs roughly double the real figure in almost everybody. The sensation is accurate. The number attached to it is a guess you can check.
</div>

You are walking the length of an open floor.

Twenty desks, most of them occupied, a printer at the far end that you may or may not actually need. It is about eleven steps. Somewhere around step three your arms stop working. Not literally. They carry on doing whatever arms do, and you become aware of them, and awareness turns out to be the exact thing that arms cannot survive. You try letting them hang. That looks wrong. You try putting your hands in your pockets, which feels like a decision somebody could see you making. By step seven you have arrived at a compromise that satisfies nobody, including you, and by step nine you have realized you are also thinking about your face.

You get to the printer. Nothing happens. Nothing was ever going to happen. And on the walk back you do the whole thing again in reverse.

Then there is the counter.

You are third in a queue and you have three words to say when you get to the front. You know the words. You have said them hundreds of times. And you are running them anyway, silently, in order, checking the ordering, adjusting the emphasis on the middle one, and preparing a version for the possibility that they ask you something back. When your turn comes it takes four seconds and goes exactly as it has always gone, and you rehearsed it for ninety.

Then there is the laugh.

A burst of laughter goes up somewhere behind you, from a group you are not part of, about something you did not hear. And you know. Not suspect, not wonder about. You know, with a physical certainty that arrives in the chest before any thought does, that it had you in it. Nothing in the room supports that. You cannot even see who laughed. And the certainty is complete.

Then there is half past one in the morning.

You are awake, and you are holding a thirty second exchange from an afternoon three days ago, in which you said something slightly wrong and somebody's face did something that might have been nothing. You have rerun it enough times that the version you are watching is no longer the original. The other person put it down before they got to the car park and has not touched it since. You have been carrying it for seventy-two hours and it is heavier now than when you picked it up.

And then there is the table by the window.

You are eating alone, in public, with a book you are not reading. There is no task. There is nobody to talk to. There is no reason to move. And into that space arrives a running commentary, in the third person, about a man eating alone in a restaurant, which you are supplying, and which you cannot switch off, and which is not being written by anybody else in the building.

None of that is dramatic. Every one of it is small enough that you have never described it to another person out loud. You are probably here because a word got used about it, or because you went looking for one. This page will give you the accurate vocabulary, tell you exactly how strong the evidence behind it is and where the popular version of it overstates the case, and then it will do the thing that makes it worth reading rather than skimming. It will hand you the one belief on this page that makes a prediction, and show you how to test it against your own week.

What it will not do is tell you that nobody is thinking about you. That sentence is the entire competing genre, it is not quite true, and it has never once held for more than about ninety seconds in anybody's life.

## Where Does This Show Up Across an Ordinary Week?

In smaller places than the ones you would list, which is exactly why it has never registered as a single pattern.

You arrive four minutes late to something and the cost of those four minutes has almost nothing to do with the four minutes. What you pay for is the door, the heads, the walk to the only free chair, and the twenty minutes afterward in which you are still slightly outside the meeting.

You have a preferred position in every room, and it is never the middle. You have never explained this to anybody and you would find it strange to hear yourself say out loud.

You take the escalator behind somebody rather than beside them, so that you are not a person standing next to a person.

You choose the self-checkout when the staffed till is faster, and you know it is slower, and you do it anyway.

You have a specific version of yourself for the changing room at the pool, which involves a towel and a sequence and a route, and the sequence has been the same for eleven years.

You will not eat something that requires two hands in front of people you do not know well.

You go quiet in a group of five and are perfectly fluent in a group of two, and you have concluded from this that you are bad at groups. It is more specific than that. In a group of five, the attention allocation is unclear at every moment, and in a group of two it never is.

You rehearse the question you are going to ask before you ask it, and then a lot of the time the moment passes and you do not ask it, and you carry the unasked version home.

You have a slightly different walk when you know somebody is behind you on a quiet street.

You have a name badge or a lanyard somewhere in your life that you turned around so the name faced your chest.

And you have the recovery you never account for. The twenty minutes in the car park before you drive off. The stop on the way home. The three quarters of an hour after getting in, during which you would say nothing is happening.

None of those is a problem on its own. Together they are a tax, levied in five and ten minute pieces, on a claim about other people that nobody has ever asked you to produce evidence for.

## What Is the Name for This, and What Does the Name Actually Cover?

Three terms are worth having, and one thing about how they appear here is worth telling you first.

Each of these three is credited, with its researchers named, on a different page here that uses it for a different behavior. Repeating the same names in this section would tell you nothing you could not get from a click, and it would make five pages look like they were written off one reading list. So the attribution sits on the pages that carry it, linked as we go, and here the terms are given as described models rather than as any one person's result. The work is real. The names are one link away.

**Self-consciousness** is the plain term and it is the least clinical of the three. Researchers in the 1970s separated it into two kinds, and the separation is the useful part. **Private self-consciousness** is attention to your own inner life, your thoughts, moods and physical sensations. **Public self-consciousness** is awareness of yourself as an object that other people are looking at. People sit stably in different places on both. This page is about the second one, and the honest thing to say about being high on it is that it describes where your attention rests rather than what is wrong with you. [The page on stepping out of photographs carries the attributed version of this distinction](/patterns/avoiding-photos/), because a camera is close to a pure test of it.

**The spotlight effect** is the measured gap between how much attention people believe is pointed at them and how much actually is. The demonstration everybody quotes involved students walking into a room wearing a shirt they had already said they found embarrassing, and then estimating how many people had noticed. Their estimates came in at roughly double the real number. Variants found the same shape for contributions to a group discussion and for how people thought their performance was being tracked. [The page on saying more than you meant to carries the attributed version](/patterns/oversharing/), where it explains why the audit you run the morning after is an unreliable instrument.

**The illusion of transparency** is the neighboring finding, and it points inward rather than outward. It describes how much people overestimate the visibility of their own internal states. You experience your nervousness at full volume, in detail, continuously, and you cannot get far enough away from that experience to imagine how thin the outside version is. This is why you have been told you seemed calm during something you remember as visibly coming apart, and why you assumed the person was being kind. [The page on waiting for somebody to notice you are struggling carries the attributed version](/patterns/waiting-to-be-noticed/), where it explains why the signal that felt enormous registered as an ordinary Tuesday.

Now what these three names genuinely capture, and where they stop.

**What they capture.** They capture that the trouble sits in an estimate rather than in your character. They capture that the estimate errs in a consistent direction, high, across large numbers of people, which makes it a property of how minds work rather than a fact about you. And they capture the thing that actually brings people to a page like this, which is that the sensation completes before you have any opinion about it.

**What they do not capture is why yours is the size it is.** A name describes a shape. It says nothing about how a particular person came to carry a particular version of it. Two people can walk the same eleven steps across the same floor and produce identical estimates, one because that is simply where their attention rests, one because a year of real scrutiny taught them to run the count, and one because the observer they are performing for is not in the building at all. Same walk, same feeling, three unrelated situations, and no term above separates them.

**And none of them tells you the size of your own error.** That number does not come from a page. It comes from a measurement, which is what the second half of this is about.

## When Does This Belong With Somebody Qualified Rather Than a Page Like This?

Four situations, and none of them is the ordinary version this page is written for.

If this is running your logistics rather than costing you minutes, that belongs with a licensed clinician instead of an article. The markers are concrete and behavioral: turning down work, avoiding whole categories of building, physical symptoms in ordinary rooms, or a life that has quietly narrowed to places where nobody can look at you.

If it started suddenly, or arrived with physical symptoms, or has no relationship to any change in your circumstances, that is a question for a doctor rather than for a page about a pattern. Something that has been true since you were twelve is a different object from something that started in March.

If the sense of being observed carries content, meaning that you have a specific belief about who is watching and why, that is outside what this page describes and outside what it can help with, and it belongs with somebody qualified now rather than later.

And if what you recognize here sits alongside anything you are already being seen for, the person seeing you is better placed than this page and should hear about it.

For everybody else, which is almost everybody reading, the rest applies.

## Why Is the Sensation Itself Accurate Equipment?

Because detecting attention is one of the oldest and best-tuned things your visual system does, and it is worth establishing that before anything gets called an error.

Faces are handled fast. A face turned toward you is handled faster still. And where the information is poor, your visual system does not sit on the fence: it leans.

Mareschal, Calder and Clifford reported in 2013 that when observers are asked to judge where a face is looking, and the image is degraded so the gaze direction is genuinely ambiguous, people default to judging that the gaze is directed at them. The uncertainty does not produce a random spread of guesses. It produces a bias toward direct gaze. Put plainly, when your visual system cannot tell whether somebody is looking at you, its standing assumption is that they are.

That is worth sitting with for a moment, because it means the feeling that arrives when you walk into a room has a mechanism behind it. What you are feeling is a prior. In a crowded, poorly lit, fast-moving environment where you cannot resolve twenty gaze directions, the assumption your equipment ships with is that some of those gazes are yours. There is nothing broken in that and it was probably useful for a very long time.

The caveats belong here too, since this page is going to be strict about them elsewhere. That work was done with computer-rendered faces on a screen, under deliberately degraded viewing conditions, in a lab, with the small samples that perceptual studies typically use. It tells you about a bias in a judgment task. It does not tell you that a room full of people whose gaze direction you can plainly resolve is full of people looking at you, and I am not going to stretch it that far.

What it does establish is the first and most important thing on this page. The sensation of being looked at arrives unbidden, from equipment that is working correctly, and it is not the part of this you chose or the part you can argue with. Everything else here is about the layer on top.

## How Big Is the Error, Actually?

About a factor of two, which is large, useful, and considerably less flattering than the version you have met.

The demonstration that made this famous is one of the most repeated anecdotes in popular psychology. A student puts on a shirt with an image on it that other students have already rated as embarrassing. They walk into a room where several people are filling in questionnaires. They are pulled out again shortly afterward and asked how many of those people could name the image on the shirt. The people in the room are asked the same thing.

The estimates came in at roughly twice the actual number.

That is the finding. Now here is what happens to it on its way to the internet: it becomes "nobody noticed." And that is not what the numbers say. The actual figure was not zero. In the region of a quarter of the room could name the shirt. A quarter is a lot of people. If you walked into a meeting with something wrong with your appearance and I told you that two or three of the eleven people there would be able to describe it afterward, you would not experience that as reassurance, and you would be right not to.

So the honest version of the finding is this: **you are wrong by about half, in a consistent direction, and you are not wrong by all of it.** That version is less comforting and enormously more useful, for a reason that matters for everything below. A claim of "nobody noticed" is disproved by a single memory of somebody who did, and you have several, which is why that reassurance has never lasted for you. A claim of "you are running about double" survives the memory of the person who noticed, because it predicted them.

Four more things worth being straight about, because this page is going to lean on this research and you should know how much weight it can take.

**These are small laboratory studies.** The sample sizes are modest by any modern standard, and the participants were overwhelmingly undergraduates in a particular country in a particular decade, recruited on campus and tested on campus.

**The effect sizes are moderate rather than enormous.** The doubling is a robust-looking pattern rather than a law of nature, and individual studies vary a good deal around it.

**The measure is a self-report of an estimate against a self-report of noticing.** Both halves are reports. Somebody who noticed a shirt and did not think about it again may or may not say so accurately twenty minutes later, and the actual figure is therefore itself an approximation.

**And the popular retelling has drifted a long way from the paper.** The shirt story is now told in books, talks and videos by people who have never read the study, and every retelling rounds the number down toward zero because zero makes a better sentence. Treat any version you meet that says nobody was watching as an ornament rather than as a finding.

| Nobody is thinking about you | Your estimate runs about double the real figure, and the real figure is not zero |
| The feeling is irrational | The sensation comes from equipment that defaults to direct gaze under uncertainty, which is rational |

## Why Does Your Inside Leak Outward as Their Attention?

Because the only model of a mind you have ever had direct access to is your own, and you build every other one out of it.

Nickerson reviewed this at length in 1999, under the heading of imputing your own knowledge to others. His argument, gathered across a large body of studies, is that people build models of what specific other people know by starting from what they themselves know, and then adjusting. The adjustment is real and it is systematically too small. Once you know something, it becomes very difficult to reconstruct not knowing it, and your estimate of what somebody else can see stays anchored to your own vantage point.

Apply that to the walk across the floor.

You know exactly what you did with your arms, because you were monitoring them at high resolution for eleven consecutive steps. That information is loud, detailed, continuous and unavoidable to you. You then need to produce an estimate of what a person at desk fourteen took in. You start from your own version, which is the only material available, and you adjust downward. And you do not adjust nearly far enough, because there is no procedure available for adjusting down to the correct answer, which is that the person at desk fourteen was reading an email and did not register that anyone crossed the floor.

That is the mechanism underneath both the spotlight effect and the illusion of transparency, and it is why they behave like siblings. In one direction, your detailed knowledge of your own appearance leaks outward and becomes an estimate of how visible you are. In the other, your detailed knowledge of your own state leaks outward and becomes an estimate of how readable you are. Same anchor, same insufficient adjustment, two different outputs.

There is a satisfying consequence to this, and it is the basis of the check further down. The error says nothing at all about how interesting you are. What it describes is where you are standing. Everybody in the room is running the identical error about themselves, from their own vantage point, at the same time. The person at desk fourteen who did not notice you crossed the floor is at that moment convinced that everybody has an opinion about the email they just sent.

## Does Being Watched Actually Change How People Behave?

This is where the popular literature is at its weakest, and it is worth taking apart, because the belief sitting underneath your dread is that being observed has consequences.

The famous demonstration is a good one. Bateson and colleagues ran a study in 2006 in a university coffee room with an honesty box, where people were expected to pay for their drinks on trust. Above the price list they alternated two images week by week: a picture of flowers, and a picture of a pair of eyes. Payments were substantially higher in the weeks with the eyes. The result was elegant, cheap to run, and immediately famous, and within a few years pictures of eyes were being stuck up in bike sheds, car parks and cafeterias on the strength of it.

Then the accumulated evidence was pooled. Northover and colleagues published two meta-analyses in 2017 across the studies that had built up, and found no evidence overall that artificial surveillance cues increase generosity. Not a smaller effect than claimed. No effect they could detect, across the body of work as a whole.

I want to be careful about what that does and does not mean, because it would be easy to overclaim in the other direction now.

It does not mean that being observed never changes behavior. A real person actually present in the room is a different stimulus from a photograph of eyes on a wall, and everyday experience that people behave differently when genuinely watched is not overturned by this.

What it means is narrower and more interesting for you: **the version of "being watched" that lives on a poster, in the corner of your imagination, is a weak influence even in a laboratory.** The eyes you carry with you into an empty corridor are the poster version. They are not a person. And the best evidence available says that the poster version of an audience, pooled across dozens of attempts to demonstrate it, does not reliably move even something as simple as how much change somebody drops in a box.

That is worth having on a page like this, and no competing page on this subject will tell you, because it cuts against the idea that the stakes of being observed are as high as the feeling insists.

## Why Do Two People Feel Watched in the Identical Way for Completely Different Reasons?

Because the sensation is the output, and what produces it sits underneath and out of view.

Two people walk into the same meeting four minutes late. Both feel the heat in the face. Both take the chair nearest the door. Both spend the following twenty minutes slightly outside the conversation, and both would describe it in almost the same words afterward if either ever described it, which neither will.

For the first, nothing at all is wrong. Attention rests where it rests, the room registered, and it will be gone by lunch.

For the second, an estimate is running that says nine of the eleven people looked up and three of them formed a view.

Same door, same chair, same heat, entirely different situations, entirely different next steps, and no description of the moment separates them.

Five versions follow. The first is the most common and the most under-said, and it goes first for that reason.

## What If This Is Just Ordinary Social Awareness?

This is the most likely answer for a large share of the people reading, it deserves to be said generously, and almost nobody says it at all.

Noticing that you are among people is no defect at all. That awareness is the equipment working. A person who walks into a room of thirty and registers nothing about being among thirty people is missing something, and the things they miss have costs of their own. The awareness that makes you adjust your volume, notice that somebody wants to speak, catch that a conversation has turned, and know without being told when you have arrived somewhere formal is the same awareness that makes you conscious of your arms on the walk to the printer.

You cannot have the first without the second. They are one instrument.

So the sentence you were probably owed at about fifteen and did not get is this: registering the room is a capacity rather than a symptom, and being high on it puts you at one end of an ordinary distribution rather than outside one.

The tells for this root are reassuringly boring.

**It has no number attached.** You feel the room, and what you feel is a general sense of being among people. You are not producing a headcount, and if somebody asked you how many people had looked up you would say you had no idea, because you would have no idea.

**It releases.** The feeling arrives when you walk in and it fades within a few minutes of you having something to do. It does not follow you to the car and it does not turn up at half past one.

**It is not selective about approval.** It does the same thing in a friendly room as in a hostile one, because it is about being among people rather than about being assessed by them.

**And it comes with the corresponding skills.** You are usually good at reading rooms, catching moods, and knowing when to stop talking. People tell you that you are perceptive. Those reports are describing the same instrument from the outside.

If that is you, the useful thing this page can do is take away a verdict you did not earn. There is a whole industry telling people with functioning social awareness that they have a problem, and a certain number of readers arrive here having been sold that idea and would be better off putting it down.

One honest complication, since this root is the most comfortable place to stop. It is also what nearly everybody says first, including people for whom one of the other four is running hard. The distinguishing question is whether a quantity ever appears. Ordinary awareness never produces a figure. The moment your experience contains "most of them" or "everybody at that end", you have crossed from awareness into estimation, and estimation is the next root.

## What If the Process Is Right and the Estimate Is Wrong?

This is the central version, it is the most relievable, and if you recognize a number in your experience it is probably you.

In this root nothing is malfunctioning. The detection works. The judgment about what the detection means works. What has gone wrong is a single quantity: how many people, on average, are pointing attention at you at any given moment. You are running an accurate process on a wrong input, and the output of an accurate process on a wrong input looks exactly like an accurate conclusion from the inside. That is precisely why it feels so certain and why arguing with it does nothing.

The tells here are the sharpest of the five.

**Your experience contains numbers.** Not vague awareness. Specific claims. Half the room. Everybody on that side. The three people who definitely saw.

**You are surprised by the outside version.** Somebody mentions the meeting where you spoke and remembers a completely different thing about it than the thing you have been carrying, and their version has no kindness in it. What they hand you is what they actually retained.

**Photographs and recordings of you contradict you.** You have seen a recording of yourself doing something you remember as visibly terrible and it looked like a person doing a thing.

**The error is worse for features you know about in advance.** The mark on the shirt you noticed at breakfast produces a much larger estimate than the identical mark you discover at four in the afternoon, and nothing about the shirt changed between those two moments. Only your own knowledge of it did.

**And other people's estimates about themselves strike you as obviously too high.** Your friend is convinced everybody noticed the thing she said. You know for a fact that nobody did. That asymmetry is the whole finding, visible in your own life, from the outside, for free.

If this is your version, the second half of this page is written for you specifically. An estimate is the one part of this whole arrangement that responds to evidence, and it is the only part that is cheap to test.

## What If You Genuinely Were Watched Once?

This root has an entirely different shape, and it is the one most often mistaken for oversensitivity, usually by the person carrying it.

Somewhere there was a setting where scrutiny was real and sustained. A house where your face was monitored for changes and there were consequences attached to what was found. A school where a specific group tracked you for a year. A workplace where your work was being examined for a reason and you were the only one who did not know why. A period of being the subject of a story that other people were telling.

In that setting, the count you were running was accurate. Somebody genuinely was watching. Running the count was the correct response to the situation and it may well have been protective.

Then the setting ended, and the count did not.

The tells for this root are quite specific.

**You can locate a period.** Not a single incident necessarily, but a stretch of months or years where the watching was real, and you know which one it was as soon as the question is asked.

**The alertness is oddly specific.** It fires hardest around one kind of person, one kind of room, one tone of voice, one age of authority. The specificity is the fingerprint, because a general trait would not be selective.

**It arrives before the room does.** You are already scanning in the corridor. The count has been started before you have any information to run it on.

**And it is disproportionate to the current setting rather than to rooms in general.** Rooms full of strangers may be entirely fine. The one that undoes you is a room that rhymes with the old one, and it may be objectively the friendliest room in your week.

What this root needs is not a corrected estimate. Somebody whose count was accurate for three years does not have an arithmetic problem. What it needs is an update to the question of which rooms it applies in, and that is slower work than the check further down, and it is honest to say so rather than to sell you a four minute exercise for it. The check is still worth running, because it will show you the current figure. It just will not be sufficient on its own.

## What If the Observer Is Not in the Room?

This root explains the thing that puzzles people most, which is why leaving does not help.

In this version the judging is happening, and it is yours. Other people are the surface it is being projected onto. That is why the commentary continues in the car, in an empty flat, in the shower, and in the ten minutes before sleep, when there is demonstrably nobody present to be doing any of it.

You can test this one in about a day, and the test is unusually clean. Notice whether the running assessment stops when the people do. If it does, the people were doing it. If it carries on, uninterrupted, into rooms containing nobody at all, then removing people was never going to work, because the people were never the source. Some readers discover at this point that they have spent years managing rooms for a process that was never in the rooms.

More tells.

**The voice has a register.** It sounds like somebody. Sometimes you can name who. Sometimes it is a composite that has been running so long it has stopped sounding like anybody in particular.

**It is unimpressed by evidence.** Somebody tells you directly that the presentation was good, and the assessment continues at exactly the same volume, which is the tell that the assessment was never waiting for external data in the first place.

**It runs about things nobody could possibly have seen.** A thought you had. A moment of feeling something uncharitable. A private version of yourself you are certain would be found unacceptable, though nobody has ever met it.

**And it is much harsher than any actual person has been.** If you wrote down the worst thing a real human being has said to you this year and set it next to what you said to yourself about the thing you did on Tuesday, the comparison is not close.

Where this is the root, the material that applies to you sits underneath the room entirely, and this page will get you no further than the sorting. It is worth being straight about that rather than letting you carry a room-shaped framework away and apply it to something that has nothing to do with rooms.

## What If You Really Are Conspicuous?

This one is not a distortion at all, it belongs on the list, and any page that leaves it off is doing you an unkindness in order to keep its argument clean.

Some people are actually being looked at, consistently, in the settings they spend their days in.

Being the only person in the room of your background, your color, your size, your accent, or your gender in that particular job. Being visibly different in a way people look at, including anything about your body or how you move. Being new in a place where everybody else has fifteen years of history. Living somewhere small enough that your car being outside somebody's house is information. Holding a role where being looked at is literally the job. Being in a period where your work is genuinely under review and everybody knows it. Being a teenager, which is a stage of life where peers really are tracking each other with an attention that adults forget the intensity of.

In every one of those, the sensation is a correct reading of the situation. There is no error to correct. The count is accurate.

The tells are the reverse of the second root.

**Other people confirm it independently.** Somebody else in a similar position describes the same experience, or a friendly observer mentions unprompted that you get looked at.

**It is setting-specific in a way that maps onto reality.** It is heavy in the one room where you are visibly the exception and absent in the airport, where nobody knows anything about anybody.

**It has content that turns out to be right.** You thought people were talking about you, and later you found out they were.

**And it changes when the setting changes.** Move, or change jobs, or spend a week among people like you, and it drops away entirely rather than following you, which is the clean separation from the fourth root.

If this is your version, the useful question stops being whether the perception is accurate and becomes what carrying it costs and what you want to do about the setting. That is a different conversation, and it concerns arrangements rather than the correction of a distortion. It is also the version where a page telling you nobody is watching does the most damage, because you know from your own evidence that it is false, and you may then throw out everything else along with it.

## Which of the Five Fits, and What Would Separate Them?

The separating question is different for each, and none of them is about how sensitive you are.

| The root | The question that separates it | What you would notice |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary social awareness | Does your experience ever contain a number? | A general sense of the room that releases within minutes and never follows you home |
| A wrong estimate | Are you regularly surprised by what other people actually retained? | Specific quantities in your experience, and an obvious sense that other people's estimates about themselves are too high |
| Scrutiny that was once real | Can you name a period when the watching was genuine? | Highly selective triggering, alertness that starts in the corridor, and friendly rooms that undo you |
| An internal observer | Does the commentary stop when the people do? | It runs in an empty flat, it is unimpressed by reassurance, and it judges things nobody could have seen |
| Genuine conspicuousness | Would somebody else in your position report the same thing? | It maps onto the setting, it has content that turns out to be accurate, and it drops away when the setting changes |

Read the right-hand column slowly. Most people find one row lands differently from the other four. A fair number find two are running together, most often the second and the fourth, which is a coherent combination: an inflated estimate gives the internal observer material to work with, and the observer keeps the estimate topped up.

## What Is the Check, and How Do You Run It?

Here is the part that makes this page worth the time, and it turns on a single property of the second root that nothing else on this list has.

**The belief makes a prediction.**

"Everybody is watching me" behaves like a claim about a quantity in the world rather than like a mood, and quantities can be compared against other quantities. That is genuinely unusual. Most of what people carry around about themselves cannot be checked at all, which is why most self-directed exercises end up being about deciding to feel differently. This one can be checked in four minutes with a pen.

**Step one: write the prediction down before the event.** Two lines, before you go in. First, how many of the people there you expect to notice the specific thing you are worried about. Use a number, not a word. Second, what you expect them to be able to say about it tomorrow if somebody asked.

Writing it down matters more than it sounds, for a reason you will discover the first time you try. Predictions that stay in your head get quietly edited afterward to match whatever happened. A prediction on paper cannot be edited, which is why it is the only version worth collecting.

**Step two: get one actual number.** There are two ways and both work.

The direct way is to ask one person a narrow question the following day. Not "did you notice anything", which invites politeness and gets you nothing. Something with an answer in it: "what do you remember about the first ten minutes of that meeting?" Then listen to what they actually produce, without helping them, and count whether the thing you have been carrying appears in it. Usually it does not appear, and usually what does appear is something you had not thought about once.

The indirect way requires nobody, and for a lot of people it is the more convincing of the two. Take the room you were in yesterday. Without looking anything up, write down what the person two seats away was wearing, one thing they said, and whether they seemed at ease. Do it for two more people. Then look at how thin your answers are.

That thinness is the point. That is your entire attention budget for other people, honestly measured, on an ordinary day, in a room you were fully present in. Theirs is the same size. The people you are performing for have a budget of exactly that shape, and almost all of it is already committed to themselves.

**Step three: put the two numbers next to each other and do not draw a conclusion yet.** One pair is an anecdote. Three pairs is a pattern, and three pairs takes about a week to collect.

**Step four: expect the answer to be "some" rather than "none".** This is the part that separates the check from the reassurance genre, and it is why it survives. If your prediction was nine of eleven and the honest figure is two, you have not learned that nobody sees you. You have learned that your instrument reads about four times high in that room, which is precise, actionable and consistent with the two people who genuinely did notice. A conclusion that has room in it for the people who noticed is a conclusion that will still be standing next month.

A few practical notes, because people run this badly in predictable ways.

Do not run it on the biggest thing you are afraid of. Run it on something ordinary: the question you asked, the way you looked at lunch, the sentence that came out wrong. The mechanism is identical and the ordinary version is where most of your minutes are actually going.

Do not change anything while measuring. No avoiding, no compensating, no new technique. You are collecting a baseline. Changing your behavior halfway through is how you end up with no usable data and a conclusion you already had.

Do not use somebody who loves you for the direct question if you can help it. They will manage your feelings, and you will know they are managing your feelings, and the answer will be worthless in both directions.

And do not expect the feeling to change during the week. It will not. What changes is what you know, and the feeling follows knowledge slowly and at its own speed. Anybody promising otherwise has not run this.

## Why Does Being Told Nobody Is Thinking About You Make It Worse?

Because it is an unverifiable claim about other people's inner lives, offered to somebody whose entire difficulty is an unverifiable claim about other people's inner lives.

Look at the structure of it. You are carrying a belief you cannot check, and it is causing you trouble. Somebody hands you a second belief you also cannot check, pointing the opposite way, and asks you to hold that one instead. Nothing about your position has improved. You have been given a different guess and told to prefer it.

There is a second problem, which is that in the strict form it is usually delivered, it is false. Some people do notice. You have memories of people who noticed, and possibly of people who commented. Every time the reassurance meets one of those memories it loses, and it loses instantly, because a universal claim is defeated by one counterexample. This is exactly why that sentence has never held for you for more than about ninety seconds, and why you have concluded that you must be beyond help. You are not beyond help. You were handed a claim that was easy to break.

The third problem is what it does to the person receiving it. Told that nobody is looking, and continuing to feel looked at, you now have evidence that your perception is defective in a way other people's is not. The reassurance quietly installs a second problem underneath the first.

What the check offers instead is small and durable. Not "nobody is watching", which is false and fragile. Something more like "in that room, on that day, I predicted nine and the honest figure was two." That is a fact you own. It does not require you to trust anyone's account of what other people are thinking, it does not break when you remember somebody who noticed, and it does not depend on my telling you anything.

## Why Do Exposure Drills and Affirmations Fail Here?

Because both of them aim at something other than the estimate, and the estimate is where the trouble lives.

Take the manufactured embarrassment approach first, since it is recommended constantly on this subject. You are told to go and do something conspicuous on purpose: lie down in a public place, ask a stranger for a discount you have no expectation of receiving, sing on a train. The reasoning sounds fine. Do the frightening thing, discover you survive.

What happens in practice is that you complete the drill and acquire a story about the day you did the drill. The situation was designed, it was chosen by you, it was survivable by construction, and nothing in it resembled the eleven steps to the printer. Your nervous system is not fooled by a scenario it wrote. And the ordinary scenes that are actually costing you five minutes at a time, forty times a week, remain entirely unmeasured, because the drill was never about them.

There is a further cost that gets skipped. A drill you fail, or half-do, or feel ridiculous during, hands you a new piece of evidence pointing the wrong way. The check on this page cannot be failed, because it does not ask you to do anything except write two numbers down.

Now the affirmation approach. You are told to say that you are confident, that you belong in the room, that other people's opinions do not define you. Look at what each of those is about: you. And the belief causing the trouble concerns other people rather than you. What it claims is how many people at the other end of a room have attention pointed in your direction. Saying warm things about yourself leaves that number untouched, because that number carries no verdict on your worth. It is a guess about a quantity, and guesses about quantities respond to counting.

There is one more thing this page will not prescribe, and it is the popular one: stop caring. Genuine indifference is not available to a human being and would be poor equipment if it were. [That argument is made properly on its own page](/insights/how-to-stop-caring-what-people-think/), and it is a different argument from this one, because caring what people think is a stance you hold over days and feeling watched is a sensation that arrives inside a room, often about something nobody could have an opinion about, like how you are holding a tray.

## Which Part of This Did You Actually Choose?

Almost none of the sensation and all of the arithmetic, and separating those two is the most useful thing on this page after the check itself.

You do not get a say in the sensation. It arrives from equipment that leans toward direct gaze under uncertainty, it completes before you have a thought, and by the time you notice it, it is a report rather than a decision. Being annoyed with yourself for it is aimed at a part of this that was never up for negotiation, which is why the annoyance has produced nothing except more of itself for however many years you have been running it.

The estimate is a different matter. It is a number. You have never written it down, never compared it against anything, and never noticed that it was a claim rather than a perception. It has been running unexamined for decades, deciding whether you speak, where you sit, which invitation you accept, and whether you go at all.

That is the whole division, and it is unusually clean. One half of this is weather and the other half is arithmetic. Almost everybody spends their entire effort on the weather.

Two things so the split does not get misused.

The first is that a checked estimate does not make you comfortable. You will still feel the room. What changes is that the feeling stops being treated as a headcount, and it was the headcount that was making decisions.

The second is that this is not a demand that you audit your life. Three pairs of numbers in a week is the entire program. There is no daily practice attached and nothing to keep up.

## Where Does This Stop and Something Else Begin?

Six neighboring patterns get folded into this one, and each is handled properly somewhere else, which matters because the wrong name sends you at the wrong work.

**Caring what people think of you.** If what you recognize is the shape of your decisions around anticipated disapproval, the softened message, the thing you did not wear, the plan you altered because of how it would look, [that is a different pattern with its own page](/insights/how-to-stop-caring-what-people-think/). The clean separation is time and content. That one runs in days, before and after, and it is about opinion. This one runs in minutes, inside a room, and a great deal of it concerns things nobody could have an opinion about at all, like your arms. Plenty of people carry one heavily and the other hardly at all.

**Taking criticism personally.** If the trouble arrives when somebody has actually said something about your work, and the comment then behaves like a verdict on you rather than information about the output, [that is covered properly elsewhere](/patterns/why-do-i-take-criticism-personally/). That page owns the delivered comment. This one owns the situation where nothing has been said and you are estimating.

**The audit after you said too much.** If your version is the replay that follows a moment of having told somebody more than the moment could hold, [that has its own page](/patterns/oversharing/), and it carries the attributed research on why that replay is an unreliable instrument. The overlap is the replay. The separation is what triggered it: a disclosure you made, rather than the ordinary fact of being present.

**Waiting for somebody to notice.** If your recognition is the opposite problem, sending a small signal about a hard week and watching it register as nothing, [that is a different mechanism with its own page](/patterns/waiting-to-be-noticed/), and it carries the attributed research on how much of your inner state actually reaches the outside. The two are mirror images and they sit in the same person surprisingly often, which stops sounding contradictory once you notice that both are the same anchoring error pointed in opposite directions.

**Stepping out of photographs.** If the sharp version of this arrives specifically when a camera comes out, [that is dealt with fully elsewhere](/patterns/avoiding-photos/), including the gap between the face you carry and the file. That page owns the lens. This one owns the room.

**Scripting conversations before and after.** If most of your hours are going on rehearsing and replaying conversations with specific people, [that behavior has its own page](/patterns/rehearsing-conversations/). The rehearsal of three words at a counter belongs here, because it is about being seen doing an ordinary thing. Rehearsing a difficult conversation with your mother for four days belongs there, because it is about the conversation.

What this page owns, and what the others deliberately leave alone: the sensation of being observed while nothing is happening, the estimate riding on top of it, and the fact that the estimate can be measured.

## What Does This Cost Over Ten Years If Nothing Changes?

More than the individual moments, and in a shape most people cannot see until it has been running a long time.

The rooms get subtracted. Not by decision. One reasonable choice at a time, each with a good individual explanation, until the range of places you go has narrowed a great deal and nobody, including you, can point at the year it happened.

The estimates stop being updated. A room you have avoided for six years carries a figure in your head that has not been compared against anything since. Uncorrected estimates only move in one direction, which is how a person who is careful about rooms at twenty-eight can be barely leaving the house at forty-two without having become any more visible.

The unasked questions accumulate. Every meeting has one or two you rehearsed and did not ask, and they do not disappear. They collect into a slowly widening gap between what you know and what people think you know, and that gap eventually starts producing real consequences at work, which then feed the estimate.

The tax compounds quietly. Twenty minutes in a car park, three quarters of an hour on the sofa, a walk taken the long way. None of it is visible on any day and all of it is time.

And the people close to you build a theory. In the absence of an explanation they construct one, and the one they usually construct is about them: that you did not want to come, that you were bored, that you disapproved of their friends. That theory is wrong and it is not unreasonable given the evidence, which is that you left early and said very little. The cheapest correction available is one sentence said in advance, and most people in this pattern have never said it once.

Against that, the cost of the check is four minutes and a pen.

## What Does the Settled Version Look Like on an Ordinary Tuesday?

Less different from now than people expect, which tends to disappoint and is worth saying anyway.

You still feel the room when you walk in late. That has not gone and there is no reason it should, since it comes from equipment that is working. What has changed is that the feeling arrives and stays a feeling, rather than converting itself into a claim about how many people looked up.

You still notice the walk across the floor. What has changed is that you no longer treat the noticing as evidence. It has become a thing your body does in open spaces, in the same category as noticing that a room is cold.

You still get the replay occasionally, at half past one. What has changed is that it now has a competitor, which is the memory of three occasions on which you wrote down a prediction and it came in four times too high, and that memory is specific and yours and does not evaporate the way reassurance does.

You ask the question in the meeting more often than you used to. Not always. More often. And the ones you do not ask stop being carried home.

And the twenty minutes in the car park has become five, or has become nothing at all, and you got that time back without becoming a different sort of person.

What has not changed is the sensitivity itself. Ten years from now you will still register the room. Every page promising to remove that is offering you something you would not want if you got it, since the same instrument is doing your reading of everybody else.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

It runs at three speeds and confusing them is why people decide nothing is happening.

**The check takes a week.** Three predictions, three actual figures. It is concrete, it costs almost nothing, and most people are startled less by the size of the gap than by the fact that they had never once looked.

**The estimate moves over months.** A number that has been running unchallenged since you were fourteen does not update because you contradicted it three times in March. It updates because you accumulate a dozen instances over a year, and because you stop being surprised by the direction of the error. Somewhere in there, without a specific day attached to it, the walk across the floor stops producing a count.

**And which rooms it applies in moves slowest of all.** If your version is the third root, where scrutiny was once genuinely real, expect this to take considerably longer, and expect it to be uneven, and do not read the unevenness as failure. An alertness that was accurate for three years is not going to be talked out of itself in a fortnight by somebody who was not there.

Expect the sensation itself not to move much, ever. What changes is the number on top of it and how many of your decisions that number gets to make. Three of those four things are available. One of them is not, and it was never the problem.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

The research and the reasoning, separated, so you can weigh each on its own.

Mareschal, Calder and Clifford published their study on gaze perception in Current Biology in 2013, reporting that under uncertainty about where a face is looking, observers default to judging the gaze as directed at them. That is the source for the claim that the sensation arrives from equipment biased toward direct gaze rather than from an invention. Its limits are stated in the body: rendered faces, degraded images, a lab judgment task, and typical perceptual-study sample sizes.

Nickerson published his review of imputing one's own knowledge to others in Psychological Bulletin in 1999, gathering evidence that people build models of what others know by anchoring on their own knowledge and adjusting insufficiently. That is the source for the mechanism section and for the claim that the same anchoring produces both the outward and the inward version of the error.

Bateson and colleagues published the honesty box study, in which images of eyes above a price list were associated with higher payments, in Biology Letters in 2006. Northover and colleagues published two meta-analyses in Evolution and Human Behavior in 2017 pooling the accumulated work on artificial surveillance cues and finding no evidence overall that they increase generosity. Both are cited together deliberately, because the second is the honest state of the first.

The spotlight effect, the illusion of transparency, and the separation of private from public self-consciousness are named in the body without attribution attached. That is a decision rather than an oversight, and the reason is given where they appear: each is credited to its researchers on another page here that uses it for a different behavior, and repeating the same three names in a fourth place would make a set of independently written pages look like one reading list. The findings are real, they are linked, and the attribution is one click away in each case. Treat them here as described models rather than as anyone's personal result.

Two further things I have deliberately not claimed. I have not told you that the spotlight effect has or has not survived a large-scale replication effort, because I could not verify a specific result of that kind well enough to put it in front of you, and I would rather say so than imply a certainty I do not have. And I have not offered a number for how common any of the five roots is, because no such number exists and inventing one would be the same offense as the "nobody noticed" version of the shirt study.

The self-consciousness questionnaire deserves one more line of honesty. It is an old instrument, first published in the 1970s, and researchers have argued about its internal structure ever since, including whether its two headline components are really two, and whether one of them splits further. The original analysis itself found three components rather than two. None of that makes the distinction useless. It does mean that a score on it is a rough position on a continuum rather than a measurement of a thing.

The five roots are mine and carry no research weight at all. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind and I am not a therapist. They are a working sorting device built from watching the same eleven steps across the same floor turn out to be doing entirely different jobs in different people. Their value is whether one of them makes you recognize something, rather than whether they map onto any published taxonomy.
