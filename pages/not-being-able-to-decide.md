# Why can't I make a decision and stay with it?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because nothing in the search tells it when to stop. Something gets picked, the picking does not feel like closing, and the comparison starts again from the top. Most people stuck here have plenty of information about the options and no criterion, no preference they can feel, and no permission from themselves to stop looking.
</div>

- "Two mattresses, the same price, and I have given four evenings of my life to it."
- "I order the same thing every time, and it is not because I like it best."

The two options cost the same.

That is the part that will sound absurd if you ever say it out loud, and it is the reason you have not. Identical price, near enough identical specifications, both fine, both available, and the comparison has now had four evenings. You could not tell a friend what the deciding factor is, because you have not found one, and after a certain number of hours the absence of a deciding factor has stopped registering as information and started registering as your own failure to look properly.

So you look again.

By the fourth evening you have a shape to the whole thing. There is a moment around twenty past ten where one of them is clearly the answer, and there is a moment about nine minutes later where the other one is, and neither moment survives contact with the morning.

Eventually something gets picked, and here is what does not happen. The relief does not arrive. You get a version of relief that lasts about as long as it takes to complete the transaction, and then, nineteen minutes later, you are reading the cancellation policy. Not because you plan to cancel. Because a route out is the thing that makes it bearable, and finding it is the last step in the procedure.

The furniture changes and the shape does not.

There is the version with people in it. You ask your sister. She says the first one, with reasons. You ask a colleague, who says the second one, also with reasons. You ask a friend at the weekend and somebody at the gym on Tuesday, and by Tuesday evening you notice something that you will not enjoy noticing, which is that you currently agree with the person from the gym, and that the only property distinguishing his answer from the other three is that it arrived most recently.

There is the version that has been running for so long nobody counts it any more. You order the same thing. Every time, at that place, for eleven years. Not because you prefer it, and if anybody asked you would say it is easier, and the true version is that choosing again would cost something and choosing this costs nothing.

And there is the tiredness, which is the part that makes the least sense from outside. Nothing difficult happened today. Nobody was rude to you, nothing broke, nothing was demanded. You got home and you were wrung out anyway, and you could not have named a single hard moment in it. Four open comparisons ran all day in the background, reopening every time your attention went anywhere quiet, and none of them produced anything you could point at as work.

## What Does an Undecided Fortnight Actually Look Like?

Smaller and more distributed than the phrase suggests, which is exactly why it goes uncounted.

It looks like a wardrobe with two of something in it and the receipt for both still in the bag, because the return window is the real decision and it has three days left on it.

It looks like paint swatches taped to a wall since spring, in a room nobody has painted, with the tape now doing more visual damage than either color could.

It looks like a booking made with free cancellation chosen over the cheaper option without it, where the price difference has been paid for a route out that will never be used.

It looks like a message that has been drafted and left, then reopened on the bus, then left again, where the difficulty was never the wording.

It looks like a shortlist of two that was a shortlist of five in March, and has been a shortlist of two since April, and has not moved since.

It looks like the same conversation with the same friend for the third month running, in which you lay out both sides fairly and they say something reasonable and nothing shifts.

It looks like a decision that got made for you by a deadline passing, and the specific, slightly shameful relief that came with it.

Six of those in a fortnight produces a fortnight in which you were reasonable at every individual point and closed nothing.

## Why Are You Exhausted on a Day When Nothing Happened?

Because an open choice does not wait quietly. It reruns.

An unresolved comparison has a particular property. It cannot be finished and it cannot be put down, so it comes back whenever there is room. Waiting for a kettle. Walking to the car. The first ninety seconds of lying down. Each return costs a small amount, produces nothing, and reaches the same place it reached the last time, which is why the total is invisible in any single day and unmissable across a month.

Four of those running at once is a full working load with no output attached. That is the honest description of a day in which nothing difficult happened and you came home wrung out, and it is worth having, because the alternative explanation most people reach for is that something is wrong with their energy.

There is a second cost that lands on everything else. A person holding several open comparisons has less available for the next choice that arrives, which makes that one harder, which leaves it open too. The pattern feeds itself without needing any help from your character.

## What Is the Name for This, and Which Half of the Popular Version Is Wrong?

The everyday names are **chronic indecisiveness** and **decision paralysis**. The researched vocabulary nearest to it is the distinction between **maximizing** and **satisficing**, and the popular retelling of that distinction gets the interesting half wrong.

Start with what the distinction says. A maximizer searches for the best available option and therefore has no natural point at which to stop, because there is always the possibility of something better one search further on. A satisficer searches until something clears a standard they already hold, and then stops, because the standard supplies the ending. The critical piece is that the standard exists before the search begins. A standard set afterward, by whatever happens to be in front of you, can never be met, and the [page on starting over from scratch](/patterns/starting-over-from-scratch/) sets out that stopping-rule half with its attribution attached.

I am naming the distinction rather than crediting it to one person. It has been arrived at from several directions, the figures usually named for it are already carrying other work elsewhere on this site, and the model is more useful stated accurately than attached to a surname.

**The popular version, and why it is only half right.** What circulates is a two-line story: maximizers get objectively better outcomes and feel worse about them, so the cure for your misery is to want less. The usual evidence is a study of graduating job seekers in which those scoring high on the maximizing measure landed better salaries and reported more regret and less satisfaction with the search. That study is real and it is set out with its attribution on the [page about researching instead of starting](/patterns/researching-instead-of-starting/), which owns that territory.

**The correction, which is the part almost nobody supplies.** The whole story rests on a questionnaire, and that questionnaire has a contested structure. Nenkov and colleagues examined it in 2008 and found it separating into components that behave differently from one another, including how much you search alternatives, how hard you find deciding, and how high your standards are. Diab, Gillespie and Highhouse argued in the same year that the original measure conflates two things that ought to be kept apart: wanting the best, which is a goal, and being unable to stop searching, which is a difficulty. They built a measure of the goal on its own, and the association with unhappiness largely did not travel with it.

Sit with what that does to the popular story. Wanting the best and being unable to stop looking correlate often enough to end up inside one questionnaire, and they are not the same thing, and they come apart under measurement. Plenty of people hold very high standards, decide quickly, and are perfectly content. Plenty of people have modest standards and still cannot close anything. If the advice you have absorbed is that your problem is caring too much about getting it right, that advice was built on the half of the measure that does not carry the effect.

**What the vocabulary genuinely gives you.** It moves the behavior out of the character category and into a describable one with other people in it. It puts the emphasis on the search rather than on the options, which is where it belongs. And it names the actual missing piece precisely, which is a rule for stopping.

**Where it stops.** Every term here describes an act. Two people can run the identical four-evening comparison of the identical two mattresses and be doing entirely different things underneath. One is defending against a decision that cost them badly in 2019. One has never in their life been able to feel a preference and is trying to compute one instead. The vocabulary covers the what with real precision, says nothing about the why, and the why is the part that has to move before the behavior does.

|---|---|
| A personality trait you either have or do not | A search with no stopping rule, which can be present in one domain and absent in another |
| Proof that you care too much about getting it right | A difficulty that measurement separates from high standards, and that plenty of high-standard people do not have |
| Something that more options always make worse | An effect that shows up under particular conditions and averages close to nothing across the accumulated studies |

## When Does This Belong With Somebody Qualified Rather Than a Page Like This?

Three situations, and none of them is what most people arriving here are looking at.

If the inability to choose arrives alongside persistent hopelessness that does not lift on good days, or sleep that will not return for weeks, or a mind that runs all night with no off position, then the stall is sitting next to something heavier and the heavier thing goes first. That belongs with a licensed professional early rather than after months of pushing through alone.

If the choices that have become impossible include the ones this page calls cheap, so that what to eat and what to wear now take real effort and produce real distress, that is a different order of difficulty from what is described here and it deserves proper attention rather than a self-observation exercise.

And if there is any thought of harming yourself, that belongs with a licensed professional now rather than later, and everything else on this page can wait indefinitely.

## Why Do Two People Stall on the Identical Choice for Completely Different Reasons?

Because the stall is the visible part, and the visible part carries almost no information.

Two people spend four evenings on two mattresses at the same price. One of them signed a lease in 2019 that cost them eighteen months and a friendship, and every comparison since has been run at that intensity regardless of what is on the table. The other has never once been able to answer the question of what they would like, in any domain, and is trying to derive an answer arithmetically because the felt version has never been available.

From outside they are indistinguishable. Every piece of advice built on the behavior gets given to both, and it will be wrong for at least one of them. The first person does not have a decision problem and does not need decisiveness training. The second one is being told to trust their gut by people who have never had to ask where the gut is kept.

Five roots produce this often enough to be worth separating. The first goes first because it is the most common and the most immediately relieving.

## What If the Two Options Really Are Equivalent?

Then the search cannot succeed, because there is nothing in there to find, and most of the suffering is coming from expecting otherwise.

Take the mattresses seriously for a moment. Same price. Both meet the specification. Both would be fine for years. You have now spent four evenings looking for a difference that would decide it, on the working assumption that such a difference exists and you have not yet been thorough enough to surface it. That assumption is doing all the damage. If the two options genuinely sit within touching distance of each other on everything you care about, then no amount of looking will produce a winner, and the failure to find one is a correct result being read as an incomplete search.

Here is the honest sequencing, because a neighbor page owns the first half of it and is right to. A large proportion of stalled comparisons are comparisons being run without a criterion rather than real ties, and they come apart within minutes once you can say in one sentence what you are optimizing for. That test comes first, it costs nothing, and the [page on researching instead of starting](/patterns/researching-instead-of-starting/) sets it out properly.

What this page is about is the case that survives it. You can state the criterion. Both options meet it. And the stall carries on anyway, which is the situation nobody writes about, because it has no technique attached.

The relief available in this root is real and it is not a trick. If two options are genuinely equivalent on your stated criterion, then the choice between them matters less than the closing of it does, and the closeness is information about the options rather than about your judgment. The reason this does not feel relieving on first reading is that most people carrying this pattern have an unstated belief running underneath it, which is that every choice has a correct answer and a person of sufficient care would find it. Held against a genuine tie, that belief produces an infinite search and a growing case against yourself.

The tells for this root are specific. The stall is worst on the smallest and most similar choices, and noticeably better on large ones where the options actually differ. You can articulate what you want from the thing. Nothing about the stall feels frightening, only endless. And if someone else picks for you, you are usually fine with it within a day.

## What If Being Wrong Cost You Something Real Once?

Then the search is a defense, and defenses do not check the address before they run.

Somewhere behind a pattern like this there is often a single decision that went badly in a way you are still paying for. A course of study. A move. A person. A business partner. It is rarely a decision that was made carelessly, which is the cruel part, because the lesson that got installed was not be more careful. The lesson was that your judgment cannot be trusted with anything that has a consequence attached to it, and the search that follows is an attempt to be so thorough that judgment never has to be used again.

That is a coherent response to a real event and it has one design fault. The intensity is set by the original decision and gets applied to everything afterward, including two mattresses, because nothing in the mechanism checks whether the current situation resembles the one it was built for.

The tells here are the clearest of the five. The effort is out of proportion to what is in front of you and exactly proportional to something else. You can name the decision inside about five seconds of being asked, and you can usually name the year. There is a specific fear underneath the searching that has a shape and a sentence attached to it, along the lines of what happens if this turns out like that did. And the pattern is often domain-specific in a way that gives it away completely: the person who cannot choose a flat in three months signs off on decisions at work in under a minute.

The thing that does not help is being told the odds are fine and this is not that. You already know both. The defense was not built by an argument and does not respond to one.

## What If You Cannot Feel What You Want?

Then you have been trying to read something off an inner register that does not work the way you were told it does, and this is the deepest of the five.

Almost all advice about deciding assumes a step you may never have been able to perform. Check in with yourself. Notice which one you lean toward. Ask what you actually want. Every one of those instructions assumes there is a stable answer already stored somewhere, waiting to be looked up, and that your job is to get quiet enough to read it.

For some people that lookup returns nothing, reliably, in every domain, since as far back as they can remember. Not ambivalence, which is having two answers. Something flatter than that, where the question is put and no signal comes back at all, and what arrives instead is a set of arguments. So you compute. You build criteria, you weight them, you compare, and the arithmetic never resolves because arithmetic cannot generate a preference, only apply one.

The research nearby is unusually relevant here and it says something that most people find steadying rather than upsetting. Slovic set out the case in 1995 that preferences are frequently constructed during the act of being asked rather than retrieved from storage, and that the shape of the question changes the answer in ways a stable stored preference could not. Lichtenstein and Slovic had shown the sharpest version of this decades earlier: the same person, presented with two gambles, would choose one of them and then put a higher price on the other, which is not something a settled internal ranking can do.

Hold the limits with it. That work is about how preferences behave under measurement, in laboratories, on small stakes. It does not establish that you have no preferences, and reading it that way would be a misuse. What it does establish is that the picture of a preference as a fixed thing sitting in you waiting to be consulted is wrong as a general account of how choosing works, for everybody, including the people who find deciding easy.

Which matters enormously for this root. If you have spent years concluding that other people have access to an inner answer and you were built without one, the correction is that the inner answer is built rather than found, and often built by choosing. That does not make deciding easy. It does relocate the problem from a defect in you to a method that was never going to work.

The tells: you can argue both sides of any choice fluently and cannot say which you want. Asked what you would like, your first move is to ask what the options are or what the other person prefers. And none of this is confined to the hard decisions, which is why it shows up on lunch.

## What If Choosing One Means Burying the Other?

Then an open decision is holding two futures at once, and closing it is a gain and a funeral at the same time rather than a gain with a small cost attached.

While the choice stays open, both versions of your life are still live. The city you might move to and the one you might stay in. The person and the other person. The career and the other career. Every one of them is intact and available, and nothing has been given up. The moment you decide, one of them stops existing, permanently, and there is a real loss in that which almost nobody names out loud because it sounds like weakness.

The behavior this produces is exact. Nineteen minutes after booking, the cancellation policy. The paid upgrade to the flexible fare that will never be flexed. The purchase kept in the bag with the receipt in it until the return window closes on its own. Each of those buys the same thing, which is the continued existence of the alternative.

There is laboratory work that speaks directly to this, and it points in a direction most people find counterintuitive. Bullens and colleagues reported in 2011 that participants who kept the ability to reverse their choice ended up evaluating what they had chosen less positively over time than participants whose choice was final. Keeping the door open did not protect the satisfaction. It reduced it. The best-known earlier version of the same result came out of a photography course, where students who could exchange the print they had chosen liked it less afterward than students who could not.

The honest caveats go with it. These are small laboratory choices about posters and prints, the effects are modest, and the paradigm has never been run on the decisions that actually shape a life, for obvious reasons. Take it as a mechanism worth recognizing rather than as a measured fact about your marriage.

But recognize what it does to the standard advice. Keeping your options open is presented everywhere as costless prudence. On this evidence it has a price, and the price is paid in your relationship with whatever you chose. That is worth knowing before you pay extra for the flexible fare again.

The tells for this root: the option you did not pick becomes more attractive the moment you lean toward the other one. You feel genuine relief when a decision gets taken away from you by a deadline or by somebody else, and the relief has a shameful quality to it. And you can describe the life the other option would have produced in far more detail than the one you are actually living.

## Whose Approval Is the Decision Actually Being Scored Against?

Then the criterion is borrowed, and the search cannot converge, because you are trying to compute a score in a function you have no access to.

This root is quieter than the others and it produces the most research per unit of progress. The question you believe you are answering is which option is better. The question actually being answered is which option a particular person would approve of, and that person may be your mother, your father, a partner, a former teacher, a version of a friend, or a composite of everyone whose opinion has ever landed on you. They may be dead. They may never have expressed a view on anything resembling this.

More information about the options cannot help with that, because the missing piece sits in a verdict you are trying to predict rather than in the options, and predicting a verdict is not something research about mattresses can inform.

The tells are unmistakable once you know to look. You can say fluently what each significant person in your life would think of each option, and you cannot say what you would prefer. You catch yourself rehearsing the explanation for the choice before you have made it, and the explanation is aimed at a specific face. The stall is worse for choices that will be visible to others and noticeably lighter for choices nobody will ever see, which is the single cleanest diagnostic on this page: if the same decision becomes easy the moment you imagine nobody will ever know what you chose, the criterion was never yours.

Two neighbors sit close to this root and both have their own pages. This differs from waiting to be authorized to start something you have already chosen, which the [page on waiting for permission](/patterns/waiting-for-permission/) owns properly. And it differs from wanting an inherited goal you never examined, where the whole objective rather than the choice between two routes to it is the borrowed thing, which the [page on the life you will start later](/patterns/the-life-i-will-start-later/) covers.

## Which of the Five Is Yours, Judged Only on Evidence You Already Have?

Judge it by what you can check rather than by which description reads best.

| If this is true | The root is probably | The tell that confirms it | What it needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| The stall is worst on small, similar choices and mild on big different ones | The options really are equivalent | Someone else picking for you is fine within a day | Seeing the tie as a result rather than as a failed search |
| The effort is wildly out of proportion, and you can name a year | A wrong choice cost you badly once | The pattern is absent in one domain and severe in another | Looking at the original decision rather than at the current one |
| Asked what you would like, your first move is to ask what the options are | The preference cannot be felt | It shows up on lunch, not only on the hard ones | Building the signal on choices where being wrong costs nothing |
| The rejected option gets more attractive the moment you lean | Choosing buries the alternative | Real relief when a deadline decides for you | Naming what is actually being given up, out loud |
| The choice gets easy if nobody would ever know what you chose | The criterion belongs to someone else | You can predict their verdict and not your preference | Finding out whose verdict it is |

Two notes on the table. Most people are a blend, usually the third row plus one other, and the blend matters less than the dominant one. And the third row is the one people skim past because it sounds too fundamental to be true of them, so if you skimmed it, that is worth a second look.

## Why Does Asking a Fourth Person Never Settle It?

Because each of them is answering against their own criterion, and criteria do not average.

Watch what actually happens across four opinions. Your sister answers on cost, because that is what she weighs. The colleague answers on quality, because that is what he weighs. The friend answers on what she thinks you want, and the person at the gym answers on what he would do. Four coherent answers to four different questions, none of them the question you are stuck on, and the set does not converge because there is nothing in it that could converge.

Then the recency effect finishes the job. You end up holding whichever answer arrived last, because it is the most recent thing that felt like resolution, and the feeling of resolution is what you were actually shopping for.

Two things separate this from ordinary sensible consultation, and both are checkable. The first is whether you could predict what each person would say before you asked. If you could, and you asked anyway, the asking was doing something other than gathering information. The second is what happened when somebody gave you an answer you did not expect. Genuine consultation updates on that. What this page describes goes and asks somebody else.

There is a nearby version where the fifth person is being asked for a green light rather than for a view, and the [researching page](/patterns/researching-instead-of-starting/) owns that one. The version here is different in an important way: you genuinely do not know, and the polling is a substitute for a preference rather than for permission.

## Is Not Deciding Actually Neutral?

No, and this is the least comfortable paragraph on the page.

The stall feels like a pause before a decision. Structurally it works as a decision, taken slowly, in favor of whatever is already happening. The job you have not left is the job you chose this year. The conversation you have not had is the arrangement you selected. The flat you did not move out of is where you now live, by a route that never required you to be the author of it.

Samuelson and Zeckhauser set this out in 1988 across a series of experiments in which the same option was chosen substantially more often when it was presented as the current state than when it was presented neutrally, and the pull got stronger as the number of alternatives increased. More options make the default more attractive, which is close to the opposite of what the popular story about choice would predict.

The wider psychology of doing nothing, including the asymmetry that makes a bad outcome you allowed feel more survivable than an identical one you caused, is set out properly on the [page about not opening the mail](/patterns/not-opening-the-mail/) with its attributions attached. What matters here is narrower. Every week the comparison stays open, an option is being exercised, and it is the one that required no signature.

None of that argues that any decision beats none. Some decisions really are worse than waiting, you already know it, and being told otherwise is one of the fastest ways to stop trusting advice about this. The argument here is against the accounting, in which the stall shows up as zero and it never was.

## Where Does This Hide When You Would Not Call It Indecision?

In several places where it reads as a virtue, which is why it survives so long unexamined.

**In thoroughness.** Reading the fourth review, checking the third comparison site, finding out what the warranty covers. Every step of that is defensible and the set of them has no end in it.

**In fairness.** Wanting to hear everyone before deciding is genuinely considerate and it also guarantees that the decision belongs to whoever spoke most recently.

**In patience.** Waiting for the right moment reads as maturity. Some moments are genuinely worth waiting for, and the ones that never arrive look identical from inside.

**In flexibility.** Paying more for the cancellable version, the flexible fare, the rolling contract. This is the most expensive hiding place and it never appears on any list of things you are struggling with.

**In generosity.** Letting the other person choose the restaurant, the film, the weekend. Sometimes that is kindness. Sometimes it is outsourcing, and the tell is whether you are relieved rather than pleased.

**In the standing order.** The same lunch, the same supplier, the same holiday. A repeat is a stopping rule you built without noticing, and it works, which is why nobody ever files it under difficulty deciding.

## What Does This Cost, and When Does the Bill Arrive?

Later than the behavior, in a different currency, which is why the two are rarely connected.

**The hours are the smallest part.** Four evenings on a mattress is annoying and recoverable. Multiply it across a decade of unremarkable choices and the number gets large, and it still is not the main cost.

**The tiredness is bigger.** A background load of open comparisons takes something off every day, and because it produces nothing visible, most people file it as a problem with their energy or their age rather than as the cost of holding four unfinished questions.

**The options close on their own.** This is where it actually gets expensive. The flat goes. The role gets filled. The person makes other arrangements. A choice deferred long enough gets resolved by the world, and the resolution is rarely the one you would have picked.

**The relationship with what you chose gets worse.** Because you kept the door open, you never fully arrived, and the thing you chose has been on probation since the day you got it. This is the cost the reversibility research points at and it is the one nobody expects.

**And the case against yourself grows.** Every unclosed decision adds a small piece of evidence to a story about your own inadequacy, and after enough of them the story starts making the next decision harder in its own right, which is the point at which the pattern no longer needs any of its original causes to keep running.

## Which Part of This Did You Actually Choose?

Almost none of the part you feel guilty about, and one part you have probably never looked at.

The stall is not chosen, it arrives, and there is no moment in it where two options get laid out and the worse one gets picked, and applying guilt to a process you had no vote in produces nothing except a second problem to manage on top of the first. Most people carrying this have spent years applying effort at exactly the point where effort has no purchase.

The chosen part sits later and it is findable. It is the moment at which new information has stopped changing anything. You can locate it precisely on any given decision: it is the point after which every further review, comparison and conversation left your position exactly where it was. On the mattresses, that was probably somewhere in the second evening.

Everything after that point is a different activity from the one before it, and the question available there is different too. Not which option is better, because that question has already returned everything it is going to return. The question is what the search is protecting you from having to find out.

That question has an answer and the answer is usually one of the five roots above. It is uncomfortable, it takes about ten minutes rather than four evenings, and it is the only genuinely voluntary move in the whole sequence.

Notice what is not being asked for here. Nobody is telling you to pick one, or to flip a coin, or to accept that any decision beats none. Those instructions all treat the stall as the problem, and the stall is a symptom of something that has not been looked at yet.

## How Good Is the Evidence Behind Any of This?

Weaker than the confidence of almost everything else written on the subject, and the weakness is worth mapping precisely, because two of the most quoted findings in this area have real problems.

**The maximizing scale has a disputed structure.** Covered above and worth repeating in the summary, because it is the single most consequential correction on this page. The questionnaire that produced the maximizers-are-miserable story mixes at least two separable things, and the component that carries the unhappiness is the inability to stop rather than the standard itself. Any advice that tells you to want less is aimed at the wrong component.

**Choice overload largely did not survive being pooled.** The jam study, in which shoppers offered a very wide selection bought less often than shoppers offered a narrow one, is one of the most repeated findings in popular psychology. Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd published a meta-analysis in 2010 pulling the accumulated studies together and found the mean effect across them close to zero, with results scattered in both directions. Later work identified conditions under which the effect does appear, notably complex options, an unfamiliar task and no settled sense of what you want, and that moderator analysis is set out on the [researching page](/patterns/researching-instead-of-starting/). What survives is a narrow and conditional claim wearing the reputation of a universal one, which makes it a better example of a famous result that did not hold than it ever was a description of your Tuesday.

**Almost all of this was measured on shopping.** Pens. Chocolates. Jams. Posters. Prints. Gambles worth a few dollars. Laboratory choices with small stakes, short horizons and no biography attached to them. That work is careful and it tells you something real about how choosing behaves under controlled conditions. It tells you very little about whether to end a relationship of nine years, and any page that moves smoothly from the jam to the marriage is selling you an extension as though it were a finding. I am making that extension too, in places, and I would rather say so than have it pass unnoticed.

**The reversibility result is modest and narrow.** Real, replicated in more than one form, and measured on posters and prints. Treat it as a mechanism you can check against your own behavior rather than as a quantified fact about your life.

**The status quo work is in better shape than the rest.** It has been demonstrated in laboratory choices and in large real-world datasets on things like retirement plans and insurance elections, and the basic finding, that an option gets chosen more often when it is already the current state, has held up well.

**What survives all of that.** That searches without a stopping rule do not end. That a tie between close options is a result rather than a failure. That the default gets chosen when nobody chooses. That keeping the door open changes how you feel about what is behind it. That the same stall grows from several unrelated roots. Those five are modest, they are checkable against your own last month, and they are considerably more than the vocabulary delivers on its own.

## Where Does This Stop and Something Else Begin?

Six neighboring patterns get folded into this one, and the wrong name leads to the wrong work.

**Researching instead of starting.** The closest neighbor and the easiest to confuse. That pattern is about preparation standing in for beginning, where there is one course of action and it never gets underway, and the [page on it](/patterns/researching-instead-of-starting/) owns the forty tabs, the third course bought before the first was finished, and the deliberative mode that never switched off. This page owns the live choice between options that both remain available, where the difficulty is closing rather than starting. If there is only one thing you might do and you have not begun it, that page is yours rather than this one.

**Not being able to change.** If the thing you keep noticing is that you understand your pattern perfectly and it carries on regardless, the subject is the gap between insight and movement, and [that page](/patterns/why-cant-i-change/) owns it. It applies to this pattern as much as to any other and it is not specific to deciding.

**Waiting for permission.** Being unable to start until somebody authorizes you is a different mechanism from being unable to choose. The decision has already been made in that one, and what is missing is a green light. [That page](/patterns/waiting-for-permission/) owns it, including why telling yourself you are allowed does nothing.

**The life you will start later.** If the recognizable thing is that your real life is always conditional on one more thing arriving, and the condition quietly changes every couple of years, the subject is the deferral rather than the choice, and [that page](/patterns/the-life-i-will-start-later/) owns it, including the test for a goal you never actually wanted.

**Only working under pressure.** If nothing begins until a deadline is close enough to hurt, and the last night genuinely works, that is a dependency on pressure as the starting mechanism, and [that page](/patterns/only-working-under-pressure/) owns it. It also owns a version of the open-options problem that lives inside a single project, where the angle, the order and the standard all stay unsettled for the whole runway. That is task-shaped rather than choice-shaped, and it is theirs.

**Getting upset when plans change.** If the striking feature is the size of your reaction when something already decided gets moved, that is about certainty routed through a plan rather than about choosing, and [that page](/patterns/why-do-i-get-so-upset-when-plans-change/) owns it.

Three more worth naming quickly. ## What Does the Resolved Version Look Like on an Ordinary Week?

Considerably smaller than people expect, and it does not involve becoming somebody who decides quickly.

The comparison still opens. Two options, both fine, and you feel the familiar pull to look at one more review. What is different is that somewhere in the second evening you notice that the last hour returned nothing new, and you notice it as information rather than as a reason to try harder.

You still ask people. That does not stop and there is no reason for it to. What changes is one thing in the asking, which is that you say out loud what you would choose if you had to answer right now, before they reply. Most of the value in the whole conversation turns out to be in that sentence, and it takes four seconds.

You notice the cancellation policy behavior when it happens, and you notice what it is buying. Sometimes you still pay for the flexible version, deliberately, because the flexibility is genuinely worth it. Most of the time you catch that the extra cost is buying you the continued existence of an option you will never use.

The repeat order stays, and it stops being invisible. You know why you order the same thing, you know what it saves you, and once a month or so you order something else and find out that the cost of choosing again is smaller than the eleven years of avoidance implied.

And one durable change that people report and do not expect. The decisions start being closable in the order of their roots rather than their size. A whole domain lightens at once, because the thing underneath it moved, and choices you thought were separate turn out to have been one thing wearing five different faces.

## How Long Does This Take, and What Actually Moves First?

Three different speeds, and running them together is why people give this up in the third week.

The noticing moves first and it moves at the wrong end. For the first few weeks you catch it afterward, standing in a kitchen having just asked a fourth person, and that feels like failure and is the normal first stage. Within a couple of months most people catch it while it is happening, which is a different experience and does not immediately change the outcome.

The behavior takes longer and it returns under load. Expect the stall to come back during any week that is already hard, on any decision that resembles the one that cost you, and immediately after any choice that turned out badly. A return under those conditions is the return of the conditions rather than the loss of the progress.

The root underneath moves on its own schedule and does not respond to effort applied at the deciding. A defense built by a decision that cost you eighteen months softens when that decision gets looked at properly, which is slow. A preference that was never buildable becomes buildable, which is slower and more reliable than people expect. A borrowed criterion loosens when you find out whose it is, and that one can move fast once it is named.

One thing works immediately, for everybody, regardless of which root is yours. Counting. Two weeks of writing down every choice that stayed open more than a day, with one line on what you were waiting to find out. It requires no self-knowledge to start and it produces the raw material for all of it.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From a modest amount of published research and a much larger amount of watching people describe this in rooms, and the two are worth separating.

On the structure of the maximizing measure, Nenkov and colleagues published the factor analysis and short form in 2008 in *Judgment and Decision Making*, and Diab, Gillespie and Highhouse published the argument for separating the goal of maximizing from the difficulty of deciding in the same journal and the same year. On the default, Samuelson and Zeckhauser set out status quo bias in 1988 in the *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty*. On reversibility, Bullens and colleagues published the finding that keeping the option to reverse a choice reduced later evaluation of it in the *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* in 2011. On choice overload, Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd published the meta-analytic review in the *Journal of Consumer Research* in 2010, finding a mean effect close to zero. On how preferences behave, Slovic published the construction of preference argument in *American Psychologist* in 1995, and the preference reversal work with Lichtenstein dates from the early 1970s.

The maximizing and satisficing distinction itself I have described without attaching a name to it, for two reasons. The stopping-rule half of it is already set out with its attribution on another page here, and the empirical study most often used to carry the popular version is likewise attributed elsewhere on this site. Repeating either would have made this page look better sourced without making the argument any stronger.

I have also left a good deal unused. There is a substantial literature on regret and on the way people forecast how they will feel about choices, and a well-developed line of work on how the brain accumulates evidence toward a decision and why the accumulation takes longest when the options are closest in value. Both are interesting and neither would have changed anything above.

The rest is mine and carries no research weight at all. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, and I am not a therapist. None of the researchers named here has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own reading disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.

## What Is Available to You This Week, Without Closing Anything?

Two weeks of counting, and no decisions at all.

Change nothing. Do not resolve to be more decisive, do not set yourself a deadline on the thing that has been open since March, and do not tell anybody you are working on this, because announcing it turns the whole exercise into something you are being watched on, which is the fastest way to stop looking honestly.

Every time a choice stays open for more than a day, write down four things. What the choice was. What you were waiting to find out. Who you asked, if anybody. And whether anything you learned after the first day changed your position.

By the end of the fortnight you will have somewhere between four and fifteen of them, and three things will be visible that no amount of thinking would have produced. One domain will appear far more often than the rest. The fourth column will be close to empty, which is the whole finding. And the second column will start repeating a phrase, which is usually the root.

If what landed hardest here was the tie, the counting may be enough on its own and you can stop there. If what landed hardest was something else, the year you can name in five seconds, the fact that lunch is also hard, the relief when a deadline decides for you, or the realization that the choice gets easy the moment nobody would ever know what you picked, then the thing worth working on sits underneath the deciding rather than inside it. [Our Sacred Valley seminar](https://florianrolke.com/the-soulsearching-experience/) works at that layer. First we find what the stall is actually doing for you, because a pattern that reliable is never doing nothing, then what stands in the way of the version where a choice closes and stays closed, then a plan you are held accountable to afterward, back in your own week with the same wardrobe and the same shortlist of two.

One thing I will not claim, and it matters more on this page than on most. In Florian's own words: *"I cannot control that within five days you are going to ultimately find your life purpose and realize exactly what you want to do in your life, and everything is perfectly clear. It takes time to find your life purpose."* If the root underneath your stall is that you have never been able to feel what you want, five days will not install that. What five days can do is show you which of the five you are actually dealing with, which nobody has been able to tell you from outside, and give you the first honest attempt at building the signal rather than computing it. Everybody is screened before arriving, which keeps the group small enough that a person who has spent eleven years ordering the same thing gets worked with properly rather than handed a framework. One limitation worth stating plainly: exact dates, the price and the final cap on group size are still being settled, so anybody comparing programs on those numbers this week cannot yet compare ours. Ask every program you are weighing the same questions, this one included, and decide from what comes back.

The fortnight of counting costs nothing and is worth having whichever way it comes out.
