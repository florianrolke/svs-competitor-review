faqs:
- q: >-
    Why is the easiest thing on my list the one I never do?
  a: >-
    Because a list is a ranking, and the ranking runs on how much doing a job
    changes and how much not doing it costs today. A short job scores low on
    both, so it loses every comparison it enters. It is never rejected, which is
    why it never leaves the list either. It simply comes second, several
    thousand times in a row, to something with a better claim on the same hour.
- q: >-
    Why does the small job lose to bigger work every single time?
  a: >-
    Because selection is comparative and the comparison is always available.
    Anything else on your list has more consequence attached, so at every point
    where you could pick, the larger item has the stronger case. A job that
    would have taken a few minutes therefore accumulates months, not because it
    was refused but because it has never once been the best available use of the
    moment you were standing in.
- q: >-
    Is putting off an easy job just laziness?
  a: >-
    Laziness would show up as an empty hour. What actually happens is that the
    hour goes to something larger and more defensible, which is the opposite of
    an empty hour. People with this pattern are usually working hard the entire
    time the small thing sits there. That is why the word never fits, and why
    reaching for it has not moved a single item off anybody's list.
- q: >-
    Does the size of a job predict how long it stays undone?
  a: >-
    In the direction most people expect, no. Look down your own list and find
    the oldest item. It is very rarely the hardest thing on there. Difficulty
    attracts scheduling, because a hard job announces that it needs a block of
    protected time. A short job announces nothing, gets no block, and therefore
    waits behind every hard job that did.
- q: >-
    What is the mere urgency effect?
  a: >-
    A finding reported by Zhu and colleagues in 2018 in the Journal of Consumer
    Research. Given a choice between a task carrying a deadline and a task
    carrying a better payoff, people took the deadline, even where the deadline
    was artificial and the difference in payoff was visible to them. Urgency was
    doing the selecting on its own, independently of what either task was worth.
- q: >-
    How solid is the research on which task people pick?
  a: >-
    Solid enough to take seriously and narrower than the use I am making of it.
    The urgency studies used short scenarios and laboratory choices rather than
    people's actual weeks, and they measured urgency rather than size. Size and
    urgency travel together on a real list, since a job nobody is waiting for is
    usually also a job nobody has dated. The extension is mine.
- q: >-
    Why does breaking it into smaller steps not work here?
  a: >-
    Because the smallness is the problem rather than the remedy. Standard advice
    reduces a job until it is easy to begin, which is genuinely useful where the
    obstacle is that the job feels too big. Applied to something already tiny it
    moves the item further down the ranking, since a smaller job has an even
    weaker claim on the hour than the one you already were not doing.
- q: >-
    Is a short job actually a small job?
  a: >-
    Often not. Minutes and demand are different quantities. Returning one parcel
    is a short walk and about eleven small decisions: which box, which label,
    which service, which day, whether the receipt still exists. Selection
    appears to run on the decisions rather than on the clock, which is why a job
    you can describe in four words can sit still for half a year.
- q: >-
    Why do I never get reminded about it?
  a: >-
    Because nothing in your day is shaped like a reminder for it. Larger work
    arrives with meetings, dates, other people and consequences, all of which
    prompt you without being asked. A small job has no external prompt at all,
    so remembering it depends entirely on you starting the thought yourself, at
    a moment when you are also free to do something with a better claim.
- q: >-
    What is the difference between a job with a cue and a job without one?
  a: >-
    A cued job gets handled by the situation. Somebody asks, a date arrives, a
    machine stops working, and you act without deciding to. An uncued job has to
    be retrieved out of nowhere by you, and the retrieval has to happen at the
    exact moment you could act on it. Most small jobs come to mind in the car,
    in the shower, and in bed.
- q: >-
    Why does the undone small thing feel worse than the undone big one?
  a: >-
    Because the big one has an excuse built into it and the small one has none.
    Nobody concludes anything about your character from a business that
    never launched. An unchanged bulb offers no cover, so it becomes the only item
    on your list capable of returning a verdict. The smallness that keeps it
    from being scheduled is the same property that makes not doing it damning.
- q: >-
    Why do I feel fine about the enormous project I have not finished?
  a: >-
    Because size supplies its own alibi. A large unfinished thing reads as
    ambitious, complicated, blocked by circumstances, or waiting on somebody
    else, and every one of those readings is available without effort. A short
    undone job cannot borrow any of them. That asymmetry is why the shame in
    this pattern attaches to the least consequential item you own.
- q: >-
    Do two people leave the same small job undone for the same reason?
  a: >-
    No, and this is where generic advice comes apart. One person has a genuine
    ranking problem and nothing else. One is holding a job that only looks
    small, with a conversation or a number inside it. One has no place where
    small jobs get handled at all. One has stopped believing anything they
    schedule happens. Four different repairs, one identical stalled item.
- q: >-
    How do I tell which version is mine?
  a: >-
    By checking what happens on a genuinely free morning, and by checking
    whether the job is short in minutes or only short in words. If free time
    exists and the job still does not get done, ranking was not the whole story.
    If describing the job takes four words but doing it needs a decision you
    have not made, you are looking at something disguised as small.
- q: >-
    Where does the hour go, if not to the small job?
  a: >-
    To something larger, more legitimate, and easier to justify afterward. This
    is the part that keeps the pattern invisible. The day was not wasted, the
    work was real, and the list is one item shorter than it was, just never that
    item. A productive week and an untouched small job coexist comfortably, and
    most people never connect the two facts.
- q: >-
    Why does the day still feel productive?
  a: >-
    Because it was productive. The substitution runs upward rather than
    sideways, so every replacement activity has a better claim than the thing it
    displaced. That is precisely why nothing corrects itself. A pattern that
    produced obviously bad days would have been noticed and fixed years ago.
    This one produces good days with one small hole in them.
- q: >-
    Is there evidence that cuts against all of this?
  a: >-
    Yes, and it deserves stating plainly. Rosenbaum and colleagues reported in
    2014 that people often hurry to complete a small subtask sooner, accepting
    extra physical effort in order to do it, which is close to the opposite of
    what this page describes. Both patterns are real. Which one you get seems to
    turn on whether the small job is already in front of you or has to be
    summoned.
- q: >-
    What is precrastination?
  a: >-
    A term Rosenbaum and colleagues introduced in Psychological Science in 2014
    for the tendency to complete a subgoal earlier than necessary at the cost of
    extra work. In their task, people picked up the nearer of two buckets and
    carried it further, rather than picking up the far one at the last moment.
    The interpretation is still argued over. The behavior itself replicates.
- q: >-
    Where does this show up when it does not look like a list?
  a: >-
    In the reply that needs one line and has waited two weeks. In the form
    filled out except for a signature. In the appointment you could book from
    your phone while the kettle boils. In the software update permanently
    postponed. None of these look like avoidance, which is exactly why they
    never get counted as the same thing.
- q: >-
    What has this actually cost me?
  a: >-
    Rarely much in money and quite a lot in evidence. Each day the item survives
    is one more observation, made only by you, that a thing you decided to do
    did not happen. Nobody else is keeping this record. Enough entries and it
    stops reading as a scheduling quirk and starts reading as information about
    whether your own intentions mean anything.
- q: >-
    Why does my word to myself feel provisional?
  a: >-
    Because it has been tested on the cheapest possible material and did not
    hold. A promise to somebody else gets kept by the relationship. A promise to
    yourself about something nobody will check has only you enforcing it, and
    the small job is the purest version of that test. Losing it repeatedly on
    the easiest case is what makes the conclusion feel earned.
- q: >-
    Is saying I will get to it a plan?
  a: >-
    It is a sentence that produces the feeling of a plan and none of the
    properties of one. A plan has a day in it, or an event it sits behind, or a
    person who will notice. That sentence has none of the three, which is why it
    can be said honestly and repeatedly about the same item for a year without
    anything dishonest happening.
- q: >-
    Does the two minute rule work?
  a: >-
    It works well at the point of arrival and does nothing for a backlog. Doing
    a short job the moment it appears succeeds because the situation is
    supplying the cue for free. Applying the same rule to something that has
    been sitting for months fails, because the item has no arrival moment left
    and the rule contains no way of manufacturing one.
- q: >-
    Does batching small jobs into one session work?
  a: >-
    Better than most things, and it fails in a specific way worth knowing. A
    single session gives the whole category the standing that no individual item
    could ever earn, which is the right move. What it does not survive is a busy
    week, because the session is easy to cancel and nobody is waiting for it.
    Protect the slot the way you would protect a booking with another person.
- q: >-
    Why does a clear day not fix it?
  a: >-
    Because a clear day removes the competition and leaves the cue problem
    untouched. Nothing about an empty Saturday makes the bulb come to mind while
    you are standing near a ladder. What usually happens instead is that the
    free day gets spent on the largest thing you have been meaning to do, which
    is a reasonable choice and leaves the list unchanged.
- q: >-
    What actually moves this?
  a: >-
    Three things, in this order. Stop ranking small jobs individually and rank
    the category once. Attach the category to something that already happens
    every week, so the cue comes from the world rather than from you. And clear
    the list honestly, out loud, of everything you are never going to do, which
    is usually a third of it and is doing more damage than the rest combined.
- q: >-
    How do you attach a small job to something that already happens?
  a: >-
    You pick an event that occurs whether or not you are motivated, and you put
    the job immediately after it. After the trash goes out. After the weekly
    call ends. After the last meeting on Wednesday. The event is doing the
    remembering, which is the whole point, since remembering is the part a small
    job has never been able to do for itself.
- q: >-
    Is it ever right to just delete the job?
  a: >-
    Frequently, and it is the most underused option available. Some items have
    been carried for years and would produce almost nothing if completed. Saying
    plainly that a thing is not going to happen costs one uncomfortable moment
    and stops the daily charge immediately. An item removed on purpose is a
    completely different object from an item quietly rotting.
- q: >-
    What does the settled version look like?
  a: >-
    Undramatic. Small jobs still get postponed, and they get postponed for days
    instead of seasons. There is one recurring slot where the category gets
    handled, and it holds most weeks. The list is shorter because things were
    deleted rather than because everything was done. And an item you forgot
    about for a week produces nothing worse than mild annoyance.
- q: >-
    Could this be something other than what it looks like?
  a: >-
    Several situations produce the same stalled item. A job with a hidden
    decision inside it. A job requiring contact with somebody you are avoiding
    for other reasons. A week with no unclaimed hour anywhere in it. A stretch
    where nothing at all has felt worth starting. The pattern of what else is or
    is not getting done separates them quickly.
- q: >-
    When does this belong with a professional instead?
  a: >-
    When the inability to act stops being selective. If short jobs, long jobs,
    enjoyable things and obligations have all gone the same way for months, if
    sleep has been broken for a long stretch, if nothing has felt worth doing,
    or if this has been the shape of things for as long as you can remember,
    that is an assessment question for a doctor or a licensed psychologist.
- q: >-
    Is this the same as procrastinating on something frightening?
  a: >-
    It sits next to that and runs on different machinery. Avoiding a frightening
    task is about what its outcome would say about you, and the avoidance is
    aimed at a feeling. This pattern involves items with no verdict in them at
    all, which is why every explanation built on fear slides off, and why the
    discomfort arrives afterward rather than in advance.
- q: >-
    How long does this take to change?
  a: >-
    Noticing takes about a week, because the evidence is already in your house
    and only needs counting. Shifting it takes a couple of months, because it
    moves at the speed of a rebuilt week rather than the speed of understanding.
    The first honest sign of progress is a shorter list produced by deletion,
    rather than a longer record of things completed.
- q: >-
    What is one thing I can do today?
  a: >-
    Write down every small job you are currently carrying, then put a rough date
    beside each one for when it first appeared. Cross out everything you are not
    actually going to do, out loud. Then take one survivor, name the weekly event
    it will sit behind, and write it in that form. The whole exercise takes about
    half an hour and needs nothing bought.

# Why is the easiest thing on my list the one I never do?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because a list is a ranking, and the ranking runs on how much doing a job changes and how much not doing it costs today. Neither of those has much to do with how long it takes. A small job scores near zero on both, so it loses every comparison it enters, forever. What reads from the inside as a failure of discipline is arithmetic.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
These are the sentences people say about the item that has outlived everything else on the list.

- "I'll happily hoover the carpet to avoid 20 minutes on the phone"
- "I know I can do millions of things and I know that I can do it in five minutes, those five minutes will never arrive."
- "There's something about it. It's like, I'm doing other stuff and I'm like, I'll get to it."
- "I feel like I've been putting that one thing off. And every time I sit down here, I'm just like, dude, so where am I starting?"
- "But instead, I waited 24 to 48 hours to actually do it, and I could have done it straight away."

</div>

There is a light bulb in your house that has been out since February. You know which one. It is not a difficult bulb, it is not in an awkward fitting, and there is a spare in the cupboard bought at some point specifically for this. Everybody who lives there has commented on it at least twice, in a tone that started as a joke and stopped being one a while ago.

Then there is the return label. It has been sitting where you dropped it long enough that other things have been put on top of it and taken off again. The window for sending the item back has probably closed, which changes nothing about the label still being there.

And there is the reply. One line would do it. The person is not difficult, the answer is not complicated, and you thought about writing it four separate times this week.

Meanwhile you finished something genuinely hard this month. A difficult conversation, or a piece of work you were dreading, or a two hour drive to take somebody to an appointment. That is the part that makes this disorienting from the inside. The hard things move. The easy thing does not, and it has now sat there long enough to be embarrassing.

Most people arrive at a page like this having already run the obvious explanation and found it does not fit. The obvious explanation is that you are avoiding something, and avoidance implies a threat, and there is no threat in a light bulb. Nothing is inside it. Nobody is judging the outcome. There is no verdict waiting, no number, no decision. So the standard account of not doing things arrives, looks at your bulb, and has nothing to say.

Something else is running, it is much more ordinary than fear, and it can be checked against your own week rather than taken on trust.

## Why Does the Small Job Lose Every Time It Is Compared With Anything?

Because a list behaves less like a container than like a tournament, and the small job has never once won a round.

Look at what actually happens at the moments where something could get done. You are almost never deciding whether to change the bulb. You are deciding between changing the bulb and every other thing available to you in that half hour, and the deciding is fast, mostly wordless, and finished before you notice it started. Two properties do nearly all the work in that comparison. How much does doing this change? How much does not doing it cost today?

Run your bulb through both. Doing it changes the lighting in one room. Not doing it costs nothing at all today, or tomorrow, or in April. It scores near the floor on both axes at once, which is a very specific and very unlucky position to be in, because those two axes are the entire basis on which anything gets picked.

So the item loses. That by itself would be unremarkable. What makes it a pattern is that losing has no consequence. The job is never refused, never crossed off, never argued about. What happens to it is deferral, and deferral has no end state, so it simply stays and gets compared again tomorrow against a fresh field of things with better claims. A thing outranked ten thousand times is indistinguishable, from the inside, from a thing you have been refusing to do. It feels like refusal, and the machinery producing it is arithmetic.

There is a second half to this and it is the part that surprises people. Difficulty attracts scheduling. A genuinely hard job announces that it will need a protected block, so it gets one, gets a day, gets talked about, gets prepared for. The announcement is what buys it the slot. A short job announces nothing whatsoever, which sounds like an advantage and is the opposite of one. Nothing that never asks for a block ever gets a block.

You can test this without leaving your chair. Find the oldest live item you are carrying. Not the one that bothers you most, the one that has been there longest. For most people it is not the hardest thing on the list, and it is not close. What sits there instead has almost no content in it at all.

| What the list looks like | What is actually deciding | Where the small job lands |
|---|---|---|
| Ten items, one obviously easiest | How much doing it changes, and what not doing it costs today | Bottom, on both counts, permanently |
| A hard job with a deadline | Consequence is visible and dated | Gets a block, gets prepared for, gets done |
| A hard job with no deadline | Consequence is visible and undated | Gets talked about, gets rescheduled, eventually gets done |
| A short job with no deadline | Nothing to weigh on either axis | Never enters the calendar at all |
| The same short job, six months later | Identical to how it scored on day one | Still there, now with a history attached |

Notice the bottom row, because that is where the damage lives. The job has not become more important with age. Nothing has changed about it. What has changed is the record of it, which is now long enough to be about you rather than about the bulb.

## When Does This Belong With Somebody Qualified Rather Than With a Page Like This?

When it stops being selective. That is the whole test, and it is worth applying before anything else here, because everything below assumes a very specific shape.

This page describes a pattern where some things get done and one particular kind of thing does not. The hard work moves. The obligations to other people get met, mostly. The stall is precise and it attaches to items with no weight in them. If that is not your situation, the rest of this will fit badly and the right help is a different kind.

Bring somebody in if any of the following is true.

1. **Everything has gone the same way, for months.** Short jobs, long jobs, work, obligations, and the things you used to look forward to. A general stall is a different question from a selective one.
2. **Sleep has been broken over a long stretch**, or you are waking early and not getting back down.
3. **Nothing has felt worth doing.** Not hard to start, but flat, with the enjoyable things gone too.
4. **This has been the shape of things for as long as you can remember**, across every setting, regardless of how much you cared about the outcome.
5. **It started after something.** A loss, a birth, an illness, a long stretch of real strain.
6. **The item you keep not doing has a clock on it.** A health appointment you have been carrying, a legal deadline, a renewal that lapses. That is a scheduling matter with real consequences attached and it goes ahead of understanding yourself. Book it, then come back.

None of that is a character question and none of it answers to a better system. A doctor or a licensed psychologist can settle in one appointment what you could spend two years blaming yourself for. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind and nothing here is medical advice or a substitute for an assessment by somebody who can actually examine you.

## What Does the Research Say About Which Task Gets Picked?

That selection can run on a property of a task that has nothing to do with what the task is worth, which is the one thing this page needs it to establish.

Zhu and colleagues reported a set of studies in 2018 in the Journal of Consumer Research under the heading of the mere urgency effect. The setup was deliberately unfair to their own hypothesis. Participants chose between two tasks, one carrying a deadline and one carrying a clearly better payoff, with the difference in payoff visible to them. They took the deadline. They took it even where the deadline was manufactured and carried no real weight, and the pull was strongest in people who thought of themselves as busy. Urgency was doing the selecting by itself.

That is not the same claim as mine, and the distance is worth stating rather than smoothing over. They studied urgency. This page is about size. What connects them on a real list is that a short job with nobody waiting for it is almost always also a job with no date on it, so both properties fail at once and the failures compound. A large undated project at least has visible consequence to argue with. A small undated job has neither.

Two honest limits on how far any of it carries.

The first is the setting. These were short scenarios and laboratory choices, not people's actual weeks, and the tasks were minutes long rather than months old. Nothing in that design speaks to a bulb that has been out since February.

The second is what the finding covers. It shows a property that is irrelevant to value driving choice anyway. It does not show that smallness works the same way, and I have no study that does. What I have is the same shape described to me repeatedly, by people with no connection to each other, which is evidence of a kind and is not the same kind.

What the research honestly buys you is one thing, and it is enough. It removes the assumption that selection tracks importance. Once that assumption is gone, a small job being permanently outranked stops needing a psychological explanation, because a mechanical one is already sufficient.

## Why Is Making It Smaller the One Move That Cannot Work Here?

Because the smallness is the mechanism rather than the remedy, and the standard advice is aimed at a different axis entirely.

The advice itself is good and I want to be precise about where it is good. Reduce the job until beginning it is trivial. Open the file and write one bad sentence. Sort ten items and stop. Put the running clothes on. [The general treatment of not starting on this site prescribes exactly that](/insights/how-to-stop-procrastinating/), and it is right, because the case it addresses is a job that feels too large to enter. Shrinking the entry point solves that case cleanly.

Now apply it to something already tiny. There is no smaller version of changing a bulb. The instruction cannot be followed, and where it can, following it makes the position worse: a smaller job has an even weaker claim on the hour than the one you were already not doing. Advice built to lower the barrier to entry has nothing to offer an item whose barrier to entry was never the problem.

There is a sharper version of this and it is worth putting on the record. That same treatment of not starting makes a claim about which tasks get avoided. Its argument is that the tasks you never postpone all have something in common, which is that their outcomes say nothing about who you are, so no feeling is stored in them and no avoidance forms around them. Buying onions. Taking the trash out. Answering the easy message.

Your bulb is on that list. It has been out since February.

So one of two things is true, and which one depends on the person reading. Either the item has a verdict hidden inside it that you have not found yet, which for a real share of readers is exactly the situation and is what the root section further down is for. Or there is a second way for a job to stall, which has no fear in it at all, and which the fear account cannot reach because it was never built to.

Both are true, of different people, and I am not going to pretend the two accounts sit comfortably together. They point in opposite directions on the same behavior. The general page is correct about the case it treats, which is far more common than this one, and I have changed nothing about it. This page exists because its account leaves a specific gap: the case where the task really is as empty as it looks and stalls anyway.

If you want a fast way to tell which side you are on, use this. Imagine the job is done, right now, by somebody else, for free, without you being told. If the feeling is relief, something was in there. If the feeling is nothing at all, apart from mild pleasure at a shorter list, you are on this page.

## Is a Short Job Actually a Small Job?

Frequently not, and the confusion between the two is doing a great deal of the damage here.

"It only takes a few minutes" is the sentence everybody says about the item, including you, including the people who live with you. It is a measurement in the wrong unit. It tells you the clock cost and says nothing whatsoever about the decision cost, and the decision cost is what selection appears to price.

Take the return. Described out loud it is one action: send the thing back. Actually performed it is a sequence of small choices, every one of which has to be made by you, in order, with nobody helping. Whether the original packaging survived. Which box if it did not. Whether the return window is still open, which means finding the order. Refund or replacement. Which carrier. Whether the label prints here or has to be done somewhere else. Which drop-off point. Which day you will be near that drop-off point anyway. Whether the item needs cleaning first. Where the receipt went. Whether you actually want to keep it after all, which reopens the whole thing.

That is eleven decisions inside a job you have been describing to yourself in four words. None of them is hard. All of them are yours.

Now compare it with something that genuinely took you a full day last month and moved without difficulty. It probably had a shape somebody else supplied: a meeting, a brief, a form with fields in it, a person waiting at the other end who had already narrowed your options. Difficulty and demand are not the same thing, and a hard job with its decisions pre-made can be far easier to enter than a short job with its decisions all still open.

There is laboratory evidence for the underlying preference, though it is narrower than what I am doing with it. Kool and colleagues published a set of experiments in 2010 in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General using what they called a demand selection task. People chose repeatedly between two options that were matched on reward and differed only in how much mental switching they required. They reliably took the less demanding one, and most of them could not report afterward that they had been choosing on that basis at all. The preference was operating below the level where they could see it.

Two limits worth stating. The work was done with card decks and shape sorting rather than with a return label, so the transfer to your table is my inference and not their finding. And it establishes a preference, not a stall: nobody in those experiments failed to act for six months. What it does support is one modest, useful claim, which is that mental demand is a cost people avoid without knowing they are avoiding it.

| How you describe the job | How many separate decisions it contains | What it competes against |
|---|--:|---|
| Send the parcel back | About eleven, all open, all yours | Any work with its decisions already made |
| Change the bulb | Four or five, plus finding the step stool | Anything already underway |
| Reply to that message | Two, and one of them is about tone | Every message with an obvious answer |
| Book the appointment | Three, and one needs your schedule in front of you | Anything not requiring your schedule |
| Cancel the thing you stopped using | Six, and two of them involve a login | Literally everything else |

Read the middle column and the mystery softens considerably. The item you cannot get done is rarely the shortest thing on your list. What it usually is instead is the one carrying the highest number of unmade decisions per minute of actual work, which is close to the worst possible profile for something with no deadline attached.

## Why Does Nothing Ever Remind You?

Because nothing in your surroundings is shaped like a reminder for it, and you have been assuming that remembering is free.

Large work does not depend on you remembering it. It is embedded in a web of things that prompt you whether or not you cooperate. Somebody asks. A date arrives. A meeting exists in the week. A machine stops working. Money moves or fails to. You end up acting on most of it without ever deciding to, because the situation did the deciding and handed you the moment.

A short job with nobody waiting for it has none of that. There is no prompt in the world for the bulb. There is no prompt for the return label. Which means the entire burden of getting it into your head at a usable moment sits on you, and it has to land not just at any moment but at one where you could actually act.

Kvavilashvili and a co-author published a study in 2007 in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General that gets at this directly. People were asked to remember to make a telephone call at a set time, some an hour later and some a week later, and to keep a record of every occasion on which the intention came into their mind in the meantime. Two things stood out. Most of those occurrences were spontaneous rather than the product of deliberate checking, and a substantial share were set off by something incidental in the person's surroundings that happened to touch the intention.

Which tells you where the bulb goes wrong. Almost nothing touches it. You are in that room in daylight, when you do not need the light, and you are in it at night with the light already off and your hands full and your mind on the next thing.

Where does the item actually surface? Ask yourself honestly and the answers are always the same set. Driving. In the shower. Lying in bed. Halfway through a piece of work you cannot interrupt. Standing in line somewhere. Every one of those is a location where acting is impossible, so the thought arrives, produces a small unpleasant flicker, and dissolves.

That is worth stating flatly because it changes what the pattern is. The job is not being refused at the moment of action, because it is never present at the moment of action. Those are completely different failures and only one of them has anything to do with willingness.

An honest limit, because this literature does not quite cover you. Prospective memory research is about forgetting, and you have not forgotten the bulb. You think about it constantly. What you have is retrieval reliably arriving at times when action is impossible, which is a relative of the problem the research describes rather than the same problem. I am extending their finding past where they took it, and you should weigh it accordingly.

## Why Does the Undone Small Thing Say More About You Than the Undone Big One?

Because size supplies an alibi and the small job has none, so the least consequential item you own ends up carrying all the weight.

Consider what you are actually carrying that is unfinished. Somewhere there is a large thing. The book. The business you meant to start. The language you began learning and stopped. It has been stalled for years and it produces almost no discomfort at all, and if somebody asked you about it you would have an answer ready in under a second. It got complicated. The money went elsewhere. It is waiting on a decision that is not mine. Life happened.

Every one of those readings is free, instantly available, and largely true. Size manufactures its own excuse.

Now try that with the bulb. There is no version of the sentence where the bulb was complicated. There is no circumstance to point at, no other person involved, no cost, no risk, no obstacle of any kind. Which makes it the only item on your list capable of returning a verdict about you, and it returns one every single time you walk into that room.

That is exactly backwards from what the content would predict, and it is why this pattern is so much more corrosive than its subject matter deserves. The item with the least in it hurts the most. People will describe their stalled business calmly and then go quiet about a light fitting.

Be precise about what the verdict actually says, because it is not the obvious one. Laziness would never survive your own evidence, since you worked hard all week. What the record actually says is narrower and lands harder: that a thing you decided to do can simply not happen, indefinitely, with nothing stopping it. Which raises a question about every other thing you decide.

And you are the only person keeping this record. The people you live with see a bulb. They do not see the fourteen separate occasions on which you noticed it, intended it, and moved on. You see every one of them, which means the evidence available to you is fourteen times heavier than the evidence available to anybody else, and it is entirely private.

Enough entries and the record stops being about lighting. It starts quietly setting the ceiling on what you are willing to promise yourself in areas with no bulb in them at all. [The slow erosion of your own trust in yourself has its own page](/patterns/why-dont-i-trust-myself/), and it is worth reading if that is the part that landed.

Here is the piece that closes the loop, and it is the honest reconciliation between this page and the fear account of not starting. A job that stalls purely on ranking does not stay empty. Every month it survives, it accumulates a little more meaning, until eventually the bulb stops being a bulb and becomes a standing question about whether you do what you say. At that point there genuinely is something inside it, the fear account becomes accurate, and the item gets harder for exactly the reasons that page describes. The stall started as arithmetic and manufactured its own weight. Which is why catching one of these early is worth far more than the job is.

## Do Two People Leave the Same Bulb Unchanged for the Same Reason?

No, and the identical stalled item is produced by at least four different situations that need four different responses.

This is the part of the argument that matters most and it is the part every listicle skips. You can watch a behavior for a year and still be standing entirely on the outside of it. What somebody does is visible. What it is doing for them is not, and the second one is the only thing that tells you what would actually help. Aim a repair at the visible half and it fails, and the failure then reads as a fact about the person rather than as a fact about the aim.

| What is actually going on | What the smallness is doing | How you would know it is this one |
|---|---|---|
| Pure ranking | Keeping the item permanently below the cut-off | A genuinely free morning appears and the item still does not get done, because it never came to mind |
| A hidden decision or conversation inside it | Letting a loaded job pass as an empty one | You can name, in one sentence, the awkward part, and it is not the physical task |
| No place where small jobs happen | Leaving the category with no address at all | Every short job has gone the same way, across every area of your life, for years |
| The record has become the obstacle | Making one item stand for the whole question of whether you follow through | You feel something disproportionate about it, and you have avoided looking at it directly |

Take the first and the fourth, because from the outside they are the same bulb and the right response to each would make the other worse.

The first person has no problem beyond scheduling. The item is genuinely empty, it genuinely never comes to mind at a usable moment, and they feel mildly silly about it and nothing more. Tell that person to sit with the feeling and look for what is underneath and you will have sent them hunting for something that is not there, which wastes a week and teaches them that self-examination does not work.

The fourth person is looking at a small object that has become the standing evidence in a case about their own reliability. They have walked past it four hundred times. Tell that person to set a weekly slot and attach the job to an existing event and you have handed them a good system for a problem they do not have, and when it does not hold they will read that as further evidence, which is exactly the wrong lesson.

Same bulb. Opposite repairs. This is why advice that transformed somebody else's month does nothing for you, and it is not because you applied it badly. [Why the thing that worked for them does nothing for you has a page of its own.](/patterns/why-did-the-advice-that-worked-for-them-do-nothing-for-me/)

A note on the second row, since it is the one that gets missed. Some jobs are short in words and not short in substance. Cancel the subscription means calling somebody and being talked out of it. Send the message means deciding what you actually think about that person. Book the appointment means finding out. Those are not small jobs wearing a disguise by accident; the disguise is doing work, because an item filed as trivial does not have to be looked at. [The version where what is inside the envelope is the whole obstacle is covered separately](/patterns/not-opening-the-mail/), and if reading that row produced a jolt, that page is the better door.

## What Happens to the Hour the Small Job Did Not Get?

It goes to something larger and more defensible, which is precisely why this has been running for years without being noticed.

Picture the alternative for a second. If the pattern produced empty afternoons, somebody would have caught it in the first month. An empty afternoon with a bulb still out in the next room is an obvious contradiction and it would demand an explanation. That is not what happens. What happens is that the hour goes to real work, or to a bigger job on the same list, or to something genuinely worth doing, and by evening the honest summary is that you were productive and the small thing is still there.

The substitution runs upward. Every replacement has a better claim than the item it displaced, which means every individual swap was defensible and the sum of them is a season of not doing something that takes one reach and one twist of the wrist.

This is also why the standard remedies keep sliding off. Nothing about your day needs fixing. Your day is fine. Your day is arguably too good, in the sense that it is full of things with strong claims, and a list full of strong claims is exactly the environment in which the weakest claim never gets a turn.

Watch for the specific version of this, because it has a tell. The substitution is often adjacent. The bulb does not get changed and the whole light fitting gets researched. The parcel does not get returned and the shelf above it gets tidied. The one line reply does not get sent and four other messages do. Adjacency is the giveaway: you were near the item, you had the moment, and the moment went to the neighbor with the better claim.

There is a version where the substitution is upward but the direction is off, and it belongs to a different page. If the larger thing you keep doing instead is reading, watching and preparing rather than acting, [the loop where getting ready substitutes for starting is treated separately](/patterns/researching-instead-of-starting/). And if the pattern is that nothing at all moves until a deadline exists to force it, [that sprint has its own machinery](/patterns/only-working-under-pressure/).

The practical consequence is worth taking on board before the repair section, because it rules out an entire family of solutions. You cannot fix this by having a better day. You have been having good days the whole time.

## Why Does the Evidence Also Run the Other Way?

Because it does, and a page that only showed you the half supporting its own argument would not be worth reading.

Rosenbaum and colleagues published a set of experiments in Psychological Science in 2014 that point in close to the opposite direction. Participants walked along an alley with two buckets standing in it, one near them and one further along, and were asked to carry one of them to the far end. The efficient choice is obvious: take the far bucket, carry it the shorter distance. Most people picked up the near one and carried it much further, and when asked why, they said something close to wanting to get it done. They paid extra physical effort in order to complete a small subgoal sooner. The authors called it precrastination.

That is a real result, it has held up across a number of follow-ups, and it flatly contradicts a simple version of what this page says. If people will accept extra work to clear a small item early, the small item is not obviously the loser in every ranking.

Here is the reading I think is honest, and I want to be clear it is a reading rather than a finding. The bucket was already in front of them. The alternative was holding an unfinished instruction in mind while walking, which is itself uncomfortable, so completing it early bought immediate relief. Every job on this page has the opposite profile: it is not in front of you, holding it in mind costs almost nothing minute to minute, and acting requires summoning it out of an ordinary afternoon that was not about it. Presence appears to flip the whole calculation.

Two further pieces of honesty about that literature. The behavior replicates well and the explanation for it does not have consensus. Reducing the load of a held intention is the account most often given, and it has been argued over since the original paper. Naming a finding and its unsettled interpretation as two separate things is the only accurate way to hand it to you.

And there is a competing explanation of your own list that I should say out loud rather than leave you to discover. Not everything that has sat for a season is a stall. Some of it is a decision you have already made and have not admitted, and the item stays on the list because removing it would mean saying out loud that you are not going to do it. That is not a failure of any mechanism described above, but an unstated verdict wearing the costume of an outstanding task, and it is common enough that the repair section below opens with it.

## Where Does This Show Up When It Does Not Look Like a List?

In six places, all of which have a respectable cover story, which is how the same mechanism runs across an entire life while being counted as six unrelated quirks.

1. **The one line reply.** Two weeks old. The answer has not changed and is not complicated. What has changed is that a reply now needs an opening sentence about the two weeks, which is the only genuinely awkward part and did not exist on day one.
2. **The form completed except for one field.** A signature, a reference number, a date of something. Nearly all of the work is done and sitting in a drawer, which reads as almost finished and functions as not started.
3. **The booking you could make from your phone.** While the kettle boils, standing up, in the time it takes to read this paragraph. It has outlived several things you scheduled and attended.
4. **The permanently postponed update.** Software, a renewal, a change of address, a password that has been flagged for months. Each dismissal costs one tap, and each tap is a small confirmation that this can be dismissed.
5. **The thing you canceled in your head.** The subscription, the membership, the service you have not used since winter. You decided months ago. The deciding was the part that felt like acting.
6. **The nice thing.** A card, a message to somebody you like, the thing you saw and meant to send them. This one is worth separating out, because it destroys the theory that this is about unpleasantness. There is nothing unpleasant in it anywhere and it is on the same list, behaving the same way.

The sixth is the giveaway. If avoidance of discomfort explained the pattern, the pleasant items would be exempt. The pleasant items stall exactly like the rest, which tells you the machinery is running on standing rather than on feeling.

One cross-cutting test for all six. Ask whether you could have done the thing at any point in the last month, at a moment you were actually in, with no preparation. If the answer is yes, and it did not happen, the item is not waiting on conditions, only on a turn that never comes.

## What Does This Cost, Given That It Costs Almost Nothing?

Very little in money, a surprising amount in the room, and most of it in a currency nobody itemizes.

Start with the part that is easy to see and easy to underrate. The item is in a shared space, and it is visible to the people you live with. To you it is one entry among many. To them it is a standing exhibit, and after the second or third mention their options narrow to two, both bad. They mention it again and become the person who nags. They stop mentioning it and become the person quietly keeping score. Neither of those is a good position and neither was chosen.

That is the part where a small job stops being small. Not because the job grew, but because a second person now has a relationship with it. The bulb has stopped being a bulb and become a topic, topics have a history, and the history is what makes the next mention land badly. If you have ever reacted disproportionately to somebody pointing at a trivial undone thing, that is what you were reacting to. The disproportion was accurate. It was just measuring the history rather than the object.

None of that makes this a page about your household. The people around it are the setting, not the subject, and there is nothing here about managing them. What is worth noticing is the direction of the loop: the more the item becomes a topic, the more loaded acting on it becomes, because doing it now would look like compliance. So a job that was stalled on nothing acquires an actual reason to stay stalled.

| Where the cost lands | What it looks like | Why it stays uncounted |
|---|---|---|
| Money | A lapsed return window, a renewal missed, a service still charging you | It is small each time and never itemized |
| Attention | The item arriving in the car, in the shower, in bed | Each arrival is short, and there are hundreds |
| The room | A visible object that produces a small drop every time you pass it | You stopped consciously seeing it inside two weeks |
| The household | A topic with a history, and two people stuck in bad roles | It gets filed as an argument about tidiness |
| Your own record | Private evidence that a decision of yours did not become an event | Nobody else is keeping it, so nobody can correct it |

The last row is the expensive one and it is the only one with no upper bound. Everything above it is finite. That one compounds.

## What Does Saying You Will Get to It Actually Commit You To?

Nothing, and it produces the full sensation of a commitment, which is what makes it the most expensive sentence in this whole pattern.

Look at what a real plan contains. There are only three things that can make an intention happen, and a plan needs at least one of them. A day and a time. An event it sits immediately behind. Or a person who will notice if it does not occur. That is the entire list. Everything else is a description of wanting.

Now hold "I'll get to it" against those three. No day. No event. No witness. It contains none of the three ingredients and it feels exactly like a decision, which is the problem, because the feeling of having decided closes the matter. Nothing further gets thought about the item until the next time it surfaces, at which point the same sentence is available and works just as well.

You can say it honestly for a year. That is not hypocrisy, and reading it that way misses what the sentence is doing. The sentence is true every time it is said: you do intend to get to it. What it lacks is any property that would cause getting to it to happen.

The same shape shows up in the other sentences that pass for planning. When I have a clear run at it. Once this week is over. When I am next in town anyway. Every one of them names a condition and specifies nothing that would produce the condition, which is why the condition has not arrived in six months and will not arrive in the next six.

There is one honest test for whether a sentence you said about the item was a plan. Look at the two weeks after you last said it. Did anything visible change? Not resolve, not feel different, change. If nothing did, the sentence was a description of a preference, and the correct next step is to convert it into one of the three real forms rather than to say it again with more conviction.

Worth separating from a neighboring case, because the failure looks the same and the machinery is not. If the sentence was said to somebody else, out loud, and then did not happen, you are in different territory: a promise has a witness in it, and the cost lands on a relationship rather than on your own record. [Meaning a promise completely and still not keeping it is treated on its own page.](/patterns/promises-i-dont-keep/)

## Why Do the Usual Fixes Move It Rather Than Finish It?

Because almost every one of them is aimed at either difficulty or motivation, and this stall runs on standing and cues.

Go through them properly, since you have probably tried most and concluded something unkind about yourself when they did not hold.

1. **Do it immediately if it takes two minutes.** Genuinely effective, and only at the point of arrival. It works because the situation is supplying the cue for free: the thing has just appeared and you are standing in front of it. Applied to an item that arrived last spring, the rule has no arrival moment left to attach to and contains no way of manufacturing one. It is a filter for incoming work rather than a treatment for a backlog.
2. **Batch them into one session.** The best of the standard set, and it fails in one specific way. Batching is correct because it gives the whole category the standing that no single item could earn, which is the actual mechanism. What it does not survive is a normal busy week, because a session with nobody waiting for it is the easiest thing in the week to cancel, and canceling it costs nothing visible.
3. **Wait for a clear day.** Removes the competition and leaves the cue problem completely untouched. Nothing about an empty Saturday brings the bulb to mind while you happen to be near a step stool. What free days actually get spent on is the largest thing you have been meaning to do, which is a reasonable choice and leaves this list exactly where it was.
4. **Put a reminder on your phone.** The notification arrives at a moment chosen by a machine that does not know where you are standing or what is in your hands. So it gets dismissed, correctly, because acting was impossible. Then it gets dismissed again. What that builds is a reliable habit of dismissing the item, which is the last thing you needed.
5. **Write it on a list.** The list is the ranking. Putting the item on it enters the item in the competition it has already lost several hundred times. Writing it down does real work for remembering and none at all for selecting.
6. **Tell somebody you will do it.** This one works, and it works by converting a ranking problem into an obligation with a witness. Worth knowing what you are trading for: obligations to other people have their own failure mode, and a stack of small promises can produce a different and worse pattern than the one you started with.
7. **Try harder.** Included because everybody tries it first. It fails without teaching you anything, since effort applied to an item that is never present at a moment of action has nothing to act on.

The common shape in the failures is worth naming. Every one of these treats the item as a job that needs to be started. The item does not need to be started so much as it needs to arrive at a moment where starting is possible, carrying enough standing at that moment to beat whatever else is available. Those are two separate problems and neither is about effort.

## What Changes If You Stop Trying to Do It and Start Trying to Rank It?

Everything worth changing, because the item was never failing at the doing stage. The failure was happening at the selection stage, several hundred times, silently.

Three moves, in this order. The order is the part people get wrong, and doing them out of sequence is why previous attempts held for a week.

**One. Clear the list out loud before you do anything on it.**

Write down every small job you are currently carrying. All of them, including the ones you would not mention to anybody. Then go through and mark the ones you already know you are not going to do. Be honest rather than aspirational: the test is not whether it would be good to do, it is whether you can name a version of your life in which it happens.

For most people that is somewhere around a third of the list, and it has been sitting there for years producing a daily charge in exchange for nothing at all. Say it out loud, to one person if you have one available and to the room if you do not. I am not going to do this. That sentence is not a defeat, and it is not laziness either. A decision finally catching up with a fact is all it is, and the charge stops the moment it is said.

Do this first, before any of the doing. Spending your first session on a job you had already privately decided against is the most demoralizing possible way to begin, and it is what happens by default.

**Two. Rank the category once instead of ranking the items forever.**

An individual small job cannot win a comparison against your real work. It never could and it never will, and no amount of resolve changes what it scores. A category can win, because a category has weight: all the small things, together, once a week, is a defensible use of an hour in a way that one bulb is not.

Then, and this is the actual mechanism, no ranking happens inside the slot. You take them in the order they were written, oldest first, and you stop when the time is up. The thing that kept your item down for a season is comparison, so you build one place where comparison is not permitted to operate.

Oldest first matters more than it sounds. Easiest first feels better and is the ranking again in miniature, which is how a session that was meant to clear the backlog ends up clearing the six newest items and leaving the one you have carried longest exactly where it was.

**Three. Attach the slot to an event, not to a time.**

Times get moved. An event that happens anyway does not. After the trash goes out. After the weekly call ends. After the last meeting on Wednesday. Straight after breakfast on the day the cleaner comes. The event does the remembering, which is precisely the function a small job has never been able to perform for itself.

This is the piece that survives a bad week. A slot at four on Thursday competes with everything else at four on Thursday. A slot that sits immediately behind something already fixed in your life inherits that thing's reliability for free.

Two rules that keep the whole arrangement running.

- **Stop when the time is up**, even mid-flow, especially in the first month. What you are building is a session with a guaranteed end, and a session with a guaranteed end is one you will still be holding in eight weeks.
- **Read a lapse as a lapsed support**, not as a fact about you. If the slot disappears for two weeks, the correct conclusion is that the event it was attached to changed, and the repair is to reattach it. That reading is not generosity, it is accuracy, and the other reading has already cost you enough.

One exception, and it is important. The item that has become a standing question about your own reliability does not belong in the slot. Putting it in a batch with nine other things is a way of not looking at it. Do that one alone, on purpose, out of sequence, and then say one sentence out loud to one person about how long it actually took compared with how long it sat. The private record is what has been doing the damage, and the only thing that touches a private record is making one entry in it public.

## What Does the Settled Version Look Like on an Ordinary Week?

Considerably less impressive than you are imagining, and much closer than it currently feels.

| Where it stands now | Where it goes | What that looks like in a normal week |
|---|---|---|
| One item carried since February | Items get postponed by days rather than by seasons | You notice something is a week old and it registers as ordinary |
| The list only ever grows | The list gets shorter mostly by deletion | Four things crossed off on purpose, none of them done |
| Small jobs compete with real work | The category has one protected slot | Half an hour behind something that happens anyway |
| Nothing ever reminds you | The event does the remembering | You are standing there before you have decided anything |
| Every undone item is evidence about you | An undone item is an undone item | You forgot one in April and thought nothing of it |
| The bulb is a topic in the house | The bulb is a bulb | Somebody mentions it and nothing tightens |
| You do the easiest ones first | You do the oldest ones first | The item you have had longest goes early, every time |

Look at the second row rather than the third, because that is the one people skip and it does most of the work. A shorter list produced by honest deletion is worth more than a longer record of completed tasks, and it arrives faster.

And notice that nobody in the right hand column has become an organized person. They still leave things late. What changed is that nothing reaches the age where it starts meaning something, and that the category finally has somewhere to happen.

## Could This Be Something Other Than What It Looks Like?

Worth checking, because at least six situations produce an identically stalled item and they want different responses.

| What you are looking at | What it might actually be | What tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| One short job, stuck for a season | Ranking, with nothing else underneath | A free morning appears and it still does not come to mind |
| A short job you can name in four words | A job with a decision or a conversation inside it | You can state the awkward part in one sentence, and it is not the physical task |
| Every small job in your life, for years | No place where the category happens at all | There has never been a slot, in any period, including the good years |
| One item you feel disproportionately about | A private record that has outgrown its subject | You avoid looking directly at it, and you know exactly how long it has been |
| Everything, including things you enjoy | Something flatter running underneath all of it | Whether anything in the last month has felt worth starting |
| A list that keeps growing regardless | A week with no unclaimed hour anywhere in it | Whether a genuinely free hour has existed at all this month |

None of these is settled by how long the item has been there. The information is in what else is or is not moving, and that is visible from where you are sitting without any introspection at all.

Two of the six are worth a further word. If the honest answer to the fifth row is that nothing has felt worth starting, this page is the wrong tool and the earlier section on when to bring somebody in is the right one. And if the answer to the second row is yes, that there is a conversation or a number inside the job, then what you have is a loaded item filed as an empty one, and the loading is doing real work by keeping the item from being examined.

## How Long Does It Take Before Anything Feels Different?

Noticing takes about a week. Shifting it takes a couple of months. Those are two different speeds and confusing them is why people quit at the point where the thing is actually working.

The noticing part is fast because the evidence is already in your house and only needs counting. Write the list, put a rough first-appearance date beside each item, and you will have the whole pattern in front of you in one sitting: the ages, the fact that the oldest item is not the hardest, and the size of the section that is really a decision you have not admitted. Most people find that sitting uncomfortable and clarifying in roughly equal measure.

The shifting part is slower because it moves at the speed of a rebuilt week rather than at the speed of understanding. A slot that holds, attached to an event that holds, with a deletion pass behind it. Each of those is a structural change and none of them is difficult, and the reason it takes two months anyway is that a week has to run several times before you know whether the arrangement survives a bad one.

Expect the first two weeks to feel like nothing is happening, because the visible output is small. Four items deleted and two done is not a dramatic result and it is the correct result. The dramatic version, where a whole afternoon clears the entire backlog, is the one that reliably rebuilds itself inside a few months, because nothing about the week changed and the inflow was never touched.

It also comes back under load, and it comes back first in the stretches where you are already struggling, which is exactly when it feels like proof that nothing took. A pattern that solved a real problem returns whenever the conditions it solved for return, and a hard month reproduces them faithfully. Progress shows up in how quickly you notice, and in how young the oldest item is, rather than in never postponing anything again.

One honest thing about the timeline. I cannot tell you which of the four situations in the root table is yours, and neither can any page. That is the part that takes a person and a conversation rather than a method, and it is the reason two people can do everything above and get completely different results.

## Where Does Any of This Come From, and How Solid Is It?

From four public bodies of work, none of which you need to go through me to check, plus a set of extensions that are mine and carry no research weight at all.

Zhu and colleagues published the mere urgency studies in 2018 in the Journal of Consumer Research, reporting that people chose tasks carrying a deadline over tasks carrying a better payoff, including where the deadline was manufactured and the payoff difference was visible. Kool and colleagues published their demand selection work in 2010 in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, showing a reliable preference for the less cognitively demanding of two equally rewarded options, mostly operating below the point where participants could report it. Kvavilashvili and a co-author published a diary study of remembering to act at a set time in 2007, also in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, finding that intentions surfaced spontaneously far more often than through deliberate checking, and were frequently set off by something incidental in the surroundings. Rosenbaum and colleagues published the bucket experiments in Psychological Science in 2014, describing precrastination, and that one runs against the argument here rather than for it.

Now the limits, stated because they matter more than the citations do.

The urgency work measured urgency and this page is about size. The demand work used card decks and this page is about a return label. The prospective memory work is about forgetting and you have not forgotten anything. In all three cases the finding is real and the extension to your list is mine, and if any of the three turns out not to carry that far, the corresponding section of this page is weaker than it reads. The precrastination result is the only one pointing the other way, its behavior replicates, and its interpretation is still contested.

None of the four establishes the central claim of this page, which is that smallness itself is the mechanism. There is no study I can point you to that shows that. What supports it is the same shape described to me repeatedly by people with no connection to one another, and a mechanism that is at least consistent with what the selection research does show. That is a weaker foundation than a replicated finding and it is the honest description of what you are reading.

The rest is mine. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. ## What Is Worth Doing Today, Before You Touch the List?

Write the list, date it, and cross things off it. Nothing gets done today. That is deliberate.

Here is the whole assignment.

1. **Write down every small job you are carrying.** Not the projects. The short ones. Aim for everything, including the two you would not say out loud.
2. **Put a rough date beside each.** The month it first appeared, or the season if the month has gone. Guess rather than skip.
