# Why do I feel more attached to people who treat me badly than to people who are good to me?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because attachment tracks unpredictability far more closely than it tracks kindness. Warmth that arrives on no schedule, after stretches of cold, produces a pull that steady warmth cannot match, and the relief when it returns is louder than any contentment. What you are most attached to is often the ending of a distress, and the person is simply where the ending came from.

Somebody asked you, once, about the most intense relationship of your life, and you answered without thinking. Then somebody else asked about the happiest, and you noticed that you had named a different person. You have been carrying that for a while now. It is a small fact and it will not sit still.

The intense one is easy to describe, or it feels easy until you start. There were stretches of cold that lasted days, sometimes longer, and there was no warning before them and no explanation after. And then there would be a return. A message, a visit, an evening that went the way evenings used to go, and something in you would unclench so completely that you would find yourself grateful, actually grateful, to a person who had spent thirteen days not answering. You knew what the gratitude looked like from the outside. It changed nothing on the inside.

Meanwhile there is somebody else. Somebody who answers. Who drove over with jumper cables when the car died at the gas station, who asks how the appointment went and remembers the answer, who has been steady for months now. You keep waiting to feel something about them and you keep feeling polite. You have caught yourself describing them to a friend as nice, in the voice people use for a nice apartment, and you have heard what that voice does to the word.

And when you try to explain the difficult one to that same friend, something else happens. You start with the good parts, and they are real, and then you notice you are reaching. The account gets thinner the longer you talk, and by the end you are defending a person to somebody who has not attacked them, and you are no longer sure who you are trying to convince.

So you have three facts that do not agree with each other. The most intense relationship you have had was one of the least happy. A kind person registers as almost nothing. And the relief when a difficult person is warm again is bigger than anything a steady person has ever produced in you, which you know is the wrong way round, and knowing has not once changed the size of the relief.

This page is about the size of the relief. There is a mechanism underneath it that has been described for the better part of a century, it explains the whole pattern without needing to say anything about who the other person is, and it is far more honest than the vocabulary that usually gets attached to this behavior online.

One thing before anything else, and it is the one sentence on this page written without any softening: if you are being frightened, threatened, or hurt by the person you are thinking about while you read this, that belongs with a licensed professional or a domestic abuse service today rather than with a page, and nothing written here asks you to stay, to wait, or to understand it better first.

## What Is the Name for This, and What Does the Name Actually Cover?

The term is **intermittent reinforcement**, and unlike most of the vocabulary that circulates around this behavior, it comes with decades of measured evidence attached, which is exactly why it is worth being precise about what it does and does not say.

Intermittent reinforcement describes a reward that follows a behavior only some of the time, on no rhythm the learner can predict. The central finding, repeated across a very large body of mid-century learning research, is that behavior learned under those conditions is pursued more persistently, and takes far longer to fade once the reward stops entirely, than behavior rewarded every single time. A reward that arrives reliably teaches a rule, and when the reward stops, the rule visibly breaks and the behavior fades quickly. A reward that arrives unpredictably teaches no rule, so there is nothing to break. A gap was always part of the pattern. The learner keeps going.

I am naming the principle rather than a person here, on purpose, and the reason is worth stating. The finding belongs to a line of work rather than to one study, dozens of researchers built it over decades, and the pages on this site about [checking their social media](/patterns/checking-their-social-media/) and about [doomscrolling](/patterns/doomscrolling/) already name the researchers most associated with it, so there is no need to repeat them. What matters for this page is the principle itself, because it is the one piece of vocabulary in this whole territory that was measured before it was named.

Applied to a relationship, the shape is immediate. Warmth that comes after cold, on no schedule, is an unpredictable reward. Reaching for the person, checking, waiting, going back when the warmth returns, are the behaviors it rewards. And the persistence of those behaviors, long after the warmth has stopped coming at all, is exactly what the principle predicts.

What the term captures, then, is the shape of the pull and its persistence. It captures why the difficult person wins against the kind one on a straight comparison of how hard each of them holds you, because the kind one is running a reliable schedule and reliable schedules produce weaker pulls in every learner ever tested. It captures why the pull outlasts the relationship. And it captures why knowing all of this changes the size of the relief not at all, since the principle was never about knowledge.

What it cannot capture is the why.

| What the term names | What the term cannot name |
|---|---|
| The schedule: warmth that arrives unpredictably after cold | Why this particular schedule held you and a reliable one did not |
| The persistence: a pull that outlasts the reward | Whether the person or the unpredictability was doing the work |
| The burst: the pull briefly intensifying after the warmth stops for good | Whether an earlier relationship taught you to read warmth this way |
| The comparison: why steady warmth produces a weaker pull | Anything about the other person's intent, character, or awareness |
| The mechanism, independent of anyone's intent | What you should do next |

That last row matters more than it looks. The term is a description of a schedule and its effects. It is silent on whether the other person knew what they were doing, silent on whether they are a certain kind of person, and silent on whether you should stay or go. Everything written online that stretches the term to cover those questions is stretching it past its evidence, and the rest of this page tries not to. The page on [breadcrumbing](/patterns/breadcrumbing/) decodes the sending side of the same schedule, and this page stays on the receiving side throughout.

## How Good Is the Evidence Behind Any of This?

Better than almost anything else in this territory, and still short of the confident way the vocabulary around it gets used. Both halves of that deserve to be said.

The strong half. The finding that unpredictable reward produces more persistent behavior than reliable reward is one of the most replicated results in the history of learning research. It was established on animals working for food, in thousands of sessions, across decades, and it holds across species and across types of reward with a consistency that very few psychological findings can claim. On its own terms, it is close to settled.

The human half is thinner and worth describing precisely. The cleanest early demonstration in people is from Humphreys, in 1939, who conditioned an eyeblink and found that when the reinforcement arrived on only some trials, the response took markedly longer to fade after reinforcement stopped than when it had arrived on every trial. That is a human being, showing the animal result. It is also an eyeblink. Later human work found the effect real but more variable than the animal version, and sensitive to what people had been told about the schedule: a person who knows the rule behaves differently from a person who is discovering it. Nobody has run a controlled version of any of this on an adult relationship, and nobody could.

So when this page applies the principle to the person you cannot stop thinking about, that is an extension. I think it holds. The shape of the behavior matches the shape of the finding closely enough that I would be surprised if it did not, and I have watched it run in enough people, myself included, to trust the match. But you should weigh it as an argument built on a real principle, which is what it is, rather than as a demonstrated result about relationships, which nothing in this area is.

That honesty matters here more than usual, because the popular vocabulary for this behavior has none of it. The names that circulate online for the pull toward somebody who treats you badly are used with a confidence that no study underneath them supports. Most were coined descriptively, spread through advice content rather than through research, and have never been measured in the way a term needs to be before it can carry the weight people put on it. Some of them also quietly claim to know things about the other person that no term could know. I am deliberately not using them, and I am saying so rather than leaving them out silently, because a silent omission looks like ignorance and a stated refusal is an argument. The principle above is the one piece of vocabulary in this territory that was measured before it was named, and it is enough.

## Why Does an Occasional Warm Week Hold Harder Than a Steady One?

Because you never learn the rule, and a rule you never learned cannot be broken.

Picture the two schedules side by side. In the first, warmth arrives reliably. You reach out and it is there. Over months you learn, without ever deciding to, that reaching produces warmth, and that learning is solid precisely because it has been confirmed every time. Then imagine the warmth stops. Within a fairly short time the rule is visibly broken, you notice, and reaching fades. The steadiness that made the relationship comfortable is the same thing that makes its ending legible.

In the second, warmth arrives after cold, and the cold has no fixed length. Sometimes two days, sometimes thirteen. You reach, and sometimes it is there and sometimes it is missing, and nothing about the day tells you which. What you learn under those conditions is stranger. You learn that reaching sometimes works, that a gap is never evidence of anything, because gaps were always part of it, and that the next attempt might be the one. Now imagine the warmth stops for good. Nothing breaks. There is no rule to break. Every day of silence looks exactly like the silences that came before it, and every one of those ended eventually. So you keep going, on nothing, for a very long time.

That is the mechanism, and notice what it does not need. It does not need the person to be special. It does not need you to be unusual. It needs a schedule, and a schedule is something a difficult person supplies without effort and a kind person cannot supply at all.

There is a second effect stacked on the first, and it explains the anticipation. Under a reliable schedule, warmth becomes ordinary, because ordinary is what reliability means, and a body stops responding much to things that are ordinary. Under an unpredictable schedule, warmth never becomes ordinary, because it never arrives often enough to. Every return is an event. Every day that might be a return has a charge on it. The kind person has been generous with you for months, and generosity on a schedule is precisely the kind of thing a nervous system learns to stop noticing.

None of this is a flaw in you, and none of it was chosen. A learner under that schedule does what you did.

## Are Intensity and Closeness the Same Measurement?

They are two measurements, and the whole pattern runs on treating them as one.

Intensity is how much a relationship moves you. Closeness is how much of you is known, and how safely. Those come apart far more easily than it feels like they should. A relationship that swings between cold and warm generates enormous intensity, because it supplies stakes, uncertainty and contrast in quantity, and it can do that while almost nothing about you is actually known to the other person, because a person who goes cold for thirteen days is a person you learn to show less to. Meanwhile a steady relationship can carry a great deal of closeness with very little intensity, for the simple reason that nothing in it is at stake from one day to the next.

Fireworks are brighter than a porch light, and nobody has ever read a book by them.

If the only gauge you have for a relationship is how much it moves you, then the difficult person will win every comparison you run, because they have been supplying movement, and the kind person will read as almost nothing, because steadiness produces almost no movement by definition. A speedometer reads speed. It cannot read direction. You can drive very fast toward nowhere, and the dial will report that you are doing extremely well.

There is research on the cost of the mixed relationship specifically, and it points somewhere uncomfortable. Holt-Lunstad, Uchino and colleagues studied what they called ambivalent ties, people in a person's life who are a source of both support and upset, and found that time spent with those people was associated with higher physiological reactivity, measured as ambulatory blood pressure, than time spent with people who were simply negative. A relationship that is sometimes very warm and sometimes very cold, in other words, appears to cost the body more than one that is reliably bad. One note on the citation: those names belong to one research group, so this is one line of evidence rather than two, and blood pressure is a modest proxy for what a relationship costs, gathered in fairly small samples. The finding is suggestive rather than settled. It does line up with what the intensity itself has been telling you, which is that this relationship moved you more than any other. Movement is expensive. That was never in doubt. What was in doubt is whether movement was ever the thing you were measuring for.

| Reads as intensity | Reads as closeness |
|---|---|
| Your mood on a given day depends on whether they were warm | You can predict roughly how a hard conversation with them will go |
| The return of warmth after cold is the best you feel all month | Telling them something unflattering about yourself changes nothing |
| You have shown them less over time, to be safe | They know the version of you that your oldest friend knows |
| You describe the relationship in peaks | You describe the relationship in ordinary days |
| A silence is an event | A silence is a silence |

If most of what you can list about the difficult person lands in the left column, you have been running a very accurate speedometer. It has simply never been asked the right question. The page on [when the early feeling fades](/patterns/when-the-early-feeling-fades/) covers the same two measures from the other end, inside a relationship that started intense and settled.

## Why Is Relief a Louder Signal Than Contentment?

Because relief is a change and contentment is a level, and a nervous system is built to register changes.

A sprained ankle makes the point. For weeks it hurts, and then one morning it does not, and that morning is vivid in a way that the years of the ankle simply working were never vivid. The stopping of a strain is one of the strongest signals a body produces, and it is loud in proportion to two things: how long the strain lasted, and how uncertain you were that it would end. Contentment cannot compete with that. A level, however good, produces a signal that fades into background within days, because that is what levels do.

Now put a person on a schedule of unpredictable warmth into that arithmetic. They create the strain, through the cold stretch. They control its length, and you cannot predict it, which supplies the uncertainty. And then they end it. What arrives in you at that moment is relief of exactly the kind the ankle produced, scaled to a strain that lasted days and that you had no way of knowing would stop, and it lands as the best you have felt all month. A kind person cannot produce that. They never created the strain, so they can never end it. What they offer is a level, and the level is genuinely good, and it registers as almost nothing, because your gauge is calibrated to changes.

This is also why the relief feels like proof. A signal that big must be measuring something big, and the nearest big thing is the person who ended the strain, so the signal gets attributed to them. It was measuring the wait.

The same relief runs a related behavior, [testing the people who love you](/patterns/why-do-i-test-people-who-love-me/), where the strain is manufactured by the reader rather than supplied by somebody else, and that page covers the manufactured version. Here the strain arrives from outside, and the question is why its ending holds you the way it does.

## What Are You Actually Attached To?

To the ending of the distress. The person is where the ending came from, and that is a different thing to be attached to than the person, even though from the inside they feel identical.

Here is the test that separates them. Think about the moment the warmth returned, the most recent time. What did you feel first? For most people running this pattern the honest answer is release rather than joy: the unclenching, the breath, the feeling of a weight coming off. Joy comes later, if it comes, and it is quieter. What you were most attached to, in that moment, was the weight coming off. And the weight was put there by the same person who took it off, which is the whole structure of the thing, because it means the only person who can produce your strongest feeling is the person producing your worst.

Follow that through and something important falls out of it. If what you are attached to is the ending of a distress, then a person who never causes you distress cannot compete, and it has nothing to do with how good they are. They have no weight to lift. They can be kind for years and never once produce the feeling of the weight coming off, because they never put it on. The comparison you have been running, between how much the difficult person moves you and how little the steady one does, was a comparison between a lifted weight and no weight at all rather than between two people, and no weight always loses that comparison, and always will, until the measure changes.

None of this says the feeling was not love. Love and a schedule can run inside the same relationship at the same time, and often do, and this page has no way of knowing which parts of yours were which. What it can say is that the size of the pull settles nothing either way, because unpredictability alone produces pulls that size in every learner ever tested.

There is a version of this pattern where the poor treatment matches a verdict you already hold about yourself, and staying with it is easier than the open question of being alone. That is a different root with a different page, [feeling unworthy of love](/patterns/why-do-i-feel-unworthy-of-love/), and if the phrase "a match is easier to hold than a contradiction" lands harder than anything here, that page is where to go.

## What If This Was Simply an Intense Relationship That Ended Badly?

Then that is what it was, and it belongs first, before any of the mechanisms, because not every painful relationship was a pattern and a page like this one can make everything look like one.

Two people can be intense together and fail. They can have a real thing, with real warmth that arrived mostly when you reached for it, and still end badly, through circumstance, through a mismatch neither of them could fix, through one hard year that neither of them survived as a pair. What that leaves is grief, and grief for an intense relationship is intense. It produces missing that feels disproportionate. It produces a pull. And it can make a kind person who arrives afterward feel flat, for the simple reason that you are still in the middle of something else.

Here is how this root reads differently from the ones below, so that you can check. The warmth in a relationship like this was mostly predictable. You knew, more or less, where you stood, and the cold stretches, if there were any, were rare and had reasons. The cruelty, if there was any, was occasional rather than rhythmic. And the pull is fading on its own, unevenly, but in one direction, with no returns of warmth to feed it. If that description fits, you are grieving a real thing that ended, and what you need is time and probably company, and none of the machinery below applies to you. The page on [what happens after it ended](/patterns/after-it-ended/) covers that territory properly, including how long the body takes.

The reason this root goes first is that it is the generous reading, and generous readings deserve to be tested before suspicious ones. Somebody who has just been through a hard ending deserves to be asked whether they were running a schedule, and then believed when they say no, rather than told.

## What If the Unpredictability Was Doing the Work Rather Than the Person?

This is the central root, the one the principle above describes directly, and unlike the others it can be tested against your own history rather than felt about.

The claim is specific. The pull you feel toward this person was built mostly by the schedule on which their warmth arrived, and a different person running the same schedule would have built a similar pull, while this same person running a reliable schedule would have built a much weaker one. The person, in other words, was the occasion. The unpredictability was the cause.

That sounds cold, and it is meant to, because it is also freeing, and the freeing part only works if you take the cold part seriously. If the schedule built the pull, then the pull is information about the schedule rather than about how remarkable the person was, and the sense that nobody else will ever produce that feeling is accurate only in the sense that nobody else running a reliable schedule will. Which is most kind people. Which is, on reflection, the point.

How to test it. Reconstruct the last eight or ten times the warmth returned. Do it in writing, in order, and for each return note three things. First, how long the cold stretch before it lasted. Second, whether anything about the day would have let you predict that the warmth was coming. Third, how big the relief was, honestly, on a scale you would be embarrassed to show anybody.

If the cold stretches vary in length with no pattern, if you could not have predicted a single return, and if the size of the relief tracks the length of the wait rather than anything the person actually did on the day, then the schedule was doing the work. That is what a reinforcement schedule looks like when you write one out. The person's specific qualities barely appear in it.

If instead the returns were fairly predictable, if the cold stretches had reasons you could name, and if the relief was roughly constant regardless of the wait, then the schedule was weak and the pull is coming from somewhere else, and the next three roots are where to look.

One caution on the test. Memory under this pattern is selective in a particular direction: it keeps the returns, which were vivid, and loses the cold, which was long and featureless. Most people who write the timeline out are startled by the arithmetic. The returns they remember as frequent turn out to be seven weeks apart. Write it out rather than recalling it, and trust the dates over the feeling.

## What If Intensity Is the Only Measure of Care You Have Ever Used?

Some people arrive at this pattern with no schedule in their history at all. What they have instead is a gauge.

Somewhere along the way, and it can be as simple as the first serious relationship having been a dramatic one, the amount a relationship moved you became the measure of how much it mattered. Big feelings meant a big thing. Small feelings meant a small thing. And that gauge was never checked against anything else, because it did not seem like a gauge. It seemed like the feeling itself.

A person carrying that gauge will read the difficult person as more significant on first contact, with no training by unpredictable warmth required, because the difficult person produces more movement, and they will read the kind person as less significant for the same reason. No schedule is needed. The pull here is a measurement error rather than a conditioning effect, and it feels identical from inside.

Here is how it reads differently. The timeline test above comes back weak: the warmth was fairly predictable and the pull is still large. Intensity has been the deciding factor in every relationship you have chosen, and not only the romantic ones. The friendships you rate highest are the dramatic ones. You have described a calm stretch, in any relationship, as something being missing. And a steady person has felt flat to you before this one, more than once, with different steady people.

That last item is the tell. A schedule produces flatness toward one particular kind person, the one who arrived after a particular difficult one. A gauge produces flatness toward all of them.

If this is yours, the mechanism section of this page is interesting and beside the point. What needs work is the gauge, and the honest news is that a gauge changes through use rather than through insight. You start measuring by something other than movement, badly at first, over months, with the old gauge complaining the whole time, until the new one becomes readable. The table of intensity and closeness markers above is one place to start reading from. The page on [attracting the same people](/insights/why-do-i-attract-the-same-people/) covers a different route into the same flatness, where the calm person feels flat because a familiar shape is missing rather than because the gauge only reads movement, and the two are worth telling apart.

## What If the Reward Was the Relief Rather Than the Person?

This root and the schedule root are cousins, and it is worth separating them because they need different things.

Under the schedule root, what holds you is persistence: you keep reaching because reaching sometimes worked and a gap never proved anything. Under this root, what holds you is the relief itself, as a feeling you have come to need, and the person is simply the most reliable way you have found of producing it.

That distinction shows up in what the pull is like between returns. Under the schedule, the between-time is waiting, and waiting is mostly about the person: where they are, whether today is the day. Under this root the between-time is a kind of flatness that has nothing in particular to do with them, a sense that nothing is happening, that life has gone quiet, and the difficult person is attractive precisely because they end that quiet. People running this root often notice that they have felt the same flatness in other settings entirely, at work, in a long weekend with nothing planned, and that they have reached for other strain-and-release patterns to end it before this one: a fight, a deadline, a risk. The person is the current supplier of a feeling you were already in the market for.

Here is how it reads differently. The relief when they return is the point, and the relief fades within a day into something closer to boredom. You have found yourself, on a calm week with them, slightly disappointed. You have provoked a cold stretch, once or twice, not on purpose exactly, and felt the return more sharply for it. And the steady person feels flat in a way that is less about them and more about the absence of anything to be relieved from.

If this is yours, the work sits underneath the relationship rather than in it. What needs finding is what the relief has been standing in for, because a feeling you go to that much trouble to produce is standing in for something, and until you can name it you will find another supplier the moment this one stops.

## What If the Shape Was Already Familiar Before You Met Them?

Some people met this schedule long before they met this person.

If warmth, early in your life, arrived on a rhythm you could not predict, if the people whose care you depended on were sometimes fully present and sometimes gone, with no signal in advance and no explanation after, then you learned the schedule as the shape of care itself. You learned that reaching sometimes works. You learned that a gap proves nothing and that the return, when it comes, is the best feeling there is. You learned it thoroughly, at an age when learning is thorough, and you learned it about care in general rather than about any particular person.

An adult who runs the same schedule then feels, from inside, less like a warning and more like recognition. This is what care feels like. The unpredictability itself registers as familiar, and familiar registers as right, and a kind person whose warmth is reliable registers as foreign, because reliability was never part of the shape you know. Their steadiness is a language you were never taught, and it reads as an absence of language.

Two things need saying about this root, and both matter. The first is that having it makes you neither damaged nor unusual. It makes you somebody who learned early what many people learn later, and who therefore had the pattern ready before an adult arrived to run it. The page on [growing up with an unpredictable parent](/patterns/growing-up-with-an-unpredictable-parent/) covers the learning itself in depth, from the child's side, and this page is about the adult pull that results, so I will hand the early part to it rather than repeat it. The second is that this root does not have to be present for the pull to form. The schedule works on everybody. Plenty of people with entirely predictable childhoods walk into an unpredictable adult and come out with the same pull, because the principle was demonstrated on creatures that had no childhood to speak of. Familiarity makes the pull faster and the steady person more foreign. On its own it creates nothing.

Here is how it reads differently. The timeline test comes back strong, and it would come back strong for earlier relationships too. The steady person feels foreign rather than flat: less boring than somehow wrong, as though they were doing care incorrectly. And the return of warmth after cold has a quality of homecoming to it that is hard to explain to anybody who did not grow up inside that rhythm.

If this is yours, the pull toward this particular person is the least of it, and the page can only point. What needs finding is the earlier schedule, and then the slow business of learning that reliability is a form of care rather than the absence of one, which takes longer than anybody wants and works. The page on [anxious attachment](/patterns/anxious-attachment-style/) covers the standing forecast that often grows out of the same early rhythm, and the two travel together more often than either travels alone.

## How Would You Tell Which of These Is Yours?

Not by feeling about it, because every one of the five feels the same from inside. By the history, and by what the pull does when it is written down.

Start with the timeline, because it is the one test that separates the central root from everything else. Ten returns of warmth, in order, each with the length of the cold before it, whether you could have seen it coming, and the size of the relief. Then ask the four questions the other roots turn on.

Was the warmth mostly predictable, and is the pull fading in one direction on its own? Then you are probably grieving something real that ended, and the mechanisms here are beside the point.

Has a steady person felt flat to you before, with different steady people, and have you rated every relationship in your life by how much it moved you? Then the gauge is doing the work, and it would do the same work under any schedule.

Does the relief fade within a day into something like boredom, and have you felt that boredom elsewhere and reached for other strain-and-release patterns to end it? Then the relief is the thing you are attached to, and the person is its current supplier.

Did care arrive on this rhythm before you were old enough to name it, and does the steady person feel foreign rather than merely dull? Then the shape was familiar, and the adult pull is a recognition rather than a discovery.

Most people find they have more than one. The schedule and the familiar shape travel together very often, and the gauge sits underneath both of them more often than people expect. That is fine. The point of separating them is to know which one to work on first, because they need different things, and the thing that fixes one leaves the others exactly where they were. Reading the relief correctly dismantles the schedule root and does nothing for the gauge. Recalibrating the gauge does nothing for a relief you have come to need. And none of the three touches the familiar shape, which needs its own work in its own place.

A page can lay the five out. It cannot tell you which are yours, and a page that claimed to would be guessing on your behalf.

## Why Does Your Explanation Get Thinner the Longer You Talk?

Because most of what you had was a feeling of understanding rather than an understanding, and feelings of understanding do not survive being asked for details.

Rozenblit and Keil showed this in 2002, and the study is worth describing because it has nothing to do with relationships, which is why it is trustworthy here. They asked people to rate how well they understood ordinary objects, zippers, flush toilets, cylinder locks. People rated themselves fairly high. Then they asked them to write out, step by step, how the object actually worked, and to rate their understanding again. The ratings dropped, consistently, and the drop was the finding: people carry a sense of understanding that is considerably larger than the understanding itself, and the gap only becomes visible when they are made to produce the explanation. They named it the illusion of explanatory depth.

Applying that to explaining a person to your friend is my extension, and the original researchers were working on zippers, so weigh it accordingly. But the shape matches too well to leave out. You have a strong sense that you understand this person and why the relationship is what it is. That sense has been built mostly from the returns of warmth, which were vivid, and from a great deal of thinking in the gaps, which felt like understanding. When you are made to say it out loud, in order, to somebody who was not there, the sense has to become an account, and the account is thinner than the sense. You can hear it thinning. Your friend can hear it too, and is usually kind enough to say nothing.

What that thinning is telling you is that the account came second. The pull was built on the schedule, on the relief, on the gauge, on the shape, on whichever of the roots above is yours, and none of those are the kind of thing that fits into an explanation to a friend, because none of them are about the person's qualities. The account was assembled afterward, to make the pull make sense. When you try to say it, you find out how much of it was assembled.

That is worth knowing for its own sake, and it is also useful. A pull that gets thinner when described is a pull that was built by something other than what it claims. That is the first honest reading you can take, and it costs one conversation. The page on [not being able to stop thinking about someone](/patterns/cant-stop-thinking-about-someone/) covers the version of this that forms on thin information, before there is any relationship to explain, and the two are different in what the friend is being asked to believe.

## Which Part of This Do You Actually Choose?

Not the pull. That needs saying without qualification, because people running this pattern spend a great deal of energy blaming themselves for a feeling that a schedule produced, and the blame changes nothing while costing a lot.

The pull is produced. Unpredictable warmth builds persistent reaching in every learner ever tested, and it built it in you, and the fact that you can now describe the mechanism does nothing to the mechanism. Relief will still be louder than contentment. The return of warmth will still land as the best you have felt in weeks. The kind person will still, for a while, read as almost nothing. None of that is chosen, and treating it as chosen is a way of adding a second weight to the first.

What is chosen is smaller and it is the whole workable surface. It is what happens in the hour after the warmth returns.

In that hour there are three decisions, and they are small enough to actually make. Whether you respond in the first minute or wait until the relief has finished being the loudest thing in the room. Whether you read the return as a verdict on the relationship, proof that it was real all along, or as one more unpredictable reward arriving on schedule, which is what the timeline says it is. And whether you tell anybody, because a return that gets described out loud to a friend, in the hour it arrives, is a return that has to survive being made into an account, and you already know what that does to accounts.

None of those three tell you to leave, or to stay, or to cut off contact. This page prescribes none of that, and would distrust itself if it did; the question of [whether to leave](/patterns/should-i-leave-my-relationship/) has its own page, with its own honesty about how little anyone else can decide it for you. What the three decisions do is put a gap between the relief and the response, and the gap is where the schedule starts to lose its grip, because a reward that has to wait an hour to be collected is a reward that gets examined on the way. The pull arrives on its own. The hour after it is yours.

## What Does This Look Like Once It Has Stopped Costing You?

Less dramatic than the pattern would have you expect, which is itself part of the answer.

Intensity still registers. A person who is warm on no schedule still produces a pull, because the mechanism has not gone anywhere and never will. What changes is what the pull is read as. It stops being the verdict and becomes a reading, the way a speedometer reading stops being an opinion about the journey once you know what a speedometer measures. You notice the pull, you know roughly what built it, and you have a fair idea of how much of it is the person and how much is the schedule, and the knowing changes what you do in the hour even while it changes nothing about the feeling.

The relief when somebody difficult is warm again gets smaller, and the way it gets smaller is instructive: mostly by your no longer waiting for it. A strain you were not counting the days of ends less loudly. The cold stretch stops being a cold stretch and becomes, slowly, just a stretch, and a stretch that ends produces a much smaller signal than a wait that ends.

And the steady person becomes readable. Not intense, because they were never going to be, and not suddenly thrilling, because that would be the old gauge reporting again. Readable. On the other measure, the one that runs on what you can say to them and what happens after you say it, they start returning a signal, and the signal grows over months rather than weeks, with the old gauge complaining the whole time that nothing is happening. Sometimes what emerges is a real fit. Sometimes it is a genuine no, arrived at honestly, on the right measure. Both count as this pattern having stopped costing you.

None of it requires the difficult relationship to have been fake, or the feeling to have been wrong, or you to have been foolish. It requires a second gauge, read for long enough to trust.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Longer than the pattern's own timeline, and that is the schedule again rather than a failure of yours.

Two clocks run here, and they run at different speeds. The first is the pull toward the specific person, and this is the clock the reinforcement principle describes directly. Behavior learned under unpredictable reward is, by the principle's own definition, the behavior that takes longest to fade once the reward stops, because a gap never proved anything before and does not start proving anything now. Worse, it usually intensifies briefly first. The weeks right after the last return of warmth are, for most people, the worst of the whole pull, arriving exactly when they expected relief, and that intensification is one of the most reliable findings in the field rather than a sign that they cannot live without the person. It passes. Then the decline is uneven, over months, with sudden returns triggered by anything that resembles the old signal, a name, a place, the particular hour of the evening when the messages used to come. It fades. It fades slowly and in steps, and each step down feels like a plateau until the next one.

The second clock is the gauge, and this one runs slower still, because a gauge changes through use rather than through insight. Reading a steady person on the closeness measure instead of the intensity one is something you do badly for a long time before you do it well, and the first months of it are mostly the sensation of nothing happening. People who have done it describe somewhere between a season and a year before the second measure feels like it is reporting anything, and longer before they trust it over the first.

I would rather give you those numbers than the ones the pattern hands out, which are that it should have faded by now and that the fact it has not means something. It means the schedule was doing exactly what schedules do. That is all it means.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From published research anybody can check, and from work I have done myself, and the two should be weighted very differently.

The reinforcement principle at the center of the page comes from mid-century learning research on schedules of reward, a body of work built by many researchers over decades, most of it on animals working for food, and I have named the principle rather than a person because it belongs to the line of work rather than to any one study. Humphreys published the early human demonstration in 1939, in the Journal of Experimental Psychology, conditioning an eyelid response and finding that intermittent reinforcement produced markedly slower extinction than continuous reinforcement. Later human work found the effect real but more variable, and sensitive to what participants had been told. Holt-Lunstad, Uchino and colleagues published their work on ambivalent ties and ambulatory blood pressure in the early 2000s, in Psychosomatic Medicine and related journals, finding higher reactivity during interactions with people who were both supportive and upsetting than with people who were simply negative; those names belong to one research group and should be read as one line of evidence. Rozenblit and Keil published the illusion of explanatory depth in Cognitive Science in 2002, on people's understanding of ordinary objects collapsing when they were asked to produce the explanation.

Applying any of this to an adult relationship is my extension, and I have said so at each point where it happens. Nobody has run the reinforcement work on a relationship, the blood pressure work measures cost rather than attachment, and the explanatory depth work was done on zippers.

The rest of it is mine and carries no research weight whatsoever. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, and none of the researchers named above has any connection to this page or to anything I run. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.

## What Is Worth Doing This Week, Before You Decide Anything About Anybody?

The timeline, and nothing else.

Ten returns of warmth, in order, in writing. For each one: how long the cold before it lasted, whether you could have predicted the day, and how big the relief was, on a scale you would not show anybody. Change no behavior while you do it. Do not decide anything about the relationship, do not go quiet, do not make any announcement, and above all do not use the timeline as ammunition in a conversation with the person, because it is a reading of you rather than a case against them.

At the end you will have a piece of paper that says one of two things. Either the returns land on no pattern and the relief scaled with the wait, in which case the schedule was doing most of the work, and you now know the size of the pull was measuring the wait and never the person. Or the warmth was fairly predictable and the pull is still this large, in which case the schedule is weak and one of the other four roots is where the pull lives, and the timeline has just saved you from working on the wrong thing.

Then take the second reading, the one that costs a conversation. Explain the person to a friend, out loud, and listen for the thinning. If the account holds, that is information. If it thins, that is information too, and it is the more common result.

If both readings come back saying the schedule and the relief, the page has done what it can, and what remains is the hour after the next return, which is yours. If they come back pointing underneath, at the gauge, at the relief you have come to need, at a shape you knew before you met them, then what needs work sits below the relationship rather than in it, and no amount of managing your behavior toward one person will reach it.

[Our Sacred Valley seminar](https://florianrolke.com/the-soulsearching-experience/) works at that layer. We do not start with the person, because you can already describe them, and you have just heard what happens to that description under pressure. We start with which of the roots on this page is yours, because a principle can be looked up in a minute and the root cannot, and as Florian puts it, "once you know the real reason, you can actually get the solution based on that reason." A solution built on the wrong root is the one you have probably already tried. Then we look at what stands in the way of the second gauge, and build a plan you are held accountable to afterward, back in your own week, with your own phone, on the evenings when the old signal used to arrive.
