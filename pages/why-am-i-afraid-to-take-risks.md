# Why am I afraid to try anything new?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Fear of new experiences treats the unfamiliar itself as the threat, before any outcome is on the table. That is what separates it from fear of failing, and it is why the same flinch shows up for a new restaurant and a new career. It usually forms where something arrived without warning once. It moves through small, survivable repetitions rather than through insight.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
See whether any of these turn up in your own voice before an unfamiliar thing.

- "Let's just go to the usual place."
- "I'll look into it properly first."
- "New experiences are unpredictable; they might be dangerous."
- "Opening up to new people is too risky."

Each one passes for good judgment from the inside, and together they quietly shrink the territory you are willing to enter.

[How the seminar works with the flinch before anything unfamiliar](https://florianrolke.com/the-soulsearching-experience/)
</div>

There is a tab open in your browser that has been there for three weeks. It is a job listing that would genuinely be a step up, and every few days you read the same four bullet points again and close the laptop. Nothing about the listing has changed in three weeks. Nothing about your reasons has changed either, mostly because you have never quite finished forming one.

The same shape turns up in smaller places, which is where it is easier to see. A friend group votes on a restaurant nobody has tried, and there is a small pull to lobby for the usual place instead. A partner says let's just drive somewhere this weekend, and it lands as stress rather than fun, and a reason to stay in arrives within about four seconds. A hobby class gets bookmarked, priced, read about for two months, and never booked. Someone at work volunteers first for the new project and there is a quiet wave of relief that it was not you.

If that string of small moments is familiar, the thing underneath it is more specific than most writing on the subject allows, and the specificity is what makes it workable. One plain line before anything else: where this traces back to something that once overwhelmed you, and where unfamiliar situations bring back memories of harm, panic that will not settle, or a blankness you cannot think your way out of, that belongs with a licensed professional rather than with a page like this one. The same holds alongside an addiction, a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, a hopelessness that has not lifted for months, or any thought of harming yourself, however quickly it passes. The rest of this is built for the everyday version: the tab left open, the class never booked, the restaurant nobody chose.

## What Does the Fear of Anything New Sound Like From the Inside?

It sounds reasonable. That is the first and largest problem with it.

The sentences below are the lines people run in their own heads about anything untried, in their own phrasing rather than a tidied-up version of it. They recur with an unnerving consistency across people who have never met and whose lives look nothing alike, which is the first clue that this is something learned rather than something anyone simply is.

- "My past experiences define my future relationships."
- "New relationships are risky; they might not understand my need for independence."
- "Commitments could limit my personal freedom."
- "Unknown experiences are just opportunities for control."

That last one deserves a translation, because it is the one people skim past. It means opportunities for somebody else to hold the reins. An unfamiliar situation is one where you do not yet know the rules, and not knowing the rules is the exact condition under which the original injury tended to arrive.

Underneath those sits a second set, and this one is harder to catch because every line in it passes as good sense at any dinner table in the world.

- "What's the catch?"
- "I'm just being sensible."
- "People are either completely trustworthy or not at all."
- "That worked out for them, and they got lucky."

Read both lists slowly. What matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word, in your own phrasing, with no translation required.

Notice how few of them sound like fear. Not one of them says I am frightened. They say prudent, experienced, realistic, independent, careful with my time. That is a large part of why this can run for twenty years without ever being examined, and why nobody in it goes looking for help with it. Nobody seeks treatment for being sensible.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, before anything practical, because this boundary matters more than every technique further down.

Where this pattern formed around something that overwhelmed you, the ordinary advice about trying small new things can make it worse rather than better. A nervous system still running the original threat response does not collect a corrective experience when it is pushed into the unfamiliar. It collects another bad one. If unfamiliar situations reliably bring back memories of being hurt, if you lose time or go blank, if panic arrives with a physical force that has nothing to do with the size of the thing in front of you, the pacing of this work belongs with a licensed clinician, and the sequence matters: some separate work on the threat response first, then the exposure.

The same holds for anything running alongside it. An addiction, a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, a hopelessness that has not shifted in months, or any thought of harming yourself, however briefly it passes through, all belong with a professional rather than with a self-directed practice. Asking in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve, and it costs nothing at all to ask.

Everything below is written for the version that is uncomfortable rather than dangerous. Most people reading this are in that version. Some are not, and the difference is worth being honest with yourself about before you start.

## Where Does the Fear of New Experiences Actually Come From?

From five fairly different places, each of which arrives at the same conclusion by its own route.

That matters more than it sounds, because the route determines what actually helps. Two people can be equally unable to book the class and need almost opposite things.

| Where it formed | What got learned | How it shows up now |
|---|---|---|
| A trust that broke | Anything not yet tested counts as unverified rather than neutral | New people get screened for far longer than the situation warrants, and warmth arrives late |
| Autonomy that was restricted | Trying something unapproved carries a repercussion | A pause before any choice that has not been sanctioned by somebody, real or imagined |
| Autonomy that was fought for | Choosing the unfamiliar never got practiced on your own terms | The untried stays untested territory decades later, even with nobody left to answer to |
| Evaluation that could never be satisfied | Untried is safer than attempted and found wanting | A visibly competent person declining things they would probably be good at |
| Something that arrived without warning | Novelty itself is the threat category, whatever is inside it | A physical charge at unfamiliar situations, before any content is even known |

The trust route generalizes outward from one specific break. One thing turned out to be unsafe, so untested becomes the category, and untested covers almost everything worth doing. [What that widening looks like across a whole life has its own page.](/patterns/trust-issues/)

The two autonomy routes look identical from outside and run on opposite engines. Where the restriction was enforced and complied with, what got learned was a hesitation to try new things out of fear of repercussions, and the fear outlives the person who supplied it. Where the restriction was fought, the child spent their energy on resisting rather than on choosing, so they still never got to pick something unfamiliar on their own terms and find out it was survivable. Either way the practice never happened. [What that kind of household does to a person is worked through here.](/patterns/controlling-parents/)

The evaluation routes, criticism that could not be satisfied and feeling routinely dismissed, both name the driver as fear of failure. They describe one childhood arrangement: everything gets graded, the grade is either impossible to earn or waved away, and the only reliable way to avoid a bad grade is to hand in nothing. [Fear of failing sits close enough to this that the two get merged constantly, and it has a page of its own.](/patterns/fear-of-failure/)

The fifth route is the bluntest. A heightened fear of the unknown, often made worse by genuinely frightening events, describes a nervous system that got overwhelmed once by something it did not see coming and reorganized around novelty as the threat category, rather than around any particular danger inside it.

What all five share is a substitution, and it is the most useful thing on this page. The original injury has already happened. It cannot be prevented now, in hindsight, by any amount of vigilance. The next unfamiliar thing has not happened yet. Declining it feels like the one remaining place where prevention is still possible, so that is where all the protective energy goes, aimed at a future that was never the problem.

## What Is Refusing the New Thing Actually Protecting?

Against being blindsided a second time, which is a more precise fear than it looks.

An experience you have never had carries an unknown, unmeasured risk. Declining it removes the possibility of a repeat of the moment when harm arrived without warning, and removing that possibility is worth an enormous amount to a system that has been through it once. From inside, this does not register as caution. It registers as the only sane response to a world that has already demonstrated what happens when you do not see something coming.

There is a second layer, and it is quieter. What is never attempted cannot be judged. It cannot be failed in front of anybody, and it cannot be added to the file of evidence the original wound already opened against you. Anyone who grew up being graded understands this arithmetic without ever having done it consciously. A thing not begun stays at an indefinite score, and an indefinite score beats a bad one.

For the branch that formed around restricted autonomy, there is a third layer that is more specific still. The protection is a working theory the person had to build in order to get through childhood, which is that safety comes from staying inside what is already known and managed, rather than testing a variable nobody has controlled for yet. That theory was accurate at the time. It was probably the best available reading of the situation. It just kept running after the situation ended, which is what every protective pattern does and why arguing with one logically has never worked for anybody.

## Is This Fear of Failing, or Fear of the Unfamiliar Itself?

Worth settling early, because five fairly different things produce the same visible behavior, which is declining to try, and the useful response to each one differs completely.

| What you are seeing | What it might actually be | The thing that tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Declining a challenge you might not win | Fear of failing | The fear points at the result. Ask whether you would do it if success were guaranteed. Fear of failing says yes immediately |
| Declining anything not arranged in advance | A need for control | Control is about arranging conditions so outcomes stay predictable. This shows up before any arranging is possible, when the option itself is still unmapped |
| Fewer new things, chosen deliberately | Introversion | Introversion sorts by stimulation, not by novelty. An introvert will happily try an unfamiliar thing quietly and alone. This declines the quiet version too |
| Weighing a genuinely risky choice and passing | Ordinary caution | Caution is proportional and stays bounded to real stakes. This grips a new coffee order as hard as a career change |
| No pull toward the new thing at all | Genuine disinterest | Real disinterest is quiet and the thing leaves your mind. This keeps the thing circling, researched, priced, bookmarked, never begun |
| Dread before an unfamiliar situation with no content yet | This pattern | The charge arrives before you know anything about what is inside. There is no outcome to be afraid of yet, and the fear is already running |

The first row saves people the most time, and it is where nearly every article on this subject stops. Fear of failing is about the outcome: will I be good at this, will I lose, will the result embarrass me. Fear of the unfamiliar is about the lack of a map, before any outcome is on the table. Someone with this pattern can be highly competent, privately confident they would do well at the new thing, and still decline it, because the score was never the object of the fear.

Run the test in that row honestly. Imagine the new thing, then imagine being told in advance that it will go fine. If the resistance drops away, you are looking at fear of failing and [the page on that is the better one for you](/patterns/fear-of-failure/). If the resistance is still sitting there unchanged, attached to the unfamiliarity itself, you are in the right place.

The control row matters nearly as much. The two travel together constantly, because declining the unfamiliar thing is one effective way of keeping conditions predictable, and plenty of people carry both. They are still separable. A person can have this pattern with none of the compulsive arranging, no color-coded calendar, no checking of other people's work, and simply a flat refusal of anything unmapped. If the arranging is the louder half for you, [the grip itself is worked through separately](/patterns/why-do-i-need-to-control-everything/), as is [the need for things to feel settled before you can rest](/patterns/need-for-certainty/).

## Why Does the Research Never End?

Because research feels like movement while keeping you entirely outside the unfamiliar situation, which is the exact trade this pattern exists to make.

Watch the sequence on any decision that would open something new. A job change, a move, a first date, a course, a different way of working. It gets researched. Then researched more. Reviews get read, forums get skimmed, three people get asked their opinion, a spreadsheet appears. Months pass in which more information genuinely does arrive, and then months pass in which no new information arrives at all and the researching continues anyway.

The tell is that point. There is a moment where additional research stops changing anything about the decision, and if the researching continues past it, the researching has become a way of rehearsing the outcome in a controlled setting, your own head, instead of finding out in real time. Rehearsal is safe, and it is the only activity that can be performed indefinitely without ever producing exposure.

There is a cruelty in that, because from the outside and from the inside it looks like diligence. Nobody criticizes the person who did their homework. The hobby class that got priced, compared, and read about for two months produced a small ongoing sense of progress the entire time, which is precisely why it could run that long without alarming anybody, including you.

The same mechanism explains something that confuses people about themselves: why understanding this pattern completely does almost nothing to change it. Reading about it is another form of preparation. [Why insight so reliably fails to move behavior is its own subject](/patterns/why-cant-i-change/), and it applies here with unusual force, because this is a pattern that can absorb unlimited understanding without ever having to do anything.

## Where Does It Hide When You Would Not Call It Fear?

In seven arenas, and most people recognize themselves in two or three at first and find the rest weeks later.

| Where it shows up | What it looks like from outside | The sentence underneath |
|---|---|---|
| With a new relationship | Careful, slow, months before anything real gets said, warmth held one step back | "My past experiences define my future relationships." |
| With friends and weekend plans | Lobbying gently for the known restaurant, the unplanned drive declined with a good reason | "Let's just go to the usual place." |
| At work | Excellent inside a mapped role, quietly absent from anything untested, relieved when someone else volunteers | "I'll look into it properly first." |
| With tools and methods | The old software, the old process, kept long past the point it stopped working well | "I'm just being sensible." |
| With offers and invitations | A spontaneous offer read for the angle before it is read for the content | "What's the catch?" |
| With commitment | A decision that would close other doors left open indefinitely, options kept alive at real cost | "Commitments could limit my personal freedom." |
| With your own growth | Courses, groups, therapy, retreats researched at length and never entered | "New experiences are unpredictable; they might be dangerous." |

The tools row lands hardest for people who read the relationship rows and concluded this was somebody else's problem. Sticking with a method that has stopped working, purely because it is known, is the same pattern with none of the emotional content attached, which makes it the cleanest evidence available. Nobody has ever been betrayed by a piece of software. If the reluctance turns up there too, it is about unfamiliarity rather than about people.

The growth row produces a specific kind of quiet frustration, because it means the pattern can block the very thing that would move it. Avoiding opportunities for growth in order to stay inside a familiar environment is a closed loop by design. It is also why so many people arrive at a course or a retreat having researched it for a year, which is a better sign than it feels like at the time.

## Why Does It Feel Like Realism Rather Than Fear?

Because a guarded reading of the world is genuinely more accurate than a naive one, some of the time, and being right occasionally is all a pattern needs to keep itself alive.

Several habits hold this in place and reinforce each other. Optimism in other people gets read as naive rather than as enviable. Good experiences get filed as exceptions rather than as evidence, so they never accumulate. Stories of things working out get treated as inapplicable, which is a category with no membership requirements and therefore no limit. Trust runs in two settings, complete or none, and since complete trust is unavailable to almost anybody, the setting stays at none by default. A cynical read gets called realism, and realism is a compliment.

The result is a system that cannot be updated by good news. Every corrective experience gets reclassified on arrival as a fluke or somebody else's luck, while every confirming experience gets counted in full. Thirty years of mostly fine outcomes can be supplied to a ledger that only records one column.

Watching somebody else take an unfamiliar risk and have it pay off tends to land badly for exactly this reason, and people are often ashamed of that reaction. Their result appears to disprove a rule you have organized a life around and paid real costs to follow. Disproof of an expensive rule is uncomfortable, even in somebody else's life, and especially there.

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. The thing I underestimated longest was how good the reasoning is from the inside. Every single decline had a defensible case attached to it, and I could have argued any one of them successfully in front of a room. What I could not do was explain the pattern across all of them, which is the only level where this is visible at all. Where it sits for me today is worth saying plainly: the flinch before an unfamiliar thing has shrunk a lot over the years, it has not gone to zero, and I would rather tell you that than sell a finished version of something nobody finishes.

## Why Does Relief Show Up When a Plan Falls Through?

Because relief is the honest answer to a question you were not asking out loud.

When a new plan gets canceled and what arrives is a small lift rather than disappointment, that lift is information. It means some part of you was dreading the unfamiliar step more than it wanted whatever the plan was going to produce. The evening was going to be interesting, the class was going to be useful, the trip was going to be good, and the cancellation still felt better than the plan did. Nothing about a scheduling conflict explains that.

This is the most reliable marker of the pattern available to you, and it is free. It requires no self-analysis and no honesty about your own motives, both of which this pattern is unusually good at defeating. It requires noticing which direction the feeling went in the two seconds after the message arrived.

The same signal shows up at work. A new project gets announced, somebody else volunteers, and there is a wave of relief. Notice that nothing bad was going to happen to you either way, and nobody was going to be assigned it against their will. The relief comes from somewhere else: a door that was briefly open has closed again, safely, without you having had to be the one who closed it.

## What Does This Actually Cost Over Ten Years?

The territory shrinks quietly, and the shrinking is nearly impossible to notice from inside, because each individual decline is so small.

No single skipped restaurant costs anything, and no single deferred job listing costs anything measurable either. Here is the honest part: for any given decision, the cautious choice is frequently fine and occasionally correct. The cost sits entirely in the aggregate, and nobody experiences an aggregate directly. What people experience instead, usually somewhere in their thirties or forties, is a narrowing sensation they cannot source, a sense that life has become the way they already do things and that the doing has stopped producing much.

Three specific costs are worth naming, because vague ones motivate nobody.

1. **Staying costs less than leaving, every single time, forever.** Staying in a job, a city, or a relationship requires entering no unfamiliar territory at all. Leaving requires entering nothing but. When the discomfort of the unknown outweighs the discomfort of an unsatisfying known, the known wins by default, and it wins without a decision ever being made. Years pass in which nobody chose anything.
2. **The corrective data never arrives.** This pattern is maintained by the absence of contrary evidence, and declining the unfamiliar thing is precisely what prevents the evidence from being collected. Each decline makes the next one slightly more likely, because the case for the unknown being dangerous stands unchallenged for another month.
3. **Somebody else carries the appetite.** In couples and friendships, the person who is willing to go first ends up supplying all the novelty for both. That works for a while and then it becomes a thing they say out loud, usually badly, usually during an argument about something else.

What people describe with real regret, later, is rarely a specific unfamiliar thing they declined. It is the wanting itself going quiet. [The version of this where the wanting has already gone quiet has a page of its own](/patterns/why-did-i-give-up-on-my-dreams/), and it is worth reading if the flinch has been running long enough that you can no longer tell what you would choose if it stopped.

## What Does Enthusiasm for New Experiences Actually Look Like?

Concrete enough that you could film it, and considerably less adventurous than the word enthusiasm suggests.

This is a polarity with two ends, and describing only the painful end leaves you with an accurate account of your problem and nowhere to walk. The other end has nothing to do with skydiving. It describes a person for whom an unfamiliar option registers first as interesting, and who then checks the practical details in service of doing the thing rather than in search of a reason not to. Here is that end, translated out of abstraction and into an ordinary Saturday.

| The fearful end | The enthusiastic end | What it looks like on an ordinary Saturday |
|---|---|---|
| The unfamiliar registers as a threat to be pre-screened | The unfamiliar registers as interesting | You read the class description once and book it |
| Checking details to find a reason not to go | Checking details in service of going | You look up the parking and the start time, then go |
| New people take an unusually long time to trust | New people take a normal amount of time | You accept a second invitation from someone you met last month |
| Relief when a plan falls through | Mild interest, or ordinary disappointment | A canceled plan leaves a gap in the day rather than a weight off your chest |
| Months of research before a reversible choice | An afternoon | You order the unfamiliar thing on the menu and find out |
| A failure proves the whole territory was dangerous | A failure is information about this one attempt | The class turns out mediocre, so you try a different one in March |
| Spontaneity is stressful | Spontaneity is fine, sometimes preferred | Someone says let's drive somewhere and you say yes |
| Guardedness reads to you as realism | Openness reads to you as ordinary | You have no particular position on other people's optimism |
| Decisions must survive an internal cross-examination | Decisions get made and then lived with | The choice takes twenty minutes because the stakes are actually small |

Read the right-hand column carefully, because it is deliberately unimpressive. Nobody in it is doing anything brave. The stakes have not gone up and the person has not become fearless. What changed is that a decision no longer has to survive a cross-examination before it is allowed to happen, which frees up a startling amount of a week.

The felt difference, day to day, is closer to appetite than to courage. New things pull rather than having to be pushed toward. That is the actual destination, and aiming at the appetite works better than aiming at the bravery, because bravery is exhausting and appetite is self-sustaining.

Do not judge yourself if that column currently reads as slightly absurd. The distance took years to build and nobody crosses it in a weekend.

## What Actually Moves This?

Repeated, survivable contact with small unfamiliar things, accumulated over months. Insight alone leaves this exactly where it found it, and so does deciding to be different.

The mechanism is evidence accumulation, and understanding it correctly is what stops people quitting in week two. A nervous system that reorganized around the unknown being where danger lives needs enough contrary data points to update its baseline. The untried restaurant that was fine. The honest conversation that did not end the relationship. The new hobby that turned out to be merely mediocre rather than humiliating. None of those does much on its own, which is exactly why people try one new thing, feel absolutely nothing shift, and conclude the whole approach is useless. One data point against a decade of accumulated evidence should not move anything. It is not supposed to.

The route it formed through changes what to do about it, which is why the formation table above was worth reading carefully.

Where it is rooted in something overwhelming, the exposure needs pacing, and it usually needs some separate work on the threat response first. Pushing into novelty while still flooded produces another bad data point rather than a corrective one, which sets the whole project backward. This is the branch where professional support is doing real work rather than being a disclaimer.

Where it is rooted in restricted autonomy or in evaluation that could never be satisfied, the lever is smaller and more specific. Practice making a reversible decision entirely alone, without checking it against anybody's imagined reaction, and let the result belong completely to you. Good or bad, it is yours. That is the exact experience that never happened, and it turns out to be the one that matters, more than any amount of being told you are allowed to choose things.

Is this slow? Yes. Realistically it is months of low-stakes practice before the reflexive flinch softens at all, and longer before the bigger decisions, the jobs and moves and relationships, stop demanding the months-long research ritual first. It is unglamorous and freaking repetitive, and it is the thing that holds when a single decisive moment does not.

## How Do You Practice Without Blowing Anything Up?

By running repetitions small enough that you will actually complete them, in places where nothing is riding on the outcome.

1. **Pick things with no downside on purpose.** The point of the early repetitions is the unfamiliarity, not the stakes. An unfamiliar route home, a dish you have never ordered, a shop you have walked past for years. If it feels trivial, it is correctly sized, because trivial is what makes it repeatable and repeatable is the whole mechanism.
2. **Set a research ceiling before you start looking.** Twenty minutes, or three sources, decided in advance. When the ceiling is reached you either do the thing or you drop it. This separates deciding from rehearsing more cleanly than anything else on the list.
3. **Make one reversible decision entirely alone this week.** No consultation, no checking it against what somebody would think. Reversible is the important word: something you could undo by Friday. The aim is the experience of having chosen.
4. **Record the result the same day, in one line.** Fine, mediocre, good, bad. This sounds like homework and it is the part that does the work, because this pattern discards good outcomes automatically. Written down, they are harder to discard, and after a month you have a page of evidence from your own life rather than from an article.
5. **Say yes before the second hurdle.** If you tend to accept and then cancel, treat the acceptance as the easy half and put something small in the way of canceling: tell one person you are going, or book it so that leaving costs a little.
6. **Notice the direction of the feeling when a plan changes.** Relief or disappointment, one word, no analysis. You are building a record of where the pattern actually runs, which is often somewhere you would not have guessed.
7. **Let one thing be witnessed.** Once the solo repetitions are unremarkable, do one unfamiliar thing where somebody can watch you be a beginner at it. That is a separate skill, and for many people it is the harder half.
8. **Leave the outcome unranked.** When something new goes well, resist calling it luck. When it goes badly, keep the conclusion the size of the event: this class was mediocre, rather than this proves the point about new things. The whole pattern lives in that second sentence.

Does the flinch disappear once you get good at this? Honestly, no. What grows is the gap between the flinch and the decision, and eventually the ability to feel the whole thing and go anyway. The alarm quiets down later, on its own, mostly because nothing bad kept happening.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Longer than a week, and the honest answer runs at two different speeds.

Recognition arrives fast. Most people can catch themselves declining something within a few weeks of paying deliberate attention, and catching it counts even on the days you decline anyway. Watching yourself construct a reasonable-sounding reason in real time, knowing exactly what you are doing, feels terrible and is a genuine step forward.

The reflex takes far longer. It was rehearsed thousands of times across years, and it fades at the pace of accumulated contrary experience rather than at the pace of understanding. Reading this page changes very little on its own. Forty small unfamiliar things attempted across four months, each one noted down afterward, changes a great deal.

Is that frustrating? Yes, and I would rather say so than sell a faster route that does not exist. The people who actually shift this are the ones still running unremarkable repetitions months after the insight stopped being interesting, at the point where it has become boring maintenance rather than a project.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Decide Anything Big?

Run a decline audit, and it takes about fifteen minutes.

Open a note and list every unfamiliar thing you declined, deferred, or researched without doing in the last ninety days. Be unsparing and include the small ones, because the small ones are where the evidence is: the restaurant, the invitation, the class, the listing, the conversation, the tool you did not switch to. Aim for at least ten entries. Most people find they have more than they expected within about four minutes, which is itself part of the finding.

Then go back through the list and write one word beside each one. Write **outcome** if what you were avoiding was the result: being bad at it, losing, being embarrassed by how it went. Write **arranging** if what you were avoiding was the loss of a predictable, managed situation. Write **unfamiliar** if, honestly, there was no outcome you were afraid of and no arrangement at stake, and the resistance was attached to the not-knowing itself. Then count the three columns. A list dominated by outcome points you at [fear of failing](/patterns/fear-of-failure/) and a different set of tools. A list dominated by arranging points at [the need to keep things controlled](/patterns/why-do-i-need-to-control-everything/). A list dominated by unfamiliar means this page was about you specifically, and it means the practice section above is the right place to start. Either way you now have something more useful than a feeling, which is a distribution, and it costs nothing but the fifteen minutes.
