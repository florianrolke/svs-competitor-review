# Why doesn't my partner feel loved when I am trying this hard?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Effort and registration run on separate ledgers. Giving heavily in one account, hours worked, problems solved, a rule kept for a year, credits nothing to a different account that happens to be the one running low. Volume is also the wrong unit. What a partner detects is attention, so the same gesture delivered while you monitor whether it landed arrives as absence.

Some of these will be yours, and one or two are what is being said back to you.

- "I have no idea what goes on."
- "From time to time I'll go through a spurt of trying to give more so that I'll get back."
- "My focus was on me, me. Why doesn't he love me? Why doesn't he care about me?"
- "I know that focusing on me, me, me, me, me is not the answer. I know I need to focus on this man that I love."

Years of effort that never registers leaves a residue, and the residue walks into the room slightly ahead of the next attempt.

You get home at eight from a day that genuinely took something out of you, and somewhere between the plates going down and the plates being cleared you are told that you were never really there. Not tonight in particular. For most of the last year.

Whatever you say next has already been said. That you work the hours you work for this family. That you have not missed a pickup since March. That you agreed to the thing you were arguing about last summer and you have kept to it since, without being reminded once. Every one of those is true, and not one of them is going to move the conversation an inch, which is what makes this specific kind of evening so bleak. You are producing evidence in a court that is not hearing that category of evidence.

Most people arrive at this question carrying one of two explanations, and both of them point in the wrong direction. The first is that the two of you are incompatible and this is what incompatibility looks like from the inside. The second is more private and more corrosive: that you are simply bad at this, cold somewhere in your wiring, missing whatever other people have. Each explanation has the same effect, which is to make the situation a fact about somebody's character rather than a problem with a shape.

There is a third explanation, and it is duller and considerably more useful. Your effort is real, large, and pointed at a target your partner is not currently short of. This page is written for the person on that side of it: the one who is genuinely trying, often to the point of exhaustion, and being told none of it counts. If the sentence that actually describes your week is that you give far more than you get and keep a private count of it, [the ledger has its own page](/patterns/why-am-i-always-the-one-who-cares-more/) and that is the one to read instead. If you are the partner who cannot feel the love that is clearly being sent, [that has its own mechanism too](/patterns/feeling-unloved/). A couple can each read their own side and meet in the middle, which usually goes better than one person reading both and reporting back.

## What Does This Look Like on an Ordinary Evening?

Small, undramatic, and repeated until it has worn a groove. The individual scenes are so minor that none of them would survive being described to a friend.

- You come home from a long, genuinely exhausting day and get told over dinner that you were never really there.
- You offer to fix the problem your partner just described and get a flat, unsatisfied "that's not what I meant" in return.
- You compliment something they made or did, and watch them wave it off as not good enough, again.
- Somewhere in an honest conversation you are handed a low score on a specific quality, how significant they feel, how loved, how much they are growing, when you thought things were basically fine.
- You agreed to a fixed limit on the thing you were fighting about a year ago, kept to it, and the argument never actually stopped. It went underground.
- You try something bigger than usual, an apology, a gift, an evening set aside, and the same flatness comes back from the other side of the table.

Read that list looking for the incident. There is no incident. That is precisely why this runs for years without either person being able to name it: nothing ever happens that is large enough to raise, and the accumulation is what people are actually living inside by the time one of them starts searching phrases like this one at eleven at night.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Before anything below, because this line matters more than every technique further down.

If warmth in your relationship is being used as a lever, restored when somebody backs down and withdrawn when they assert something, or if one person controls the money, the movements, or who the other is allowed to see, that is a different situation and nothing on this page addresses it. The same holds if there is fear in the house. Those dynamics need a licensed professional and, where relevant, people who can help somebody get somewhere safe.

The same applies from your own side. If you are carrying a hopelessness that has not lifted for months, any thought of harming yourself however briefly it passes, an addiction, or a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, bring in a clinician regardless of what happens in the relationship. And if the two of you have arrived at contempt, the habit of speaking to each other with visible disgust, a couples therapist in the room is worth far more than any exercise you run at home. Everything below is built for the ordinary version: two people who still want this to work and cannot understand why effort keeps evaporating on contact.

## Why Doesn't Effort Transfer Between Accounts?

Because a relationship runs several separate accounts at once, and a surplus in one credits nothing to another.

Think of six of them running continuously for both of you: certainty, variety, significance, love and connection, growth, and contribution. Everybody runs all six, whatever words they use. [The full framework has its own page](/patterns/six-human-needs/), and the only part that matters here is the accounting: each account is supplied by different behavior, and a deposit into one does nothing whatsoever for another. Hours worked deposit into certainty. Keeping an agreed rule deposits into certainty. Solving the problem your partner described deposits into your own sense of contribution, and often into your own significance, which is worth noticing.

| The account | What it is actually asking for | What a partner says when it is empty | Where the effort usually goes instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Certainty | Predictability, safety, a sense the ground will hold | "I never know where I stand with you" | Already the fullest account in most struggling couples |
| Variety | Something in the week that is not identical to last week | "We don't do anything anymore" | More of the same reliable routine |
| Significance | Mattering, being taken seriously, ranking first for somebody | "I was very low on the list of priority" | Providing more, which reads as competence |
| Love and connection | Being known rather than known about | "We have not had connection" | Time in the same room, phones out |
| Growth | Becoming something, moving somewhere, not standing still | "I have been in the same year three times" | Stability, which is the opposite deposit |
| Contribution | Giving something that goes beyond the household | "Nothing I do goes anywhere" | Doing more inside the household |

Look at the right-hand column. Almost every giver in this situation is depositing into certainty at enormous personal cost, then finding the receipt refused. What makes it so disorienting is that the effort genuinely is generous. Somebody working long hours is not lying about the sacrifice. The sacrifice simply lands in the account that was already comfortably in credit, and the two accounts that are empty go untouched for another year.

This is also why a negotiated compromise so often changes nothing. A rule settles the visible complaint about frequency or timing or a specific behavior, gets honored to the letter, and leaves the need underneath it exactly where it was. [Fights that keep coming back have their own page and their own reason for repeating](/patterns/why-do-we-keep-having-the-same-fight/), and the short version is that the rule and the need are different objects.

## What Does This Sound Like From the Inside?

Almost identical in both directions, which is the first strange thing about it. Here is what tends to be said by the person on the receiving end, the one whose partner is trying hard and getting nowhere:

- "I was very low on the list of priority, even though I would never admit it."
- "I also felt that I wasn't good enough and I wasn't very loved."
- "We have not had passion. We have not had connection. We have not had very much intimacy at all, and my heart has hurt, and I felt lonely."

Notice what is absent from those sentences. Nobody says the effort was insufficient. Nobody disputes that the other person was working. What they describe is a position in a ranking and a missing quality of contact, both of which are unaffected by how many hours anybody put in.

And here is roughly what it sounds like from inside the giving, in the moment it starts to come apart in a useful direction:

- "All that time I thought it was him. He has the problem, all he cares about is work."
- "I've noticed something very important. All the actions that I've taken is in fact fulfilling the need of significance."

That second sentence is the whole page in one line, delivered by somebody who had just worked out what their own giving had quietly been for. Sit with it before moving on. A great deal of effort that presents itself as devotion is also, underneath, doing something for the person making it, and that does not make it fake. It makes it aimed at two targets at once, only one of which is the person it is supposedly for.

## Why Does Giving More of the Same Make Things Worse?

Because it increases the intensity of a quality that is already being refused.

Picture the shape of it. Somebody reaches, gets a flat response, reads the flatness as evidence they need to try harder, and reaches further: more contact, more insistence, more affection offered at moments the other person has not asked for it, more asking whether things are all right between you. Each increment raises the pressure the other person is already backing away from. They withdraw a little more, which the reacher reads as the gap widening, which produces another increase. Neither person chose that loop and both are now running it at full speed.

Consider a composite of a couple several years into a rocky stretch, sleeping in separate rooms by mutual agreement. One of them says they need space to feel like a person again. The other has been working hard, reaching out constantly, and cannot understand why not one bit of it registers. Every additional approach makes the request for space more urgent, and every request for space makes the approaching more frantic. What eventually moved it was not a bigger gesture, and not a smaller one. It was one ordinary gesture delivered from settled attention instead of from a script, after a specific request for what, when and how connection actually needed to arrive.

Clinical work on couples names this cycle directly. The pursue and withdraw pattern is the central object in Emotionally Focused Therapy, and its founder's reframe of it is the useful part: coldness, criticism and silence frequently function as a protest against feared disconnection rather than as a verdict on the relationship. Read that way, a partner going flat is often reaching, badly. [The distancing side of that has its own page](/patterns/emotionally-unavailable/), and it is worth reading if the person going quiet in your house is the other one.

## Is Presence or Volume the Thing Being Measured?

Presence, almost every time, and it is measured with uncomfortable accuracy.

A gesture delivered with sustained attention on the other person lands as love. The identical gesture delivered while some part of your attention sits on your own performance, whether this is working, whether it will finally be enough, arrives as something else, because the checking is detectable. It reads as a small absence in the middle of an act of care, which is exactly the thing people are describing when they say you were never really there.

| The gesture | Delivered from presence | Delivered while checking |
|---|---|---|
| Asking how their day was | You hear the answer and ask a second question about the answer | You wait through the answer for the pause, so you can say the thing you had planned |
| An evening set aside | Phone in another room, no agenda, no outcome required | Phone face down, one eye on whether the evening is going well |
| An apology | Names the specific thing, then stops | Names it, explains it, then waits for the verdict |
| A compliment | Specific, unhurried, and it ends | Slightly oversized, followed by a glance to see if it registered |
| Physical affection | Offered without a question attached | Offered with a question attached: are we all right now |
| Fixing their problem | Only after they said they wanted it fixed | Immediately, because a solved problem is a measurable result |

The right-hand column describes somebody who cares a great deal and is anxious about the outcome, which is an entirely reasonable state to be in after two years of nothing working. It is also the state that guarantees another evening of nothing working.

Don't judge yourself for recognizing your own week in that column. Checking is what conscientious people do under pressure, and the harder somebody is trying in the ordinary sense of trying, the more reliably it shows up.

## Why Can They Tell When You Are Checking Whether It Landed?

Because checking consumes exactly the attention that presence is made of, and there is only so much of it.

Attention pointed at your own performance is attention that has left the room. Your partner cannot see the internal monitoring and can absolutely feel the result: a small hesitation before you respond, an answer slightly too prepared, warmth that arrives with a request for confirmation stitched onto the end of it. None of that is nameable in the moment, which is why the complaint reaches you in its most infuriating form, as something vague about you not being present, rather than as a specific thing you could go and fix.

This is also the reason the most common advice available on this subject, communicate more, fails so often. More communication delivered by the same anxious monitoring produces more of the same signal. Vague communication produces vague reassurance: you ask if things are okay, they say things are fine, and nothing has moved except that you have both been reminded that things are not fine. What actually closes the gap is oddly unromantic. It is specification. Precisely what, precisely when, precisely how.

If the complaint in your house runs the other direction, and it is you who keeps explaining and never quite lands, [that half of the loop is covered separately](/patterns/why-do-i-feel-unheard/).

## What Is the Resentment Doing Inside the Gesture?

Arriving first, before the content of the gesture does, and getting read accurately by the person it was aimed at pleasing.

Years of effort that never registered leave a residue. It does not need to be spoken to be present. It shows up as a fractional stiffness in the offer, a tone very slightly flatter than warmth would be, a gift handed over with something behind it that neither of you would be able to put into words. A partner scanning for whether they are actually wanted picks that up with high accuracy, closes a little further, and the giver reads the closing as fresh proof that nothing works. Which it did not. The reason is simply different from the one being assumed.

The awkward part is that the resentment is usually justified. Somebody who has given generously for a decade and received little back has a real case, and being told to simply let it go is neither possible nor fair. What works better is naming it, out loud, without an accusation attached: that you have been carrying something about how long this has gone one way, and that you would rather set it down than deliver it inside another gift. Said cleanly, that is one of the more disarming sentences available in a long relationship. [Anger that had nowhere to go has its own mechanism and its own way out](/patterns/resentment/).

## Is Your Giving Quietly Organized Around Getting Something Back?

Frequently, and the ledger is invisible to the person keeping it and completely legible to the person on the other end of it.

Somebody described their own version of this plainly: "From time to time I'll go through a spurt of trying to give more so that I'll get back." That is honest and it is also ordinary. Almost all giving in a long relationship carries some expectation, and the trouble starts when the expectation is unstated, because an unstated expectation still gets communicated. It comes through in the pause after the gesture, in the small deflation when nothing is said, in the way the next week is slightly cooler without anybody deciding it should be.

There is a harder version of the same question. Some of the effort may be feeding your own account rather than theirs. Being the reliable one, the provider, the person who solves it, all deposit heavily into your own significance, and it is possible to be genuinely exhausted by giving that was partly for you the whole time. That realization tends to land badly at first and then be a relief, because it hands you something you can actually change.

Nothing in this makes you manipulative. It makes you a person who learned somewhere that love is something you earn by producing, and who has been paying the bill on that lesson ever since, usually without ever having chosen it.

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because at least five different situations produce the identical sentence about effort not counting, and they need different responses.

| What you are seeing | What it might actually be | The thing that tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing you do ever registers | Effort aimed at an account that is already full | Score the six accounts separately; two usually come back much lower than the rest |
| Nothing you do ever registers | A partner who currently cannot take love in from anybody | Whether the same flatness shows up with their friends, their family, and at work |
| "You are never really here" | A presence problem rather than a time problem | Whether they can name a specific past evening that did feel right |
| Every compliment gets waved away | A request for a fuller response, not an accurate self-assessment | What happens if you stay with it instead of arguing the point |
| Coldness after you reach out | A protest against distance rather than a verdict on you | Whether it softens when you stay warm and ask for nothing back |
| You genuinely give far more than you receive, and you count it | The one-way arrangement, which is a different pattern | Whether your partner initiates anything at all in an ordinary month |
| Contempt, and no wish to close the gap | Something this page cannot help with | Whether either of you still wants to be understood by the other |

Most people find themselves in two rows rather than one, which is normal and does not complicate the work much. The rows point at different first moves, and the first move is all you need.

## What Actually Moves It?

A specific target, delivered with real attention, repeated for longer than feels reasonable. In that order, because aiming badly with tremendous presence is still aiming badly.

1. **Score the six accounts from inside their chair.** This is the single most useful exercise available for this problem and it takes twenty minutes. Write the six down: certainty, variety, significance, love and connection, growth, contribution. Now answer as your partner, from their position, out of ten, for how well the relationship currently supplies each one. Not how well you think you supply it. How it feels sitting where they sit. Then ask them to do the same, separately, and compare the two sheets. Two things usually happen. The account you have been pouring into comes back high from both of you, and one or two others come back near empty in their column and comfortable in yours. That gap works as a target rather than as an indictment, and until it exists the whole problem has been unaimed.
2. **Ask for specification instead of intention.** What would you want more of, when in the day does it actually work for you, and how would you want it delivered. Three questions, asked plainly, written down. Most couples have never had this conversation because asking it feels unromantic, close to admitting the relationship needs an instruction manual. It does need one. Everybody's is different and nobody is issued a copy.
3. **Mine your own history for templates.** If they cannot answer in the abstract, and many people genuinely cannot, ask for one specific past moment that felt like being loved. Any size. Then take it apart together: what was actually happening in that scene. Your attention was undivided, nothing was being solved, they were being asked about rather than told about. You now hold a template that is already proven on this particular person.
4. **Change your own state before you attempt anything.** One minute, before you walk in the door or start the conversation. Settle your attention, and deliberately recall one specific memory of genuinely appreciating this person, an ordinary one rather than a wedding photograph. Presence is a state you arrive in, and it cannot be performed from a standing start. This step looks the most skippable and is the one that decides whether anything else works.
5. **Answer the bid rather than the content.** Small approaches happen a dozen times a day: a comment about something on the news, a mention of somebody at work, a self-deprecating line about something they made. Each one is a request for attention wearing an ordinary sentence. Turning toward it takes about four seconds and costs nothing. Turning away is what accumulates.
6. **Respond to their good news like it is good news.** When they tell you something went well, the response that builds something is enthusiastic and curious: ask for the details, ask what the best part was. Muted approval, immediate problem-spotting, or waiting for your turn all register as flat, however supportive they feel from inside.
7. **Name the resentment and set it down.** Out loud, once, without a bill attached. It will otherwise ride inside every attempt that follows.
8. **Stop increasing the volume, and keep the effort.** Withdrawal reads as confirmation to a partner who already suspects they are not wanted. Redirect it. Same energy, better aim, less broadcasting.
9. **Expect a stretch where the new effort still gets nothing back.** A partner who has spent years watching effort miss will not update on the first correctly aimed week. They will assume it is a phase, because it has been a phase before. Tolerating that stretch without demanding credit is most of the work.

Is any of that romantic? No. It is specific, slightly awkward, and freaking unglamorous, and it is what changes the situation while the grand gesture keeps not changing it.

## What If They Cannot Tell You What They Need?

Assume they cannot, at least at first, and stop treating that as obstruction.

Very few adults have ever been asked to name an emotional need directly, so the honest answer to what do you need from me is usually a blank. What comes out instead is a complaint about a behavior, because complaints are easier to locate than needs. Take the complaint as a pointer rather than as the request itself, and go looking for what sits under it: somebody who wants the phone put away is rarely campaigning against phones.

Three questions get further than asking directly. When did you last feel really wanted here. What did I do that week. What has been missing that you have not bothered mentioning because it seemed small. That last one produces more useful material than the other two combined, because the small unmentioned things are exactly where a need has been quietly going unmet for years.

If they will not engage with any of it, and the refusal is consistent rather than a bad evening, [that situation has its own page](/patterns/my-partner-wont-work-on-themselves/) and a different set of options, including some honest ones about what you owe yourself.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

The diagnosis takes one conversation. The repair takes months, and the resentment takes longer than the repair.

Those three speeds are worth separating, because collapsing them is how people give up in week three. Locating the shortfall genuinely is fast: an honest scoring conversation can reorganize the whole problem from we are not compatible into two named categories in a single evening, and that reorganization alone tends to change the temperature in a house.

Changing where your effort actually goes is slower, mostly because the old aim is a habit with decades behind it and reasserts itself the moment you are tired. Expect to catch yourself mid-gesture, delivering into the full account again, for a long while. Catching it counts even on the evenings you go ahead anyway.

The residue takes longest on both sides. Your partner has years of evidence that effort from you lands badly, and they will keep testing the new pattern against that record without meaning to. Their skepticism is arithmetic rather than obstruction. Somewhere in the second or third month, if the aim has genuinely changed, the arithmetic starts coming out differently, and it usually shows up as something small and undramatic rather than as a breakthrough conversation. Somebody describing the far side of exactly this shift put it like this: "But now it's completely switched around. So I just think it's amazing. We have a winner."

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From publicly available relationship research and one widely known popular framework, and all of it can be checked without going through me.

Gary Chapman, a marriage counselor, published *The Five Love Languages* in 1992, proposing that people give and receive care mainly through one of five channels: words of affirmation, acts of service, gifts, quality time, and physical touch. That book is the most recognizable public name for the translation problem this page describes, and it deserves an honest caveat rather than an endorsement. A 2024 review article in *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, led by Emily Impett at the University of Toronto, examined the evidence and found the central claims poorly supported: people are generally not well described by a single dominant language, and matching a partner's stated language has not reliably predicted relationship satisfaction. The authors proposed a balanced diet as a better metaphor. The underlying observation, that care delivered in your own preferred currency often misses, holds up well. The tidy five-category version of it does not, and it is worth knowing which part you are relying on.

John Gottman and Nan Silver set out the idea of bids for connection in *The Relationship Cure* in 2001, drawn from years of observational work with couples in a laboratory apartment at the University of Washington. A bid is any small request for attention or affection, and a partner responds by turning toward it, away from it, or against it. The figure most often quoted from that program comes from a follow-up of newlyweds: couples still together six years later had turned toward each other's bids around eighty-six percent of the time in the lab, against roughly a third of the time for those who divorced. Take the exact number with appropriate caution, since it comes from one sample in one setting, and take the shape of the finding seriously.

Shelly Gable, a psychologist in the University of California system, ran a research program on what happens when somebody shares good news, published with Harry Reis, Emily Impett and Evan Asher in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* in 2004. It distinguishes four response styles and finds that only the enthusiastic, engaged one reliably builds relationship quality over time, regardless of how supportive the responder believes they are being. That is the citable basis for why a well-meant, mild response to your partner's news can land as indifference.

Sue Johnson developed Emotionally Focused Therapy with Les Greenberg in the 1980s and described the pursue and withdraw cycle for general readers in *Hold Me Tight* in 2008. Her reframe, that a partner's coldness or criticism often functions as a protest against feared disconnection rather than as a verdict, is doing real work in the middle of this page.

The rest is mine and carries no research weight whatsoever. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind and I am not a couples therapist. None of the researchers named here has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my experience disagree, go and check theirs.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Try Anything Bigger?

Take a piece of paper, turn it sideways, and make three columns. In the first, write the six accounts: certainty, variety, significance, love and connection, growth, contribution. In the second, score each one out of ten as you believe your partner would score it, answering from their chair rather than from yours. In the third, and this is the column that does the work, write the specific evidence you would point to if they challenged your number. An actual scene, from the last month, with a day attached to it.

Then read the third column on its own. Most people find two or three rows where the evidence is vivid and detailed, and two or three where there is nothing there at all, or where the only thing they can produce is something they did rather than something the other person visibly received. Those empty rows are the answer you have been trying to get out of arguments for a year. That costs you fifteen minutes and a piece of paper, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever show it to anybody, and whether or not you ever go anywhere.
