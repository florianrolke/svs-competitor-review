# Why does a changed plan hit me so much harder than it should?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because the plan was carrying more than logistics. For most people who react this way, one plan is the only route currently supplying a felt sense of certainty, so a change to it registers as a threat to the whole supply rather than as a rearranged evening. The size of the reaction reports how much load that plan was under, and load can be redistributed.

- "I get angry when someone is late, and I try to lock everything into place so it cannot happen again."
- "I'm always preparing for the moment when things fall apart."

The cost of that arrangement is that every ordinary change to a schedule gets charged at the price of your safety.

The message arrives at ten past six. Something has come up, they are so sorry, could you do next week instead. You type back that it is completely fine, no problem at all, and then you stand in a kitchen that has gone strange, holding three free hours you cannot make any use of.

By seven you are short with whoever happens to be nearest, over something that has nothing to do with dinner. You have reread the message twice looking for a tone in it. Some part of you knows, clearly and the entire time, that this reaction does not fit the event, and knowing that has changed exactly nothing about the evening.

Most people land on a page like this having already tried the two explanations available to them. The first is that they are controlling, which does not survive contact with how little they actually want to run anybody else's life. The second is that they are simply bad at change and ought to relax more, which sounds true, prescribes nothing you have not already attempted, and quietly implies the problem is a character flaw you have failed to correct. Both have been run. Neither survived. Something more specific is going on underneath, and it has a shape you can check against your own last three deviations.

One plain line before any of it. If a difficulty of this kind has been with you for as long as you can remember, or it is severe enough to be costing you work, sleep or relationships, it is worth raising with a doctor or another qualified professional, since this page is about the everyday pattern rather than about any medical question.

## What Does This Actually Look Like on an Ordinary Week?

Small, undramatic, and almost impossible to describe afterward to anybody who was not in the room.

- A friend cancels dinner two hours before you were supposed to meet, and the whole evening feels ruined rather than simply rearranged.
- A meeting gets pushed back fifteen minutes and you feel a spike of irritation completely out of proportion to fifteen minutes.
- Someone in your family arrives outside the window you had agreed on, and you feel a flash of anger before you have even said hello.
- You make a loose list for a Saturday, one item shifts, and the entire day feels off even though nothing else actually changed.
- Someone suggests winging it, or figuring it out when you get there, and what shows up in you is a real spike of anxiety rather than lightness.
- A trip you had planned for months gets rearranged by weather or an airline, and what you feel sits closer to dread than to ordinary disappointment.

Read that list looking for the disaster. There is no disaster anywhere in it, and that absence is the reason this goes unexamined for decades. A reaction with a real catastrophe behind it explains itself. A reaction this size with nothing behind it gets filed as evidence that you are difficult, and filed away again the next time, and the time after that.

Treat the repetition as information instead. You already know the reaction is disproportionate. You knew it last Thursday at seven o'clock, in real time, while it was happening. A conclusion you reach that reliably, that changes that little, is telling you something about the structure you are running rather than something about your character.

## Why Is the Reaction So Much Bigger Than the Change?

Because the size of the reaction reports how much the plan was carrying, and not how much the change actually cost.

Certainty is one of the ordinary things every person runs on, and nobody has ever perceived one of these needs directly. You do not feel a shortage of certainty. You feel that the plan changed. Somebody whose certainty runs through a business does not feel a shortage of certainty either; they feel that the numbers are down. The need is abstract and permanently invisible. What you get instead is the concrete stand-in currently delivering it, and that stand-in absorbs the entire emotional charge.

When one plan is carrying that need essentially alone, with no other route running in parallel, an ordinary fluctuation in it stops being an inconvenience. It gets read as the working supply line failing. That is the whole disproportion in a sentence, and it also explains the strangest feature of the pattern, which is how flat the reaction sizes are across wildly different events.

| The event | What it actually cost you | What the reaction was sized to |
|---|---|---|
| A dinner canceled two hours out | Three free hours and a small disappointment | The one thing making that evening feel settled |
| A meeting moved fifteen minutes | Fifteen minutes | The arrangement that made the whole morning readable |
| A weekend trip rearranged by an airline | Some money, some hassle, different dates | Months of predictability anchored to a single fixture |
| A Saturday list with one item shifted | Close to nothing | The frame that told you what the day was going to be |
| Someone arriving forty minutes late | Forty minutes | Evidence that arrangements hold when you rely on them |

Read the middle column on its own and the case for calling yourself unreasonable looks strong. Read the right-hand column on its own and the reaction turns out to have been perfectly proportionate the whole time, to a quantity nobody around you could see and you had never counted.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

In a narrow set of situations, and it is worth naming them before anything further down.

If a low mood has sat on you for months without lifting, if there is drinking or using in the picture that has started to worry you, or if any thought of harming yourself has crossed your mind however quickly it passed, that belongs with a licensed professional today, ahead of anything written here. The same holds if unpredictability is being used against you: someone controlling your money, your movements, or who you are allowed to see, or restoring and withdrawing warmth depending on whether you comply. None of that gets solved by anybody becoming better at handling a changed schedule, and it needs people whose job is helping you get somewhere safe.

Everything below is built for the ordinary version of this. The six o'clock text. The fifteen-minute delay. The Saturday that fell apart because one item on a list moved.

## What Is a Plan Actually Doing for You?

Supplying certainty, on a route you probably never chose deliberately and have never counted.

There is nothing suspect about wanting predictability. It is one of the ordinary drivers everybody runs on, and plenty of people want a great deal of it and feel entirely fine, because theirs arrives through four or five independent channels. A steady job, a body that behaves, a marriage that has been the same for eleven years, a Sunday routine, a bank balance with some slack in it. Any one of those can wobble and the others keep the floor where it was.

The trouble is concentration. Ask yourself, honestly, how many things in your life currently deliver a felt sense of predictability without depending on anyone else's cooperation. Most people who recognize themselves on this page can name one, and it is the calendar. Everything else has been outsourced to arrangements that other people can move.

There is public research language for exactly this experience, and it is worth having because it is checkable. Intolerance of uncertainty is a well-documented individual-difference construct describing a tendency to experience ambiguous or unpredictable situations as threatening, regardless of the objective odds of anything bad happening. It has published, peer-reviewed measurement instruments behind it. Separately, and from an entirely different literature, need for cognitive closure describes the pull toward a firm, definite answer, any answer, over an open-ended situation. That second one explains something the first does not: why an unresolved plan can be aversive on its own terms, before you know whether the eventual outcome is good or bad.

Here is the flat verdict on the part people most want answered. This moves. It measures as a trait-like tendency, and trait-like tendencies of this kind still shift with deliberate practice and changed circumstances. What does not move is the wanting itself, and no useful version of this work involves needing certainty less. [The underlying driver, and what it looks like across a whole life rather than in one evening, has its own page](/patterns/need-for-certainty/).

## Why Does a Good Outcome That Arrived Off-Schedule Still Bother You?

Because being on-plan and being okay have been fused into the same thing, and the schedule is what actually gets defended.

Stated plainly it sounds absurd. Does it matter what time good news arrives? The builder finishes two days early. The offer comes through on Tuesday instead of Friday. A friend turns up an hour before you expected them, delighted to see you. Every one of those is a better outcome than the one you planned for, and something in you still logs a violation and refuses to fully settle.

Two different tests are running, and they have been treated as one for so long that separating them takes deliberate effort. The first test asks whether this turned out well. The second asks whether this happened the way you expected. A plan-dependent system runs the second test and then reports the result as though it had answered the first, which is why you can spend an evening unable to enjoy something that went better than planned, and be unable to explain to anybody, including yourself, what your complaint even is.

The useful thing about this particular symptom is that it settles the diagnosis on its own. If your distress survived the outcome being good, the outcome was never the thing at stake. What was at stake was control over how and when it happened, and that is a certainty question rather than a preference about builders.

## Why Does the Reaction Arrive Before You Have Decided Anything?

Because meaning gets assigned by your state before conscious thought has run.

The sequence is faster than it feels. A sensation arrives, gets evaluated against a stored rule, and comes back as a meaning, and all of that completes before you have consciously registered that anything happened. Your state does the assigning, and state here means something quite physical: your posture, where your attention was pointed, and the internal language already running in your head thirty seconds before the message landed. The same canceled dinner read against a state that was tired, braced and slightly behind produces a different meaning from the same dinner read against a state that was loose and unhurried.

This is why the reaction cannot be talked down in the moment. By the time a reasonable counter-argument shows up, the meaning has already been assigned and the body has already responded to it. You are arguing with a conclusion that was reached before the argument was available.

It also explains the more demoralizing version of the same thing. You sit down on Wednesday, genuinely see that Monday's reaction was excessive, and agree with yourself completely. By Thursday evening the next deviation produces the identical spike. Nothing failed there. An insight delivered into an unchanged state gets overwritten by that state within hours, because the sequence runs meaning-first and the meaning is generated somewhere the insight never reached. [Why understanding a pattern so rarely changes it has its own page](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/), and it is the single most useful thing to accept early here.

## What Turns a Preference Into a Rule You Cannot Break?

Two words, usually, and they are "has to."

Underneath a disproportionate reaction there is almost always an absolute sitting where a preference could be. Not "this would work best if it goes as scheduled" but "this has to go as scheduled." Absolutes get evaluated as pass or fail conditions, automatically, beneath conscious thought, which is why you never catch the rule being applied. You only ever catch the verdict arriving.

The distinction matters because the rule is the editable layer and the need is not. Nothing useful comes from wanting certainty less. A great deal comes from noticing that you have been running a pass or fail condition on something that could have been a preference, and that the condition was written at some point by you, probably years ago, probably after something that genuinely did fall apart.

The honest caveat belongs right here, because getting the order wrong is the most common way this work fails. Loosening the rule directly, before a second source of certainty exists, tends to backfire. You end up demanding flexibility from a system that currently has one supply line and no spare, and what that produces is a person who feels less safe rather than a person who feels more relaxed.

## Why Does an Over-Scheduled Life Feel Like Discipline From the Inside?

Because it produces real, visible results, and a function that is working stays invisible while it works.

This is where two completely different readers show the identical behavior for opposite reasons, and where getting the cause wrong sends someone into years of the wrong repair. Both people run a full calendar. Both get restless in open time. Both describe themselves as organized, reliable, a bit intense about scheduling. From outside there is nothing to tell them apart, and quite often there is nothing to tell them apart from inside either.

For the first reader, the calendar is a certainty strategy. It manufactures predictability, deliberately, in a life where predictability arrives from nowhere else. For the second, the calendar is doing that too, and it is also holding a door shut. A tightly scheduled life can be a protective structure formed early to keep a specific, harder feeling from ever getting room to surface, by making sure no hour is ever unaccounted for. Unplanned time carries a charge for that reader, because it is the exact condition under which the guarded thing gets its opening.

| What anyone would see | The certainty version | The protective version |
|---|---|---|
| A full calendar, weeks out | Predictability, manufactured on purpose | Every hour occupied so nothing has room to arrive |
| Restlessness in open time | Mild, and it eases once something gets scheduled | Sharp and specific, and it worsens the longer the gap runs |
| What a canceled plan removes | The route to feeling settled | The route, plus the cover over a particular feeling |
| Clearing the diary deliberately | Discomfort, then a new structure appears elsewhere | The guarded feeling starts surfacing within a day or two |
| How the person describes it | I like knowing what is happening | I am just busy, I have always been like this |
| What actually helps | A second, independent source of predictability | Naming what the busyness has kept out, in small doses |

Two public frameworks are worth knowing here, both attributed honestly. Experiential avoidance is a construct from the acceptance and commitment therapy literature describing the effort to avoid contact with unwanted internal experience, and the observation that the avoidance itself often costs more than the experience would have. Separately, internal family systems, a therapy model built around the idea that protective parts form early and keep doing their job long after the conditions that produced them have gone, gives the clearest popular account of why a structure like this defends itself when you try to dismantle it. That second one is a clinical model, delivered by trained practitioners, and nothing on this page is therapy or a substitute for it. What both are useful for here is naming the thing you may be looking at.

Consider a composite. Somebody whose weekends have been fully scheduled since the children were small, still fully scheduled now that the children are mostly grown and gone. An unplanned free afternoon makes him restless in a way he cannot explain, and he is always first to suggest a project rather than sit in it. Loosening the schedule made that worse rather than better, every time he tried. What moved it was naming what the busyness had been keeping out, without needing to trace where it came from, and then letting exactly one hour a week stay genuinely unplanned on purpose, treated as rehearsal rather than as relaxation.

## Why Does Being Told to Be More Spontaneous Change Nothing?

Because that advice aims at the vehicle and says nothing at all about the load.

The standard suggestions cluster tightly: relax, be more flexible, practice spontaneity, lower your expectations, communicate calmly when things shift. Every one of them is reasonable, and they fail for two separate reasons that stack.

The first failure is timing, described above. Meaning gets assigned before the reframe arrives, so a good suggestion delivered mid-reaction lands on a conclusion that has already been reached. The second failure is arithmetic. Telling somebody to want the plan less attacks the vehicle without supplying a replacement for whatever the vehicle was carrying. For the certainty version, the need does not shrink because the calendar loosened. It reroutes, usually somewhere worse: tighter locking-in on a different front, a low background irritability, more elaborate advance planning to compensate for the flexibility you just performed. For the protective version, removing the structure while leaving the guarded feeling untouched reads to the protective part as a door being opened with nothing arranged on the other side, and the part responds by tightening.

Take a third composite. Somebody agrees enthusiastically to see how the day goes with friends, then spends the whole day anxious and short-tempered once the plans stay genuinely open, and eventually engineers a reason the group has to commit to something concrete. She wants to be the spontaneous person she describes herself as. Her system has no second route to feeling safe once the explicit plan disappears, so it manufactures one by pulling the group back toward structure. From outside it looks like controlling other people. What is actually happening is an attempt to restore her own footing using the only mechanism she has.

"Be more spontaneous" treats this as a taste or a skill you have failed to acquire. It sits much closer to an arithmetic problem: one need, one vehicle, no backup. No amount of wanting to be different changes that arithmetic until a second vehicle actually exists.

## Why Does Notice Change How Big the Same Change Feels?

Because notice is rerouting time, and rerouting is the thing that has to happen.

A trip moved six weeks out barely registers. The same trip moved the night before produces something closer to dread. The practical difference between those two is usually modest and sometimes zero, which makes the gap in reaction look irrational until you notice what the extra weeks were doing. With time, the load quietly redistributes. You make other arrangements, you get used to the new shape, you re-anchor the month somewhere else, and none of that requires a decision. With ninety minutes, there is nowhere for the load to go, so the whole amount registers at once.

This has one immediately practical implication. If you decide to work on this, do not start with the changes that arrive at ninety minutes' notice. Start with the ones that come with a week, where there is enough room to watch the rerouting happen and enough slack to notice what you do with it. Trying to build tolerance during the hardest available version of the event is how most people conclude, wrongly, that none of this works on them.

## Why Does Lateness Produce Anger Rather Than Ordinary Irritation?

Because somebody else's reliability had quietly become part of the supply, and their being late reads as the supply failing.

"I get angry when someone is late, and I try to lock everything into place so it cannot happen again." That sentence contains the whole cycle, including the part that makes it worse. The anger arrives, sized to a threat rather than to forty minutes. Then, afterward, comes the correction: more advance confirmation, tighter arrangements, an explicit agreement about timing, a reminder the day before. Each of those feels like prudence, and each raises the amount you have riding on other people behaving predictably. The next deviation therefore costs more than the last one did, and the system tightens again in response.

One thing needs saying plainly, because it decides what this page can and cannot do for you. Nothing here is about what to say to the person who was late, how to get them to be more reliable, or what their behavior means about them. They may be thoughtless. They may have had a genuinely good reason. Either way, the reaction being decoded here is yours, it was generated on your side of the exchange, and it is the only half of this you can do anything about. [The version of this where the need has generalized into managing other people has its own page](/patterns/why-do-i-need-to-control-everything/), and it is worth reading if you recognize more of yourself there than here.

## Where Does This Sit Next to Needing Control, Overreacting, and Scanning for Danger?

Next to all three, and it separates from each of them at a specific, checkable point.

| What you might be looking at | The driver underneath it | The thing that tells it apart |
|---|---|---|
| A plan carrying your certainty alone | Safety through predictability | The reaction eases once the day gets re-anchored to something else |
| A generalized need for control | The same need, now requiring other people to comply | You manage other people's arrangements in advance, not only your own |
| Scanning for what could go wrong | Danger detection rather than predictability | The alert runs even when nothing has changed at all |
| Reacting big to most things | A response pattern that fires across many triggers | Plans are only one of several places it shows up |
| A change that genuinely cost something | Ordinary proportion | Written down in plain numbers, the cost holds up |

That last row deserves more respect than it usually gets. Sometimes a canceled plan really did cost you something substantial: childcare that cannot be replaced, a flight that cannot be rebooked, an evening you had arranged your whole week around for reasons that hold up on paper. Proportion is a real category, and deciding in advance that every strong reaction of yours is excessive is its own kind of distortion.

For the neighbors, the distinctions are worth making explicitly. [Scanning constantly for what might go wrong is a different mechanism](/patterns/hypervigilance/), because it runs on threat detection rather than on predictability, and it fires during calm stretches when no plan is even in play. [A reaction size that shows up across many unrelated triggers points somewhere broader](/patterns/why-do-i-overreact/). And [a life filled to the edges by default may be worth reading about from the busyness side](/patterns/why-am-i-always-busy/) rather than from the schedule side.

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because six different situations produce the same ruined Thursday evening and only one of them is the thing this page decodes.

| What it feels like | What it might actually be | The thing that tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| That plan mattered enormously to me | One vehicle carrying the whole load | Changes of very different sizes produce roughly the same reaction |
| I am just an organized person | Certainty routed entirely through a schedule | Open time produces restlessness rather than relief |
| I need my routine | A structure doing protective work | Loosening it makes things sharply worse rather than gradually easier |
| I overreacted to nothing | A change that genuinely did cost something | Written out in plain numbers, the cost still holds up |
| I have always been like this | A period of real instability that concentrated the supply | It got noticeably worse after a stretch you can name |
| I am fine with change at work and hopeless at home | The load is unevenly distributed | Work has several independent sources of predictability; home has one |

None of these gets settled by how the reaction looked from outside, and none of them gets settled by asking somebody who was in the room. The information lives in what the plan was carrying, which nobody else can see and which you have probably never counted. That count is the actual diagnostic, and it takes about twenty minutes with a piece of paper.

## What Actually Moves It?

Two pieces of work, run in a specific order, and the order matters considerably more than the effort does.

Nothing below is a validated protocol, and anybody offering you one for this is overselling something. What follows is worked out from how the pattern holds, and it is built to be done by one person without anybody else agreeing to anything.

1. **Find out which version you are, in writing.** Not noted abstractly, written down. One page: what the calendar has actually been doing for you. Certainty on its own, or certainty plus keeping something at bay. The test is what shows up in genuinely open time. Mild restlessness that eases once something gets scheduled points one way. Something sharper and more specific, that gets worse the longer the gap runs, points the other. That answer decides everything below it.
2. **Build one fixed source of predictability nobody else can cancel.** A walk at the same time each morning. A Sunday hour that belongs to you. A standing commitment kept only to yourself. The requirement is not that it be impressive; the requirement is that no other person's cooperation is involved anywhere in it.
3. **Give it weeks before you judge it.** What makes a second source work is accumulated evidence that it holds, and evidence takes repetitions. Three days of a morning walk supplies nothing. Six weeks of one supplies a great deal, and you will usually notice it first as something missing rather than as something gained.
4. **Only then, edit one rule.** Take the single most absolute one you can find, the one that says this has to happen exactly as arranged, and rewrite it as a preference in plain words. Do this after step two has been running, not before, for the reason given further up.
5. **If it is the protective version, rehearse open time in small doses, alone.** One hour, deliberately unplanned, treated as practice rather than as rest. Notice what arrives in the first twenty minutes. That is the thing the structure has been holding off, and it usually turns out to be nameable and considerably smaller than the effort spent avoiding it.
6. **Stop adding structure after a deviation.** The reflex to lock everything down after something goes wrong once is the single most reliable way to make the next deviation cost more. Notice the reflex, and put it down.
7. **Separate the two tests out loud.** When something lands off-schedule, say both parts: did this turn out well, and did this happen the way I expected. Two answers, said separately. This sounds trivially small and it interrupts the fusion better than anything else on the list.
8. **Practice on low-load plans first.** The Tuesday coffee, not the trip you booked in March. Capacity built on small deviations transfers upward. Capacity attempted on the biggest available deviation mostly produces evidence that you cannot do this.

Is any of that comfortable? No. Sitting in an unplanned Sunday hour, on purpose, while everything in you wants to go and be useful, takes real, freaking discipline, and it is a specific discipline rather than a general instruction to be a calmer person.

One more honest note. None of this changes how big the very next canceled plan feels by very much. What it changes, across a season rather than a week, is whether the reaction stays sized to that one plan's actual load or stays sized to your entire sense of safety.

## What Does the Other End of This Look Like?

Considerably less dramatic than the word flexible suggests, and made mostly of things that stop happening.

| Inside the pattern | The other end of it | What it looks like on an ordinary Tuesday |
|---|---|---|
| One plan carrying everything | Several independent routes to predictability | A canceled dinner costs you a dinner |
| Deviation read as a threat | Deviation read as a change of arrangements | You feel the drop, and the evening still happens |
| More structure added after every wobble | The wobble absorbed and nothing added | No new rule gets written on Thursday night |
| Rules held as absolutes | Preferences held as preferences | You would rather it went as planned, and it did not |
| Other people's reliability load-bearing | Your own arrangements load-bearing | Someone runs late and you read a chapter |
| Open time filled by default | Open time occasionally left open | A Sunday hour goes unscheduled and nothing bad arrives |
| Good news off-schedule logged as a violation | Good news counted as good news | The builder finishes early and you are simply pleased |
| Recovery measured in evenings | Recovery measured in minutes | The spike arrives at six and is gone by half past |

Nothing in the middle column is impressive, and none of it would be worth mentioning to anybody. That is the shape of the destination here, and it is worth knowing in advance, because people looking for a transformation walk straight past it.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Weeks to notice something, closer to a season for it to hold, and it moves unevenly.

Two speeds are worth separating, because confusing them is what makes people quit in week three. Recovery time changes first. The spike still arrives at full size, and then the evening comes back at half past seven instead of being written off entirely. That is the earliest real progress, and almost nobody counts it, because everybody is watching for the spike to shrink and the spike is the last thing to move.

Intensity changes later, and only once the second source has enough history behind it to be trusted at the level where trust actually operates. That is months rather than weeks. It also regresses. A stretch of genuine load, an illness, a job ending, a move, a period where several things went unpredictable at once, puts the newer arrangement under stress it has not been tested against, and the old response runs again at full speed. Expect that rather than reading it as evidence the work failed.

Where this sits for me personally is worth saying plainly. I still feel the drop when something moves at short notice. It arrives less often than it used to, it takes minutes rather than an evening to pass, and the reflex to immediately lock the next thing down is the part that took longest to put down. I would rather tell you that than describe a finished version of something nobody finishes.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From public research on uncertainty and appraisal, and from widely read practitioner work, all of it checkable without going through me.

The closest research language for the central phenomenon here is intolerance of uncertainty. Mark Freeston and colleagues introduced the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale in a 1994 paper, and Michel Dugas and Robert Ladouceur at Université Laval built a model of chronic worry around the construct through the 1990s, arguing that people high in it experience ambiguous situations as threatening regardless of the objective likelihood of a bad outcome. Nicholas Carleton and colleagues published a twelve-item short form in 2007, and the construct remains an active area of published study. From a completely separate literature, Donna Webster and Arie Kruglanski introduced the Need for Closure Scale in a 1994 paper in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, describing the pull toward a firm, definite answer over an open-ended one, which is why an unresolved plan can be aversive before anybody knows how it turns out.

For the timing problem, two strands carry the weight. Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman set out the appraisal account of stress in *Stress, Appraisal, and Coping* in 1984, arguing that an event produces its emotional consequence through how it gets evaluated rather than through the event itself. Robert Zajonc argued in a 1980 paper in *American Psychologist* that affective reactions can arise before and independently of the sort of thinking we would recognize as reasoning, which is the clearest public statement of why a flash of anger can genuinely precede your own understanding of what set it off. Albert Ellis, who developed rational emotive behavior therapy from the mid-1950s and wrote about it for general readers in *A New Guide to Rational Living*, is the standard reference for the difference between an absolute demand and a preference, and for the claim that the demand is the editable part.

For the protective reading of a rigid routine, two more. Steven Hayes and colleagues set out experiential avoidance as a functional construct in a 1996 paper, describing the effort to avoid contact with unwanted internal experience and the costs that effort accumulates. Richard Schwartz developed internal family systems from the 1980s onward, and it is the most widely read popular account of protective parts that formed early and keep working long after the conditions are gone. That one is a clinical therapy model rather than a self-help framework, and nothing here is therapy or a substitute for it.

The rest of it is mine and carries no research weight at all. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, I am not a doctor or a therapist, and none of the researchers or authors named above has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, theirs is the one worth checking.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Change Anything About Your Calendar?

Run a deviation log for two weeks. Open a note on your phone, and every time something moves, gets canceled, or runs late, write three lines and nothing else. First, what changed, and what it cost in plain units: minutes, money, one evening, nothing. Second, the size of your reaction out of ten, scored within the hour rather than the next morning. Third, the exact sentence that arrived in your head in the first few seconds, in its real wording rather than a tidied one.

At the end of two weeks, read the first two columns side by side and ignore the third for a moment. Two things usually land. The first is how small the plain-unit costs are once they are written in plain units, which is uncomfortable and useful. The second is how flat the scores are. A fifteen-minute delay and a canceled weekend come out within a point or two of each other, over and over, and that flatness is the finding. The number was measuring the load all along, rather than the change. Then read the third column on its own, and count how many distinct sentences you are actually running. Most people find three. That costs you two weeks of noticing and nothing else, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever go anywhere.
