# Why can't I stop going over the details of the affair?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because going over it is a checking behavior, and checking has no finish line. Each detail you gather becomes another scene available to intrude at one in the morning, so the file grows instead of closing. The question underneath, will this happen again and do I actually know everything, has no single fact that answers it. That is why an honest answer buys hours.

- "I tried to confirm it."
- "I found out certain things weren't true, things he told me."
- "I don't trust him anymore."
- "I don't think I can have a relationship with someone I don't trust."
- "I feel a lot of positive things came up, but negative things came up too, and it's crazy that I'm still thinking that."

The last one is the sentence that brings most people here, and the cost of this pattern gets paid in hours taken back out of every week.

It is ten past one in the morning and you are running the same three sentences of a text exchange through your head for the tenth time tonight. You know every word of it already. You go through it again anyway, and somewhere around the fourth pass you notice that nothing new is arriving, and the noticing does not stop the fifth.

It happens in daylight too, which is the part nobody warns you about. You are folding laundry or driving to pick up groceries and you catch yourself several minutes into a scene you built yourself, of the two of them, in a room you have never been in, and you have no idea how long you have been in there. Later you start telling a friend a completely different story and hear the affair come up anyway, without having meant to bring it there.

One thing before anything else. If this has moved past thinking and into not eating, not sleeping for several days, drinking to get through the evenings, or any thought of harming yourself, however briefly it passes, that belongs today with a licensed professional or a crisis line rather than with a page. The same goes if there has been violence or threat in the relationship, or if reading detail about what happened is producing panic you cannot bring down. There is a fuller version of that boundary two sections below, and it is the most important section here.

## What Does This Sound Like From the Inside?

Flat, factual, and far less dramatic than the state underneath it.

These are the sentences people actually use, at the level they run at rather than smoothed into a description of a mood:

- "I feel my husband doesn't really love me."
- "And he betrayed me."
- "All that time I thought it was him."
- "He doesn't trust me with money, but I think it's ill-founded."
- "I'm just going through transition. I don't know if maybe I'm not using the word depressed, right? I think I'm just going through a difficult time."

Read them slowly. What matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours with no translation required.

Three things in that set are worth flagging before going further. The first is how ordinary the language is. Nobody says intrusive thoughts or hypervigilance at one in the morning. They say I keep thinking about it, and they say it apologetically.

The second is the sentence sitting at the top of the recognition list above, the shortest one: *I tried to confirm it.* Trying to confirm something and having confirmed it are two different states, and the entire mechanism of this page lives in the gap between them. Somebody can spend eight months trying to confirm whether their partner ever loved them and treat the attempt itself as the finding.

The third is the last sentence in this list, the euphemism. Going through a difficult time is doing a specific job: it keeps you functioning, keeps the conversation short when somebody at work asks, and keeps the actual state unnamed and therefore unaddressed. Reaching for the softer word buys you the ability to keep functioning, and it holds up for roughly the first month.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, near the top, because this boundary matters more than everything below it put together.

Go to a licensed professional, a doctor, or a crisis line rather than to any page, including this one, if any of the following is true. You have had any thought of harming yourself, however fast it passed. You have not slept properly for several consecutive days. You are drinking or using something to get through the evenings. You cannot eat, or you cannot stop. The checking is consuming hours of your day and you cannot function around it. Detail about what happened produces panic or physical symptoms you cannot bring down. There has been violence, coercion, or threat in this relationship, in which case the priority is your physical safety and people who can help with that, and none of the material below applies in the way it is written.

Two practical additions, said plainly because they get skipped. If there is any question about sexual health after what happened, that is a medical appointment this week rather than a conversation. And if you are the one holding a household together while running on four hours of sleep, your own doctor is a reasonable first call, not an overreaction.

Nothing here is a diagnosis. This page describes a pattern, and patterns are not conditions. Nobody, including a page and including a partner in an argument, can give you a clinical label, and a label is worth far less to you right now than an accurate description of what is actually happening in your week. Asking for help in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve.

## Are You Confirming Something, or Checking It?

Confirming ends. Checking does not, and from the inside the two feel identical.

Confirming resolves a question with an answer that lands and then stays landed. You wanted to know when it started, you asked, you were told, and now you know. Checking is an action performed to produce relief from a fear, and it structurally cannot conclude, because the question underneath it has no piece of evidence that fully closes it. Will this happen again. Do I actually know everything. Can I trust what I do not see. No sentence your partner can say answers any of those, which is why the honest answer you got on Tuesday needed asking again by Thursday.

There is a test, and it takes ten seconds. Before you ask the question, predict the answer. If you can predict it accurately, and you know that hearing exactly that answer would still leave you unsettled, you are topping up a supply rather than gathering evidence.

The reason this is so hard to catch in yourself is that checking arrives dressed as diligence. It takes real nerve to ask your partner about the affair at eleven at night. It feels like facing something rather than avoiding it, and in a sense it is. So the behavior gets rewarded internally, every single time, whether or not it moved anything at all. That internal reward is what keeps it running, and it is why being told to stop dwelling on it never works on anybody. Nobody experiences a courageous act as a bad habit.

This exact structure has a name in the clinical literature, and it is worth knowing that it was studied long before it happened to you. Safety behaviors, in the cognitive model of obsessional problems, are the checking, the reassurance-seeking, and the mental reviewing that a person performs to keep a feared outcome from being true. The finding that has held up repeatedly is that safety behaviors block the disconfirmation that would settle the fear, because the person credits the behavior rather than the absence of the feared event. The belief underneath never gets put to a real test, so it never updates, so it keeps commissioning more checks.

| The same question, two operations | Genuine new information | A checking loop |
|---|---|---|
| What you can predict beforehand | Not much | The answer, almost word for word |
| What changes after you get it | Something you do next | Nothing you do next |
| How long the relief lasts | The question is closed, so the point is moot | Two hours, often less |
| What the question does afterward | Stays answered | Reopens, slightly rephrased |
| How it feels while you are doing it | Uncomfortable and specific | Necessary, careful, almost brave |
| What it costs you | One difficult conversation | The same conversation, indefinitely |
| What it costs your partner | An honest hour | The growing sense that nothing they say counts |

Don't judge yourself for the right-hand column. Every single question in it is individually reasonable. The pattern only becomes visible in aggregate, which is exactly why it can run for a year inside a person who thinks of themselves as being careful.

## Why Does Getting More Detail Make the Replaying Worse?

Because a detail you did not have before becomes an image you did not have before, and images are what intrude.

Getting the full picture promises closure and delivers inventory. Where were they. What did the room look like. What did he say to her afterward. What was he doing on the Saturday I was at my mother's. Every answer arrives as a scene, and scenes are the currency the replay runs on. So the search expands as you conduct it, and the thing you were trying to finish gets larger with each honest answer, which is a genuinely cruel piece of design.

Say the flat version of this plainly, because a great deal of advice says the opposite. Gathering the complete timeline does not reliably produce closure. The search rarely completes, because completion was never the mechanism holding the file open.

There is one real exception, and it deserves its own treatment rather than being swept up with the rest. Some information genuinely changes what you do next, and you are entitled to it:

1. **Health.** Whether there is anything you need to be tested for. This is a fact with an action attached, and it is not negotiable.
2. **Money.** What was spent, from where, and whether anything was borrowed against or hidden.
3. **Whether it is actually over.** Not whether he says so. Whether contact has ended, and what happens if the other person makes contact again.
4. **Who else knows.** Because walking into a family dinner as the only person at the table who did not know is its own separate injury.
5. **Anything that affects a decision you are about to make.** A house, a pregnancy, a job move, a lawyer.

Those are decision-relevant. Ask them once, in one sitting, and write the answers down so you are not holding them in your head at three in the morning. Everything else, the tone of a message, the exact wording, what she looked like, whether he laughed, is scene-building. It has no action attached to it. It only ever adds to the reel.

The distinction matters because it lets you stop arguing with yourself about whether you are allowed to ask things. You are. Sort them first.

## Why Does a Good Conversation Stop Working by the Next Afternoon?

Because a resolved feeling and a resolved belief run on separate machinery, and only one of them moved in that conversation.

You know the shape of it. You finally ask the hard question. Your partner answers fully, without defensiveness, and something drops in your chest that has been held for weeks. For a few hours you feel almost normal. You sleep. Then by four the next afternoon the same dread is sitting in exactly the same place, and nothing new has happened in between. The obvious conclusion is that the conversation was a lie, or that you are broken, and neither is usually correct.

What happened is that the conversation changed your state. States lift fast and fall just as fast. The belief that generated the fear, some version of *I cannot trust what I do not see* or *I was not enough*, was never addressed in that conversation, so it was still there when the state came down, and it immediately commissioned another round of questions.

There is a move here that costs nothing and changes the picture. While you are still in the calm, before it fades, ask yourself directly whether the specific fear is actually settled. Not whether you feel better. Whether the thing you were afraid of has genuinely stopped being frightening. Almost everybody who tries this finds the answer is no, and finding that out at nine in the evening while you feel steady is a great deal cheaper than finding it out at one in the morning while you do not.

That single test is what redirects the work away from another round of details and toward the belief itself, which is the only part of this that stays fixed once it moves. [The general version of why reassurance keeps running out of half-life sits on its own page](/patterns/seeking-constant-reassurance/), and it is worth reading if this predates the affair rather than starting with it.

## Why Do the Same Three or Four Scenes Keep Coming Back?

Because a handful of vivid, specific images carry most of the charge, and the replay narrows onto them rather than covering the timeline evenly.

Notice what that means. You are not replaying the affair. You are replaying three or four images, and most of them are ones you constructed yourself, of events you did not witness, assembled out of a sentence he said and a photograph you saw once. Imagined scenes are frequently sharper than real memories, because nothing about them is blurred by the ordinary friction of having actually been there. The one you cannot stop watching is often the one with the least evidence behind it.

The scenes also have a schedule, and the schedule is not random. One in the morning is when there is no competing task, no meeting to prepare for, nobody in the room needing something. The replay does not particularly want the night. It takes whatever space is unoccupied, which at one in the morning is all of it.

Two handoffs are worth making here rather than half-covering both. If your version of this runs less as picture-and-replay and more as body, a startle at the door, scanning a restaurant, never quite settling, that is [a threshold that got reset rather than a thinking problem](/patterns/hypervigilance/). And if you were already replaying scenes from years before any of this happened, [the general rewind](/patterns/why-cant-i-stop-thinking-about-the-past/) is the more useful page, because the affair may have loaded an existing mechanism rather than built a new one.

## Is This Processing, or Is It Replaying?

Processing changes something over successive passes. Replaying returns the same scene at the same charge and produces nothing new.

The test is unglamorous and reliable. If you can predict exactly how a memory is going to feel before you go into it, you are replaying it. Processing has some unpredictability in it, because something is actually being worked. Replaying is a rerun, and you know the ending.

This distinction is not folk wisdom. Response styles research separates a ruminative response to distress, passively and repeatedly focusing on the distress and on its causes, from an active, problem-solving one, and the longitudinal finding is that the ruminative style predicts longer and more severe distress rather than shorter. Which means the well-meant advice to give it time is only true when the time is spent doing something with the material. Time spent passively rerunning it is not neutral, and it does not gradually metabolize the event. It maintains it.

| Same memory, two operations | Processing it | Replaying it |
|---|---|---|
| The question being asked | What does this tell me about now | Why, how, what if |
| Where the attention points | At what you will do next | At the scene itself |
| What changes between passes | Something you notice or believe | Nothing |
| How it ends | You get up and do something | You get interrupted, or you fall asleep |
| How you feel afterward | Tired and slightly clearer | Tired and slightly worse |
| What a friend hears | A different version each time | The same version each time |
| How long it takes | Minutes | Whatever is available |

That last row is the practical tell, and it is the one to watch this week. Processing has a natural end. Replaying expands to fill whatever time it is given.

## Is the Pain Coming From What Happened, or From What You Called It?

From both, and only one of the two is still available to you.

What happened is a fact. The word you chose for it is a decision that arrived so fast it felt like part of the observation. Betrayed. Cheated on. Humiliated. Discarded. Lied to about something that mattered. Every one of those can be a true description of the same event, and they do not produce the same replay. The larger words carry a verdict about you folded inside them, and a verdict is what a scene replays at full intensity every single time.

None of this asks you to call an affair something smaller than it was. Minimizing is its own trap and it delays everything. The question is narrower: does the word you are using carry more certainty than the fact underneath it actually supports?

Here is a composite version of how that plays out, drawn from the pattern rather than from any one person. Someone discovers that their husband quietly saved money for years, in an account she knew nothing about, spending none of it. She processes the discovery through the same word she would have used for an affair: betrayed. Every replay of the moment she found the statement then runs as confirmed betrayal, at the same pitch, and never settles. What changed it was two clarifying questions. Did he spend it, or did he only fail to mention it? And where did a man who grew up with nothing learn what money was for? A second reading became available: a need for security pursued badly and privately, rather than proof of malice. The nondisclosure stayed wrong. The verdict got smaller, and the replays stopped arriving pre-decided.

The same operation applies to an affair, and it is more delicate there, which is why [the separate work of pulling the fact apart from the meaning you put on it has its own page](/patterns/can-someone-cheat-and-still-love-you/). Understanding what drove your partner's behavior and condoning that behavior are different acts. In acute pain the two collapse into one, so tracing a cause can feel like being asked to let somebody off the hook. A clear-eyed account of what drove it and a firm judgment that it was wrong can both be true at the same time, held by the same person, on the same afternoon.

## Is This a Recognized Response, or Is It Just You?

Recognized, documented, and expected, and knowing that usually has to land before any of the mechanism above becomes usable.

Most people arrive here privately convinced they are handling this worse than other people do. They are not. Clinical writing on infidelity has described the betrayed partner's experience for decades in terms that will sound familiar: intrusive images, constant scanning, and obsessive replay of the discovery and its details, a picture closer to an alarm response than to ordinary jealousy or grief. That description carries no diagnosis of you, and nobody can give you one from a page. What it does say is that whatever you are doing at one in the morning is a known response to a known kind of event.

Couple therapy adds a second piece that changes how the asking looks. Models of repairing a serious breach describe the injured partner needing to revisit and re-tell the event, sometimes many times, before the bond can settle. The retelling is a documented phase of the repair rather than evidence that something is wrong with the person doing it. So the instruction to stop bringing it up, which is what most people around you will eventually say, is bad advice delivered with good intentions.

Which leaves a real question: how do you tell the retelling that helps from the checking that does not, given they use the same words and happen at the same kitchen table? Three things separate them. Retelling is about your experience and what it was like for you. Checking is about their account and what exactly happened. Retelling changes shape over time. Checking asks the same question in new clothes. And retelling ends when you have been heard, whereas checking ends when the other person runs out of patience.

## Is This the Same Thing as Overthinking, Jealousy, or Trust Issues?

No. It gets folded into all three constantly, and each of those has a different mechanism and a different way out.

The distinguishing feature of the thing on this page: it is anchored to one discrete, confirmed event, and it is aimed at a question that no quantity of information can close. That makes it a self-fueling loop rather than a disposition, which is good news, because a loop has a specific place where it can be interrupted.

| What it can look like | What it might actually be | Where that belongs |
|---|---|---|
| Going over one confirmed event constantly | A checking loop with its own fuel supply | This page |
| Going over everything constantly | A general habit of thinking in circles | [Overthinking](/insights/what-is-overthinking/) |
| Replaying scenes from years before any of this | The rewind, aimed at things nobody breached | [Replaying the past](/patterns/why-cant-i-stop-thinking-about-the-past/) |
| Needing to be told you are loved, repeatedly | Reassurance-seeking with no specific event under it | [Seeking constant reassurance](/patterns/seeking-constant-reassurance/) |

The affair-specific loop tends to sit on top of whichever of the others was already installed, and it usually quiets first, because it has a definite event under it that can actually be worked.

## Where Does the Urge Actually Run in an Ordinary Week?

In more places than the obvious one, and several of them do not look like thinking about the affair at all.

1. **The phone, one more time.** Opening a call log, a message thread, or a location app an hour after telling yourself you were done. This is the version everybody recognizes, and it is not the most expensive one.
2. **The third version of the same question.** Asking about the timeline a third different way in one conversation, watching their face for something that was not there the first two times.
3. **The silence around it.** Telling nobody, to manage the shame or to protect them from the story. This removes the one outlet that reliably takes weight off the replay and leaves only the private version, which has no witness and no natural end.
4. **The extra four hours of work.** Converting relational uncertainty into output, because work is a domain where effort reliably produces results and this one currently is not. It reads as coping well. It is a way of being somewhere else.
5. **The standard held in one direction.** Condemning by an absolute rule, he should never lie, while doing something adjacent yourself without ever naming it the same way, then relitigating the rule mentally instead of testing it against your own record.
6. **Blame spread across every available target.** Circumstances, your partner, the other person, yourself, all at once. It feels thorough. It multiplies the material available to go over instead of narrowing toward one lever you could actually pull.
7. **The softer word.** Going through a difficult time, a bit of a rough patch, just transition. Useful for getting through a work call. Costly if it is still in use in month six, because a state nobody has named cannot be addressed by anybody, including you.

Number three is the one worth acting on first, and it is the one most people refuse. Telling one person, chosen carefully and not a member of either family, does more in a week than another month of private replaying will.

## What Do You Actually Have Authority Over Here?

Three things, and your partner is not among them.

What you put your attention on. What you decide the fact means. What you do in the next hour. That is the entire list, and it is short enough to be genuinely useful at one in the morning when the longer lists are unavailable.

Read that as an observation about where effort actually converts into change rather than as a claim that you caused any of this, or that fixing it falls to you. Effort aimed at interrogating a person who has already answered, or at an event that has already happened, converts into nothing at all, however much of it you spend. Effort aimed at those three things converts slowly and reliably.

There is a related trap sitting right next to this one. Deciding to stop and understanding why it happened are two separate operations, and most people wait for the second before attempting the first. So months go by circling the same explanation, which feels like progress toward a decision that never actually required the explanation to be finished. You can decide the loop is no longer serving you while the why is still open. Plenty of people never get a complete why, because their partner does not have one either.

## What Actually Moves It?

Small, repeated, and unglamorous work aimed at the loop rather than at the memory, over months.

Nothing below is a validated protocol, and anybody selling you one for this is overselling something. What follows is worked out from how the loop actually runs, and it is built to be practiced while it is running rather than understood on a calm afternoon.

1. **Test the calm while you are still inside it.** After a good conversation, before the relief fades, ask whether the specific fear is genuinely settled. Get in the habit of separating I feel better from that question is closed. Doing this two or three times permanently changes what you expect a conversation to be able to deliver.
2. **Sort every question into decision-relevant or scene-building.** Two columns. Ask the left column once, in a single sitting, and write down the answers so you do not have to keep them in your head. Read the right column as a list rather than asking it.
3. **Stop trying to not think about it.** Instructing yourself not to think about something reliably increases how often the thought arrives, particularly under load, and you are under load. Fighting the thought is still attention paid to the thought.
4. **Feed a different state instead of fighting the urge.** When the urge to check arrives, go deliberately into something concrete and absorbing: a task that needs both hands, a walk with a destination, a specific good memory you enter on purpose. The aim is to starve the loop rather than to win an argument with it.
5. **Give the replay a window instead of a war.** A fixed twenty minutes, at a fixed time, at a desk rather than in bed, with a notebook. When it arrives outside the window, note the time and tell it to come back at six. Widely used, occasionally silly-feeling, and considerably more effective than trying to suppress it all day.
6. **Change what the passes are for.** When you find yourself in the scene, switch the question from why did this happen to what does this tell me about the next twenty-four hours. Same material, different operation, and the second one has an end.
7. **Name the word you are using and check it against the fact.** Write the event in one factual line with no adjectives, then write the word you have been using for it underneath. Sometimes they match. When they do not, the gap is where a great deal of the intensity has been living.
8. **Let the felt sense of safety rebuild on calendar time.** It comes from your partner's consistent, checkable behavior across months, and it does not come from further interrogation of the past. [What each person actually owes during that stretch is set out here](/patterns/how-to-rebuild-trust/).
9. **Tell one person, once, properly.** Chosen carefully. Not to get advice.
10. **Treat a flare-up as a flare-up.** Intrusive replay commonly continues in flashes for weeks or months after somebody has already decided to stop, and treating that as proof of failure is exactly what restarts the cycle it was trying to end.

Is any of that comfortable? No. Number five in particular feels ridiculous for about the first four days. The discomfort is the mechanism rather than a side effect, because the capacity being built is the ability to leave a question open and go to sleep anyway, and that only grows against something.

## Does This Ever Actually Stop?

It recedes, and the honest unit of measurement is the gap between episodes rather than their presence.

Two speeds are worth separating. Recognition arrives fast. Most people can catch themselves mid-loop within two or three weeks of paying deliberate attention, and catching it counts even on the nights you go ahead and open the phone anyway. Watching yourself ask the question while knowing exactly what you are doing feels like failure and is genuinely a step forward.

The loop itself takes far longer, because the relief that maintains it is real. The earliest honest version of progress is noticing an hour afterward. Then twenty minutes afterward. Then during. Then, eventually, early enough that a choice exists. Where both people are actively doing the work, six months to two years is the range most often described for the felt sense of safety to catch up with a decision to stay, and being told that plainly at the start saves a great deal of self-blame at month four.

A few honest complications. Anniversaries, the season it happened in, a place, a song, and any genuine ambiguity in your partner's behavior will all reopen it temporarily, and that is expected rather than disqualifying. Load makes it worse: illness, a job loss, a bad month with the kids. And the loop does not automatically end with the relationship. Leaving removes the person and leaves the belief exactly where it was, which is why some people are surprised to find themselves still running scenes two years after a divorce they wanted. The work is the same work either way.

## What Does the Other End of This Look Like?

Considerably duller than you are imagining, and made mostly of things that stop happening.

| Inside the loop | The other end of it | On an ordinary Tuesday |
|---|---|---|
| The scene arrives and you go into it | The scene arrives and you notice it arriving | You are halfway to the kettle before you register that you did not follow it |
| Asking a question you can predict the answer to | Asking a question you cannot predict | You ask about the weekend rather than about last March |
| Relief that lasts two hours | Relief that stays put | You are still working from Tuesday's answer on Thursday |
| Opening the phone one more time | The phone being just a phone | It sits on the counter all evening and nothing pulls at you |
| A word that carries a verdict about you | A word the size of the actual fact | He lied to me about something that mattered |
| Hours a week spent in a room you have never seen | Hours a week spent in the one you live in | The evening goes wherever the evening was going |
| Telling nobody | One person who knows the whole thing | Somebody asks how you are and you answer honestly |
| Watching their face for a tell | Watching what they actually do over months | You notice he did the thing he said he would, again |

Read that middle column looking for the moment it becomes impressive. There is no such moment, and the flatness is the actual content.

Be careful what you expect from the far end. It is rarely never thinking about it. For most people it becomes a thought that arrives, gets recognized, and costs four minutes instead of a night. That is a much smaller promise than most of what you will read about this, and it has the advantage of being available.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From four separate research traditions, none of which was built for affairs specifically, plus a body of clinical writing that was. Every one of them can be checked without going through me.

Paul Salkovskis, a British clinical psychologist, set out a cognitive-behavioral account of obsessional problems in a 1985 paper and developed it across the two decades after it. The part that carries weight here is the safety behavior: checking, reassurance-seeking, and mental reviewing performed to keep a feared outcome from being true. The repeatedly replicated finding is that safety behaviors block the disconfirmation that would settle the fear, because the person credits the behavior rather than the absence of the feared event, so the belief underneath is never actually tested.

Susan Nolen-Hoeksema's response styles theory, published from 1991 onward, separates a ruminative response to distress from an active problem-solving one and found, in longitudinal work, that the ruminative style predicts longer and more severe distress. Her 2008 review with colleagues, *Rethinking Rumination*, is the most readable summary of where that work landed.

Daniel Wegner's thought-suppression experiments, beginning with the white bear studies reported in 1987, are the reason nobody serious will tell you to simply stop thinking about it. Instructing somebody not to think about something reliably increases how often the thought arrives, and it gets worse under cognitive load, which is precisely the condition you are in.

Shirley Glass, an American psychologist who spent her career on infidelity, described the betrayed partner's experience as an alarm reaction carrying intrusive images and constant scanning, most accessibly in her 2003 book *Not Just Friends*. The Gottman Institute's published framework for affair recovery, written for general readers in *What Makes Love Last?* in 2012, describes a similar symptom picture and separates the repair into distinct phases rather than treating it as one long apology.

Susan Johnson and colleagues named the attachment injury in a 2001 paper on impasses in couples therapy: an event that violates a partner's basic expectation of being able to depend on the other person at a moment of need. Their clinical model documents the injured partner needing to revisit and re-tell that event, sometimes repeatedly, before the bond can settle. That is the checkable name for what may be happening at your kitchen table at eleven at night.

The rest of this is mine and carries no research weight at all. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, none of this is therapy, and none of the researchers named has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Ask Another Question About It?

Run the two-column list for one week, and answer nothing while you do it. Open a note on your phone with two headings: *Questions that change what I do* and *Questions that only build a scene*. Every time a question arrives, and they will arrive at one in the morning and in the supermarket and mid-meeting, write it under one of the two headings. Do not ask any of them yet. Do not answer any of them yet. Just file them.

At the end of the week, read the right-hand column on its own. Two things usually surprise people. The first is how short it is. Most people are running three or four questions, on repeat, for months, in slightly different clothes each time, and seeing them collapse into a list of four on a screen does something that arguing with yourself at one in the morning has never once managed. The second is how few items ended up on the left. Take that left column, ask it once, in one sitting, and write the answers down so you never have to hold them in your head again. That costs you a week of noticing and nothing else, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever go anywhere.
