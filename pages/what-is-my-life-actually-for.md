# I have what I said I wanted. Why does none of it mean anything?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because the life was built to satisfy a private specification you never chose: values, and rules attached to them, assembled in childhood around whatever earned love from the person whose love was least reliable. The conditions are fine. The scorecard grading them is old, harsher than you would pick now, and it was never rewritten once the emergency that produced it ended.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
Some version of these has run in your head recently, on a day when nothing was going wrong.

- "I got what I said I wanted and it did not feel like anything."
- "I feel I have everything. But there is something missing."
- "I am fine, mostly. There is just this thing underneath that never quite goes away."
- "I don't like what I'm doing day to day. I should be doing something else with my life. I just have these images of what I should be doing that I'm not."
- "I'm wasting my life."

The cost is a life that satisfies every condition it was built to satisfy and still gets marked incomplete every morning.

</div>

You open the banking app on a Tuesday morning and the number sitting there is the one you spent nine years working toward. You look at it for about four seconds, close the app, and go make the coffee.

Nothing moved. No relief, no pride, not even the small private flicker you had assumed would arrive on the day this finally happened. Those four seconds are the whole subject of this page, and if you recognized them, you can stop reading the parts of the internet that will tell you to try harder or want less.

One plain line before anything else, because it comes first. If what you are carrying has gone past flatness into a run of months where nothing has felt like anything, sleep or appetite that has clearly changed, drinking or working in a way you have privately stopped being able to stop, a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, or any thought of harming yourself, however briefly it passes, bring in a licensed professional rather than a page. That is a resourcing question, and it gets answered before any of this becomes useful.

Four neighboring questions get typed into a search bar in almost the same words as this one, and each of them has a different answer, so it is worth spending twenty seconds on which one is yours. If the life has already stopped working and what you need is a way to rebuild, [starting over is its own subject with its own three moves](/patterns/how-to-start-over/). If every achievement gets discounted by the following morning and the striving itself has become the problem, [the treadmill runs on a different mechanism](/patterns/why-is-nothing-ever-enough/). If you have lost contact with what you actually feel and cannot find the words for any of it, [that is the lost version, and it starts somewhere else entirely](/insights/feeling-lost-in-life/). If you are trying to locate the work, the contribution, the thing you are supposed to be doing, [purpose splits into two halves and most people search the wrong one](/insights/how-to-find-your-purpose/). This page stays on one narrow question underneath all four: what the thing you built was ever supposed to deliver, and why it did not.

## What Does This Actually Sound Like From the Inside?

Flat, reasonable, and oddly polite. People carrying this rarely arrive sounding distressed, which is part of why it goes unexamined for so long and why almost nobody around them notices.

The sentences below are what people say about it in their own phrasing, rather than a tidied version. They recur with unnerving consistency across people who have never met, work in unrelated fields, and would each describe their situation as entirely specific to them.

- "I came to take my life to the next level. It's already pretty high, but I can go higher."
- "I need to figure out what fulfills me in life."
- "I do not know why I am tired. I barely did anything today."
- "I have no doubt that I have. I mean, they are everything to me, but I'm tired of doing it."
- "I've had enough."
- "I struggled with going through a whole heap of stuff and thinking it was in vain, and I thought, well, it only is if I don't do anything with it."

Read them slowly. What matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word, with no translation required.

Notice how few of them sound like a wound. From inside, this presents as ambition, as taking things seriously, as a healthy refusal to coast. Nobody goes looking for help with being competent, which is a large part of why this can run for twenty years while every visible measure keeps improving.

There is a second tell, and it is audible rather than internal. Listen to yourself describe your own achievements out loud to somebody who asks. If the register is flat and slightly third-person, the way you would read a stranger's file, that flatness is information. A life built against a personal specification usually gets described with some charge in it, even a complicated charge. A life built against somebody else's tends to get reported.

## How Do You Tell This Apart From Burnout, Grief, or Depression?

By what changes when the load comes off. That single test separates most of these, and it is worth running before you accept any label, including the one on this page.

Burnout responds to rest and to reduced demand. Take three genuine weeks off, hand the hardest thing to somebody else, sleep properly, and burnout eases measurably. What this page describes does not move at all under those conditions. Plenty of people discover it precisely during a long-awaited break, on a beach they paid real money to reach, unable to explain why the week is not landing.

| What you are noticing | What it usually is | The thing that separates it |
|---|---|---|
| A target hit on schedule that produced nothing | The pattern on this page | The conditions are fine and the standard grading them is old |
| Exhaustion, cynicism, no capacity for work you used to care about | Burnout | Rest and reduced load change it measurably within weeks |
| A sudden pull to blow up the marriage, the job, the city | The crisis version | The move goes at the conditions instead of at the standard |
| No contact with what you feel about anything, including this | Losing touch with feeling | [Feeling lost has its own starting point](/insights/feeling-lost-in-life/) |
| Every win discounted by the next morning, then the next target | The achievement treadmill | [The striving itself is doing a second job](/patterns/why-is-nothing-ever-enough/) |
| Not recognizing the person whose life this apparently is | Identity drift | [Who you are is the live question, not what it was for](/patterns/i-dont-know-who-i-am-anymore/) |
| Months where nothing has felt like anything, with sleep and appetite changed | Belongs with a clinician | Duration, physical signs, and whether it lifts for anything at all |

Grief deserves its own line, since it gets confused with this constantly and behaves nothing like it. Grief has an object. It arrives in waves with gaps between them, it attaches to a specific person or a specific ending, and it moves. This flatness has no object anybody can name, which is the single most confusing thing about it and the reason people conclude there must be something wrong with them personally.

Don't judge yourself if you have spent a year trying on labels for this. The absence of a nameable cause is a feature of the pattern rather than evidence of your own vagueness.

## What Is the Blueprint, and Who Wrote Yours?

A private, mostly unconscious specification for how life is supposed to go, how other people are supposed to behave, and what you personally have to be. Everybody runs one. Almost nobody has read theirs.

It gets assembled young, and it gets assembled around one practical question a child is genuinely trying to solve: what do I have to become to earn love from the person whose love is least reliably available. At six years old that is an operational question rather than a philosophical one, and children answer it with real intelligence, using the evidence in the room. The answer becomes a set of standing instructions, and the instructions keep running long after the room is gone.

Three things live in the blueprint and they are worth separating, because they behave differently and only one of them is easy to change.

1. **The values.** What is allowed to feel good. Security, contribution, freedom, being worth something, being close to people. These tend to be genuine and worth keeping.
2. **The rules.** The specific conditions that must be satisfied before a value counts as met. This is where the trouble almost always sits, and it is also the one layer that moves easily.
3. **The vehicles.** The actual job, marriage, business, cause, or target through which the value gets pursued. This is the only layer most people ever examine.

Laid out side by side, the shape becomes obvious quickly.

| The value | The rule attached to it | The vehicle it got pursued through |
|---|---|---|
| Security | There has to be enough that nothing can ever go wrong again | A career, a mortgage cleared early, a business carrying no debt |
| Being worth something | I am worth something on the days I produce something | Promotions, revenue, a visible record other people can check |
| Being loved | I am loved when I am the one everybody can rely on | Being the person who handles it, at home and at work |
| Contribution | It only counts if it costs me something | Service, caretaking, a cause, an unpaid second job |
| Freedom | I am free once the last obligation is discharged | The number, the exit, the someday |

Look at the middle column. Every one of those rules was written by somebody young, under pressure, working with limited information, and every one of them sets a threshold that a whole life can be spent failing to cross. Nobody sat down and consciously agreed to any of them. They were inferred, and then they were never reviewed, because a specification that feels like reality does not present itself as a document you are allowed to edit.

That inheritance also explains why external validation from a different audience never closes the gap. The rule was written to satisfy one specific, scarce audience. A spouse, a market, a following, a room of people applauding: none of them are the audience the rule was set for, so their approval arrives and slides off. [Beliefs installed this early have their own mechanics and their own way of resisting evidence](/patterns/limiting-beliefs/), and [the case where worth itself hangs on output is worth reading on its own terms](/patterns/conditional-self-worth/).

## Why Does the Vehicle Deliver and the Need Stay Open?

Because nobody perceives a need directly. You perceive the vehicle, so that is what you go and get.

Nobody has ever felt the sentence "my need for unconditional worth is currently unmet." What gets felt is "I have everything and it means nothing," which is the same information arriving in a form that points at the furniture instead of at the mechanism. This is why the experience shows up as a diffuse absence rather than as a locatable complaint. There is no missing condition to point at, because the mismatch was never in the conditions.

A vehicle can perform flawlessly and still fail to deliver, because a vehicle is a route rather than a destination. The business succeeds. The house gets bought. The debt goes to zero. The children turn out well. Each of those was recruited to carry something, and the carrying was never checked. [The six needs everybody runs continuously, and the way vehicles get attached to them, is set out in full elsewhere](/patterns/six-human-needs/), as is [the older question of what the chase itself is reaching for](/insights/the-four-human-longings/).

Abraham Maslow put the public version of this in a 1943 paper, proposing that human needs sit in a rough hierarchy and that satisfying a lower one does not automatically satisfy a higher one. That is the checkable, unglamorous form of what is happening here. Security got met. Esteem, arguably, got met. The need to actually live according to something you selected yourself sits above both of them and stayed entirely untouched by either.

Edward Deci and Richard Ryan spent decades on the empirical half of the same point. Their work on motivation holds that well-being depends on satisfying three innate needs, autonomy, competence, and relatedness, and their research program with Tim Kasser found that pursuing extrinsic goals such as money, image, and status at the expense of intrinsic ones predicts lower well-being even when the extrinsic goals are achieved. That finding is the thing worth sitting with. Achieved, in full, on schedule, and it still predicts the flatness.

## Why Doesn't Finding a Bigger Purpose Fix It?

Because every version of that advice operates on the vehicle while leaving the rule exactly where it was. Three failure modes recur, and it is worth being able to spot which one you are currently inside.

1. **A bigger goal resets the same structure at a higher altitude.** If a goal is held as an all-or-nothing finish line, arriving there discharges accumulated fear as relief rather than producing satisfaction. Setting a larger goal rebuilds the identical structure one level up. You will arrive there too, on schedule, competent as ever, and report the same flatness with a bigger number attached.
2. **The search becomes another compulsive vehicle.** For someone whose blueprint runs on achievement as proof of worth, hunting for purpose is more striving in the same key that produced the exhaustion. This is why some of the most purpose-driven people you know describe the emptiest interiors. The searching is being graded by the same rule as everything else.
3. **A new audience cannot satisfy an old rule.** Applause from strangers does not settle a condition set for one specific person twenty-five years ago, so the validation arrives, feels good for an afternoon, and changes nothing structural.

Viktor Frankl is the most established public source for the first half of this. His account in *Man's Search for Meaning*, published in 1946, and the school of psychotherapy he built from it hold that the primary human drive is the search for meaning, and, importantly for anybody currently shopping for one, that meaning cannot be handed to a person. It gets found through work, through love, or through the stance taken toward suffering that cannot be avoided. A purpose selected from a list because it sounded like the right shape has the same problem as an inherited rule: somebody else specified it.

The second half has a name and a research base too. Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell described hedonic adaptation in a 1971 paper, and later researchers including Sonja Lyubomirsky extended it: people adapt to both good and bad changes in circumstance and drift back toward a fairly stable baseline of satisfaction. A major achievement produces a real lift, and then the lift decays. That is ordinary human wiring rather than a personal failure, and no amount of wanting it differently changes the decay curve.

## Is Your Goal a Finish Line or a Threshold?

Ask what happens the day after. That question separates the two structures faster than any amount of examining the goal itself, and the difference decides whether arrival can feel like anything.

A terminal goal borrows its entire emotional charge from the fear of not reaching it. All the energy in the pursuit is fear energy, which is genuinely effective as fuel and has one structural consequence: when you arrive, what discharges is the fear. Discharge feels like relief, relief is quiet, and quiet lands where you were expecting something loud. A threshold goal is a point passed through on the way somewhere else. Nothing terminal is riding on it, so the achievement can register as an achievement rather than as the end of a threat.

| | Held as a finish line | Held as a threshold |
|---|---|---|
| What fuels the pursuit | Fear of not arriving | Interest in what is on the other side |
| What arrival produces | Relief, then a gap | Satisfaction, then a next step already visible |
| What a delay means | A verdict on you | A change to the schedule |
| What an imperfect result means | The whole thing failed | Most of it counted |
| What happens the following morning | A new target, chosen quickly | The same direction, continued |
| What it costs to hold | Sustained low-grade dread | Ordinary effort |

The exhaustion people describe around this is usually the finish-line structure rather than the workload. Carrying a terminal goal for nine years means carrying nine years of low-grade dread underneath every ordinary week, and the dread is what wore you out. Rewriting the same goal as a threshold changes almost nothing about the work and changes the entire experience of doing it.

## What Are You Actually Grading Yourself Against?

Something you have almost certainly never written down. The whole point of an unexamined standard is that it operates as reality, so the first job is turning it back into a sentence with words in it.

Three distinctions do most of the work here, and each one is easy to state and slightly uncomfortable to check.

**Sincere effort versus sincere effect.** You can be entirely sincere in building the life you believed would deliver meaning, and the life can still fail to deliver it. Sincerity of intent and completeness of effect are separate measurements. From the inside, meaning what you built feels indistinguishable from the building having worked, which is why the gap surfaces as an unaccountable flatness rather than as an obvious error. Nothing about this makes you a fraud. It makes you somebody who followed a specification faithfully.

**Being on plan versus being okay.** If the actual standard is adherence to a plan, a genuinely good outcome that arrives off schedule still registers as a violation. Said out loud that sounds ridiculous, and for a plan-dependent person it is completely accurate, because the distress was always about control rather than about the result. Watch how you respond the next time something good happens at the wrong time.

**Should versus want, before either has been named.** Asked in general terms what is missing, most people produce a list of duties adopted from outside themselves. That list is not the same as what they actually want, and the two rarely get compared, because a should presents itself as an objective fact about responsibility rather than as one option among several that was adopted by default.

That last one has a five-minute version. Two columns, one page. On the left, everything you think you should be doing with your life. On the right, everything you actually want, written without editing for reasonableness or for how it would sound to your mother. Most people find the right-hand column embarrassingly modest: more time with specific people, work that interests them, some ease, an afternoon that belongs to them. It looks too small to be the real answer, which is precisely the problem. To somebody trained to treat effort as the only legitimate currency, values like presence and ease do not read as a serious answer, so they get dismissed for decades in favor of something more impressive and less wanted.

There is a version of this that looks like sabotage from outside. A project stalls, a long-held goal goes dormant, and the person explains it with a dramatic story about a hidden agenda to fail or some deep unworthiness. That story is more identity-consistent for a high performer than the plain explanation, since secret self-sabotage at least sounds like an interesting problem. The plainer mechanism is usually the accurate one: the person is already living what they actually value, and grading the results against an achievement-based standard those values were never aimed at satisfying. [The genuine version of self-sabotage exists and looks different](/patterns/self-sabotage/), which is worth checking before accepting the more flattering diagnosis.

## Why Does Doing Everything for Everybody Leave You Emptier?

Because compliance and the outcome compliance was supposed to buy have quietly come apart, and nothing in the arrangement announces when that happens.

A blueprint sets conditions: be successful, be responsible, be available, be the one who handles it. A person can satisfy every single one of those, for decades, at genuine cost, and still not receive the amount of love, safety, or meaning the compliance was supposed to purchase. As long as compliance continues, the felt logic holds. More of this will eventually get me what I want. That logic stays intact long after the strategy has been tested for twenty years without delivering, because testing it would require stopping, and stopping is the one move the rule forbids.

There is a specific tell for this. Notice what happens when somebody declines your help. If it lands as a scheduling change, the helping is freely chosen. If it lands as a threat, the helping is doing identity work, and every act of service is also risk management for your own worth. That version is exhausting in a way generosity is not, and it explains the strange, guilty tiredness of people who are unambiguously good to everyone around them. [The compulsion to fix everybody has its own page](/patterns/why-do-i-always-try-to-fix-everyone/), and so does [the guilt that shows up the moment you rest](/patterns/why-do-i-feel-guilty-when-i-rest/).

Here is the part that catches people off guard. Watching the people you did everything for turn out perfectly fine often makes the hole bigger rather than smaller. The service had been carrying something. When the service is no longer required, whatever it was carrying arrives unmediated, and it arrives at exactly the moment everybody expects you to be relieved.

## Why Does This Surface at Forty-Five and Not at Twenty-Five?

Because the first-half strategy finally finishes delivering, and the question it had been deferring comes due. Building an identity, a career, and a family occupies decades, and while the building continues, what any of it is for stays comfortably out of view.

Carl Jung wrote about this directly in his essay "The Stages of Life," arguing that the psychological program suited to the morning of life is structurally unsuited to the afternoon, and that a person who keeps running the morning's program into the afternoon will experience precisely this kind of flatness, however well the morning went. That last clause is the one worth underlining. The flatness shows up most reliably in people who built extremely well, because they are the ones who reached the end of the building and found out what was waiting there.

Erik Erikson placed the same territory in a developmental sequence, naming the middle-adulthood task as generativity, contributing to something beyond yourself, against the alternative of stagnation. He put it at midlife specifically, independent of how much a person had achieved. Between the two of them, a fairly practical conclusion emerges: the question is a scheduled event rather than a personal failure, and turning up to it early is an advantage rather than a symptom.

An outdated target explains the rest of it. A life built to satisfy an earlier version of you, or a parent's version, can be fully achieved while the dominant need underneath has already moved on. Hitting the target then produces flatness for a very simple reason. Nobody currently living is asking for that target anymore. The natural reading of that flatness is ingratitude or a defect in you. The more accurate reading is that a specification aged out while you kept executing it faithfully, which is what a conscientious person would do.

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because four things get confused with this constantly and each one points somewhere different.

| What you are seeing | What it might actually be | The thing that tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| A settled life that feels stable and slightly bad | Familiarity with a state you never chose | Would you pick this again, knowing exactly what it costs |
| Flatness you read as ingratitude | A mismatch between conditions and specification | Gratitude practice helps the first and does nothing for the second |
| A stalled project you explain as secret self-sabotage | Real values being graded by an effort-based standard | Look at what you have recently chosen freely and at real cost |
| Restlessness you read as needing a bigger goal | A rule that never turns green at any altitude | Whether the last three goals, once hit, changed anything internally |
| Constant helping you read as generosity | Worth being managed through service | What happens in you when somebody declines the help |
| Instant acceptance of bad news as meant to be | A reframe protecting a plan-dependent belief | Whether any feeling registered before the meaning arrived |

The familiarity row is the one people underestimate. A state can be entirely stable, thoroughly known, and quietly unpleasant, and the stability alone will keep it in place for decades. [What your default emotional state is, and how it gets set, is a separate subject worth its own hour](/patterns/emotional-home/).

## What Actually Moves It?

Three steps, in order, over months. The honest version is that the insight alone almost never holds, and the sequence matters as much as the content.

**First, see the specification as a specification.** Name where it came from and whose approval it was originally built to earn. A rule that has an author and a date stops being reality and becomes an editable artifact. This step is done badly by most people, because they name the value rather than the rule. The value is usually fine. The rule is the thing with an impossible number in it.

**Second, keep what is genuinely good in it.** This step gets skipped constantly and skipping it is why the whole project stalls. Somebody whose entire operating system feels under attack will defend all of it, including the parts that are hurting them. So name the real generosity, the actual competence, the sincerity of the effort, out loud, first. Nothing gets edited while everything is being protected at once.

**Third, rewrite the rule rather than the value.** This is the actual lever, and it is the least identity-threatening move available, because nobody has an emotional investment in defending the precise threshold at which something is allowed to count. "It only counts if it costs me something" can become "it counts when it helps, and cost is not the measure." That sentence looks trivially small on a page, and it decides whether the next twenty years produce the same result or a different one.

What that looks like in practice, over a few months rather than a few minutes:

1. **Write the rule out as a sentence with a number in it.** Take one thing in your life that is objectively fine and finish this: "This would count if ___." The specificity is the exercise. Vague versions cannot be argued with, which is exactly how they survive.
2. **Find the author.** Whose voice is that sentence in. Most people know within four seconds and spend a while resisting the answer.
3. **Ask what that person's love actually required.** Rarely "be happy." Usually something narrow: be successful, be no trouble, be the responsible one, be better than they were.
4. **Say the good part out loud before touching the bad part.** The rule protected something. Name what.
5. **Convert one terminal goal into a threshold.** Take the biggest thing you are carrying and write the sentence that comes after it. If nothing comes after it, that goal has been holding your whole horizon.
6. **Run the two-column page.** Should on the left, want on the right, no editing on the right.
7. **Do one small thing from the right-hand column this week.** Small enough that it needs no justification and no announcement.
8. **Leave one ordinary bad event unreframed for twenty-four hours.** Resist the instant meaning. Notice what shows up in the space where the reframe usually goes.
9. **Watch the rule fire for a month without obeying it.** Not arguing with it, not fixing it. Just noticing it in flight, which is what eventually strips the authority out of it.

Is any of that comfortable? No. Step two in particular asks you to praise the thing you came here to dismantle, and step nine is weeks of noticing with nothing to show anybody. It takes real, freaking patience, and it works in a way that a dramatic weekend of insight reliably does not.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Months, not minutes, and the honest marker is that the rewrite holds only after it has survived several months of ordinary life. Real payments, real setbacks, real temptation to revert to the old standard when something goes wrong. An insight accepted in a room and a specification actually rewritten are separated by that stretch, and nothing skips it.

Recognition, on the other hand, is fast. Most people can name their rule inside an afternoon once somebody asks the right question, and can name its author about four seconds after that. The speed of the recognition is what makes the slowness of the rest so frustrating, and it is also what convinces people the work is finished when it has barely started.

Expect the old standard to reassert itself under load. A bad quarter, an illness, a difficult stretch with someone you love, and the original rule will be back at full volume, grading everything, sounding entirely reasonable. Progress shows up in how quickly you notice what is running rather than in whether it runs.

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. The thing I had wrong the longest was assuming the answer would be a bigger and better thing to do. It was a smaller and much more boring sentence about when something is allowed to count. Where it sits for me now is worth saying plainly: the old rule still shows up, most often after a week that went badly, and it takes hours rather than months to lose its authority. I would rather tell you that than describe a finished version of something nobody finishes.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From six public bodies of work, none of which require going through me to check.

Viktor Frankl set out logotherapy and the will to meaning in *Man's Search for Meaning* in 1946, arguing that the primary human drive is the search for meaning and that meaning has to be found rather than handed over. Carl Jung's essay "The Stages of Life" is the original widely cited source for the idea that a fully successful life strategy can be the wrong specification once its season has passed. Erik Erikson placed generativity against stagnation as the central task of middle adulthood in his eight-stage model of psychosocial development, which is the most direct developmental account of why this question surfaces at midlife specifically. Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell introduced hedonic adaptation in their 1971 paper "Hedonic Relativism and Planning the Good Society," later extended by researchers including Sonja Lyubomirsky, and it is the established empirical explanation for an achievement producing only a temporary lift. Edward Deci and Richard Ryan built self-determination theory around autonomy, competence, and relatedness, and their work with Tim Kasser on extrinsic versus intrinsic aspirations supplies the checkable version of this page's central claim. Abraham Maslow's 1943 paper "A Theory of Human Motivation" gives the public vocabulary for why meeting a security or esteem need leaves a separate, higher-order need entirely untouched.

The rest is mine and carries no research weight at all: the five years and five thousand hours described above. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, and I am not a therapist or a counselor. None of the people named here has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, go and check theirs.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Change Anything About Your Life?

Take one thing in your life that is objectively fine and write down, in a single sentence, the condition that would have to be true before you would let it count. Then, underneath it, write whose voice that sentence is in. Two lines, five minutes, on your phone.

Read the first line back the next morning, when you are unimpressed by yourself. Two things usually happen. The condition turns out to be numerically impossible, or so vague that no achievement could ever satisfy it, which is not an accident and is the whole reason it has survived. And the name underneath it is usually somebody who stopped grading you a very long time ago, and in some cases somebody who is no longer alive. Seeing those two lines together does something that arguing with yourself about gratitude has never once managed. That costs you five minutes and nothing else, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever go anywhere.
