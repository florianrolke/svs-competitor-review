# Can someone cheat on you and still love you?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Yes. Fidelity and love answer two different questions, and an affair reports on the first while leaving the second wide open. What it does report is which ongoing need stopped being supplied inside the relationship, and which vehicle supplied it instead. Both facts can be true at once: the years were real, and something went unmet for a long time.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
If it came out recently, some version of these has already run through your head this week.

- "I feel my husband doesn't really love me."
- "I tried to confirm it."
- "And he betrayed me."
- "I still love very deeply."
- "I don't trust him anymore."

Two of those sentences contradict each other, and being unable to put either one down is most of what this actually feels like from the inside.

You find a text, a receipt, a login session left open, and the whole relationship reorganizes itself around that one artifact in under a minute. By evening you are reading the last two years backward, and every ordinary thing in them, the trip in March, the stretch where he was tired for most of the spring, the Sunday he was unusually kind, has been handed a second meaning it did not have on the day it happened.

Somewhere in that same week you catch yourself doing the math on how long you were already unhappy before he did anything, quietly trying to work out who broke it first. Then you stop, because the question feels disloyal to your own pain, and you go back to the ledger of every kind thing he has done in the last year, as if enough data points could settle it.

Before anything else on this page: if you are not safe where you live, or if any part of you has been thinking about not being here, that belongs with a licensed professional and with people who can help you get somewhere safe, today, ahead of anything written here. A fuller version of that boundary sits a few paragraphs down.

Almost nobody types "how do we rebuild trust" in week one. What gets typed at two in the morning is the question at the top of this page, phrased fifteen slightly different ways, with the relationship word swapped each time to check whether your exact situation still counts. It does. That question comes first, before any decision about repair, forgiveness, or leaving is genuinely on the table, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a lecture about your options.

This page answers that one question only. If you have already accepted what happened and want to know whether the relationship can be repaired, [the repair project has its own page and its own timeline](/patterns/how-to-rebuild-trust/). ## What Does This Sound Like From the Inside?

Like two people talking in one head, and both of them meaning it.

The sentences below are how this gets said out loud when nobody is being careful. They are worth reading slowly, because what matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word. This first group is the grievance, and it is usually specific and usually accurate:

- "He doesn't spend time with me. He only asks me to do things, and he doesn't want me to restrict his freedom."
- "And he betrayed me."

This second group came from the same person, in the same conversation, minutes apart:

- "I know he loves me."
- "I still love very deeply."
- "He's loved me even when I've rejected him, lied to him and withdrew from him. Am I one lucky woman."

Both groups are honest. Nobody in that position is confused about the facts, and nobody is being naive. What is happening is that two accurate reports about the same relationship have arrived at the same time, and the first week offers no way to file them together.

Notice what the first group is actually made of. Time, attention, and freedom. It reads as a list of complaints and it functions as a coverage report: here is what stopped being supplied, and here is what got supplied instead. The second group reports on something different, and the two do not sit on the same axis at all.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, near the top, because this boundary matters more than everything below it put together.

If you are frightened of your partner, if leaving would put you at risk, or if the affair sits inside a wider pattern where money, movement, or contact with your own family has been controlled, that belongs with a licensed professional and with a domestic abuse service in your country, not with a page about meaning. The same holds if any thought of harming yourself has shown up, however briefly, or if you have not slept or eaten properly in more than a few days. Grief this size can flatten a person physically, and asking for help in week one costs far less than asking in month twelve.

Two practical items belong in the same paragraph, because they are easy to lose in the emotional weather. Get tested if there is any possibility of exposure, without apology and without waiting for a conversation to go well first. And find out what has been happening with shared money, since financial concealment often travels with the other kind and is far easier to address early.

Everything below is written for the ordinary version of an awful week: the discovery, the reread of the last two years, the question that will not close. It carries no clinical qualification behind it, and it is not a substitute for a couples therapist or a counselor, both of which are worth having if you can get them.

## What Is an Affair Actually Reporting?

Which of your partner's ongoing needs stopped being supplied inside the relationship, and what supplied it instead. That is the entire report, and it says almost nothing about whether you were loved.

Everyone runs the same short list of drivers continuously, whether or not they could name them: certainty, variety, significance, love and connection, growth, and contribution. [Those six have their own page here](/patterns/six-human-needs/), and the part that matters for this one is simple. The needs themselves do not change. The only real variables are which activity, person, or role supplies each one, at what dose, and whether that supply holds up over years.

A long relationship is extremely good at some of these and structurally weak at others.

| The driver | What a settled relationship usually supplies well | Where it quietly runs short |
|---|---|---|
| Certainty | Enormously well. Someone knows where you are, what Sunday looks like, who to call from a hospital | Almost never the problem, and its abundance is what makes the next row possible |
| Variety | Poorly, and by design. The same kitchen, the same argument, the same route to work | This is the one that goes missing first, without anybody noticing it leave |
| Significance | Unevenly. Being needed is not the same as being admired, and long partnerships run on the first | Nobody at home is surprised by you anymore, which is the cost of being fully known |
| Love and connection | Usually well, and often it is the last thing standing | It can persist at full strength while everything above it has thinned out |
| Growth | Depends entirely on whether either person is still building anything | A decade of stability can look identical to a decade of standing still |
| Contribution | Often well, through children, family, and shared work | Can quietly become the only vehicle either person has left |

Read the variety row again, because it is the structurally interesting one. Everything that makes a relationship safe is the same thing that makes it unsurprising. Predictability and novelty pull against each other, so a partnership that is winning on certainty is usually paying for it somewhere else, and neither person did anything wrong to arrive there.

Now put a new person into that gap. Someone new is, by definition, unknown, and unknown supplies variety at a dose no established relationship can match. Someone new has no history with you, so their admiration arrives uncomplicated by the last fifteen years, which supplies significance at a speed a spouse cannot compete with. None of that requires the marriage to have been bad. It requires only that two of the six had been running dry long enough for attention to drift toward whatever could supply them.

This is the argument the psychotherapist Esther Perel made publicly in *The State of Affairs* in 2017: that infidelity is often connected to the unfaithful partner's search for a lost sense of aliveness or identity rather than functioning as a verdict on the relationship or on the person betrayed. Her version is worth reading in full, and it is available anywhere without going through me.

One line has to be held on every paragraph of this page. A mechanism explains how something happened. It never makes it acceptable, and nothing here is written to soften what he chose. The gap was real. The vehicle was his.

## Why Do "He Cheated" and "He Never Loved Me" Feel Like the Same Sentence?

Because in the first week they arrive together, at the same speed, with the same authority, and nothing inside you flags one of them as unverified.

There is a distinction here that changes what happens next, and almost nobody makes it while the shock is fresh. A verified fact that your partner does not love you and a rehearsed fear that your partner does not love you produce identical internal certainty. They feel the same. They carry the same weight in the chest at three in the morning. Only one of them has actually been established, and there is no internal signal that tells you which one you are holding.

People say this out loud in a very particular sequence: *I feel my husband doesn't really love me*, followed immediately by *I tried to confirm it*. Read those two together. The second sentence is an admission that the first is still open, sitting right beside a sentence delivered with the full confidence of a settled conclusion.

There is a second complication worth naming here, because it changes what you are actually working on. For some people that sentence had been running quietly for years before any of this happened, for reasons that predate the marriage entirely. [Why real affection sometimes fails to register at all has its own page](/patterns/feeling-unloved/), and if the sentence sounds familiar from long before the discovery, it is worth reading beside this one. Otherwise the affair gets asked to explain something it did not cause.

| | "Was I loved?" | "Which need went unsupplied?" |
|---|---|---|
| What answers it | Years of ordinary behavior on days nobody was performing | What his time, attention, and energy actually went toward in the last two years |
| What the affair tells you | Very little on its own | Most of what there is to know |
| What would settle it | A pattern across the whole record, including the boring parts | A plain conversation, once he is capable of having one |
| How fast you can know | Slowly, and not while you are this frightened | Faster than you would expect, often in one honest sitting |
| What it decides | Whether the past was real | Whether the relationship can work going forward |

Look at the bottom row, because the two questions decide different things. Deciding what actually happened has to come before deciding what it means for the relationship, and both of those come before deciding what to do. Collapsing all three into one conversation in the first week, at the point of maximum pain and minimum information, is the single most common way this goes badly.

## Why Would Someone Act Against a Value They Genuinely Hold?

Because needs outrank values when the two conflict for long enough, and the driver wins.

This is the question people actually ask after a betrayal, once the shouting is over. How could someone who believes what he says he believes do what he did. The answer is unsatisfying and it is also the most useful thing on this page. Values operate on what a person endorses and would defend in an argument. Needs operate on what actually moves behavior under pressure, especially pressure that has been building for years without a name. Where the two disagree, behavior follows the need, and the person is left holding an act he cannot reconcile with who he believes himself to be.

That gap then gets patched with a story, because human beings cannot walk around holding an unresolved contradiction about their own character. The cheapest available patch is a story that makes the act consistent: I was unhappy for years. We had been over for a long time. I never really loved you.

Consider what that means about the sentence that wrecked you. Very often it was manufactured after the fact, by someone trying to make his own behavior survivable to himself, and then handed to you as though it were a finding about the last decade. You have been treating a patch as evidence. It is worth at least suspecting the sentence rather than accepting it as the verdict on everything you remember.

The reverse patch exists too, and it is just as unreliable. "It meant nothing, it was only physical, I never stopped loving you" is often equally constructed, for the same reason, by the same machinery. Neither of those sentences gives you the report you actually need, which is which driver had gone unsupplied and for how long. That one can be checked against how he spent his life, which makes it the only version worth arguing about.

## Why Does a Long Relationship Quietly Stop Supplying Some of It?

Because it settles, and settling is the thing you both wanted.

Nothing goes wrong in the way people expect. There is no fight that starts it. The shape of a life takes hold, and inside that shape certain kinds of contact stop happening: being asked a question nobody knows the answer to, being surprised, being looked at by someone who has not already seen you at your worst. Those are exactly the things a stable partnership trades away for the enormous benefit of being fully known.

There is a well-known clinical model for what happens next, developed by the psychologist Shirley Glass and set out in her 2003 book *NOT "Just Friends"*. She described healthy couples as maintaining a wall of privacy around the relationship, with a window of transparency open between the partners. In an affair, the structure inverts: a wall goes up against the partner, and a window opens to the outside person. What makes the model useful is that the inversion is observable before anything physical happens. The details of a day start going somewhere else first. A phone gets turned face down. A conversation stops being reported.

That inversion is why "nothing was wrong" and "something was unmet" turn out to be two entirely different audits. Most couples only ever run the first one. Absence of conflict, a working household, a marriage other people admire, and no visible symptoms at all can sit directly on top of one person receiving almost no novelty and almost no sense of mattering for several years running. That condition has no obvious tell. It shows up as a slow drift of attention.

## Did You Confirm It, or Are You Still Trying To?

Those two states feel identical from the inside and produce completely different information, so it is worth knowing which one you are in.

The checking behaves like diligence. It takes real nerve to ask your partner whether he still loves you, days after everything came apart, so the asking gets internally rewarded as courage. Then the answer arrives, it helps for an hour or an evening, and by the next morning the question has quietly reopened. You ask again, phrased slightly differently, hoping this time it lands as true.

A conclusion holds still. A fear needs re-supplying every few days. That is the entire test, and it is the reason repeated checking is functionally close to never having checked at all: it never concludes. Each round hands the fear new material to run on and postpones the only thing that would settle anything, which is a slow accumulation of ordinary behavior over months. [The full mechanism behind reassurance that keeps wearing off is on its own page.](/patterns/seeking-constant-reassurance/)

Two things need separating, though, before anyone stops asking questions. Practical facts are not part of the loop. Whether the affair has ended, who else knows, what the situation is with your health and your money, and whether he will answer a direct question without editing it are all things you are entitled to know, and they take as many conversations as they take. The loop is a different animal: it is the same emotional question, re-asked, hoping for a different quality of yes.

The evidence audit runs on the same wiring. Sitting up compiling a silent ledger of every kind and every cruel thing he has done, weighing them against each other, feels like careful thinking. It cannot work, because the ledger is trying to settle a verdict on his character when the actual question is which need stopped being met and what either of you intends to do now.

## What Is the Fact, and What Is the Meaning You Put On It?

The fact is what happened. The meaning is the sentence that arrived with it, and the meaning is where nearly all of the intensity lives.

This lands badly at first, so let me be precise about what is being claimed. Nobody is suggesting the pain is invented or that a smaller word would make you feel better. The claim is narrower and more useful: the event and the sentence you have attached to it are two separate objects, they arrived so close together that they feel like one, and only one of them has been checked.

| The fact, in one plain line | The sentence that arrived with it | What would have to be true for that sentence to hold |
|---|---|---|
| He was involved with someone else for four months | "None of it was ever real" | Every ordinary warm thing across all those years would have to have been performed, including on days nobody was watching |
| He said he never stopped loving me | "He is managing me" | The rest of his behavior would have to match a person managing you, over months, not one sentence delivered under pressure |
| I did not notice for four months | "I am the stupid one here" | Not noticing a concealed thing would have to be a failure of intelligence rather than the expected result of concealment working |
| He said the marriage had felt dead for years | "So I have been alone the whole time" | The version he constructed afterward would have to be more accurate than what either of you actually lived |

Run your own version of that table, on paper, in your own handwriting. Put the event in one factual line with no adjectives in it, and put your sentence directly underneath. Seeing the two on a page, physically separated, does something that arguing with yourself has never once managed.

Then look at the word you have chosen for what happened. "He betrayed me" and "he met a need somewhere else" describe the same event and produce completely different weeks. You are allowed to keep the heavier word. It may well be the accurate one, and choosing it deliberately is different from having it arrive on its own and never be examined. The point of the exercise is authorship, not minimizing.

## Does Understanding Why He Did It Mean Letting Him Off the Hook?

No. Understanding and excusing are two separate acts, and you can perform the first without conceding the second.

In the heat of it they feel like the same motion. Somebody explains that he had gone years without feeling admired, and something in you goes cold, because it sounds like the beginning of an argument for why this was reasonable. That reflex is protective and it costs you the only tool that actually changes anything, since understanding the mechanism is what eventually gives you a decision to make rather than a wound to sit inside.

Hold both statements in the same paragraph. The need was real and the coverage failure was mutual, in the ordinary sense that neither of you was tending to something. The choice of vehicle was his alone, made without you, and concealed from you. Explaining the first has no bearing on the second. He remains fully responsible for what he decided to do about a problem you were both living inside.

Anger tends to run hottest and longest for precisely as long as understanding feels like surrender. Once those two come apart, the anger usually gets more specific, which is uncomfortable in a different and much more workable way.

## Whose Fault Is the Affair, and Whose Fault Is the Gap?

The affair is his. The gap belonged to the relationship, which means it belonged to both of you and to the shape your life took.

Those two sentences have to stay separate, because collapsing them produces either an apologia or a lifetime of self-blame, and both are wrong. A relationship not supplying every one of six drivers is a structural fact about a shared arrangement. Two people build a life together and that life covers some needs beautifully and others hardly at all, usually without either of them ever having a conversation about it. Neither person is at fault for that. Very few couples have the vocabulary for it, which is most of why it goes unaddressed for a decade.

What sits entirely with him is what he did about it. He had every other option available: naming it, asking for it, going and building something of his own, complaining, sulking, going to therapy, or leaving. He picked the one that required deceiving you. That decision was made repeatedly, over months, in specific moments where he could have chosen otherwise.

So the honest answer to "did I do something to make him look elsewhere" is no, and there is still something worth knowing in the question. Something in the arrangement was not working for at least one of you, probably for both. That is worth learning about the marriage. Converting it into a verdict about your own adequacy is a different move entirely, and it is the move the question is usually trying to make when it arrives at four in the morning.

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because five quite different situations get filed under the same word, and they call for different things.

| What you might be looking at | What tells it apart | Where the question gets answered |
|---|---|---|
| One affair inside a relationship that was otherwise reasonably honest | It has a beginning and an end, he can describe it without editing, and the rest of the record holds up | This page, then the repair question |
| A long concealed second life | Years rather than months, multiple people, and a scale of routine deception the affair itself does not explain | Professional support, since what needs examining is the concealment rather than one gap |
| Deception as one item in a wider pattern of control | Money, movement, contact with your family, or your version of reality has also been managed | A domestic abuse service, first, before anything on this page |
| An emotional affair with nothing physical | No contact, and a window that opened outward while a wall went up at home | This page. The mechanism is identical and so is the injury |
| An affair that is still running | Nothing has actually stopped, and each conversation produces a new promise | Deciding whether it has ended, before any other question is worth asking |

Two further sorts of reader end up here by accident, and both are better served elsewhere. If you have already accepted what happened and want to know what rebuilding actually requires from each person, [that belongs on the repair page](/patterns/how-to-rebuild-trust/). If the effect has been that you now brace around everybody, including people who have never given you a reason, [the generalized version has its own page](/patterns/trust-issues/) and so does [the aftermath itself](/patterns/when-someone-close-betrays-you/).

## What Actually Ends When a Marriage Ends?

The arrangement ends. Whether the love ends is a separate question with a separate answer, and the two get treated as one thing under family and social pressure.

A marriage is a certificate, a shared surname, a set of legal and financial arrangements, a public status, and a story other people have been told about you. The relationship is the years, the private jokes, the person who knew what you were like at twenty-six, and whatever connection actually still runs between you. Those are different objects. One can be dissolved by paperwork. The other cannot be dissolved by anything, and often carries on being felt for a long time after the arrangement is gone, which is consistent rather than a sign that you have failed to move on.

This distinction does real work for people holding a decision they have already made privately and have not told anyone. Not telling your parents, or your closest friend, is rarely about not knowing what to say. Saying it out loud makes it witnessed, and witnessed makes it permanent in a way it does not currently feel.

Underneath that there are two separate pains being carried as one undifferentiated weight. There is grief, which is about losing the relationship and the future you had assumed. And there is shame, which is about being seen to have failed at something everybody watched you begin. Shame is what makes the telling feel dangerous. Grief is what actually hurts. While the two stay fused, the fear of disclosure postpones the grieving indefinitely, and the grieving is the part that eventually moves.

Pull them apart on paper. What is embarrassing to disclose goes in one column, what actually hurts goes in the other. Most people find the columns are much shorter and much less overlapping than the fused version felt.

## Why Does This Hurt in a Place Nothing Else Reaches?

Because it lands on the bond itself, rather than on any particular thing that was done.

The clinical literature has a precise term for this. The psychologist Sue Johnson, who developed Emotionally Focused Therapy, calls it an attachment injury: an event that damages a couple's sense that the other person is reachable, responsive, and safe to depend on. In that model the injury requires its own structured repair, distinct from the question of whether the underlying bond has ended, which is exactly the double truth this page is built around. A bond can be genuinely injured and genuinely still loving at the same time.

That framing explains something people find frightening about their own reaction: the disproportion. A person who has handled a bereavement, a job loss, or a serious illness with real composure can be undone by this in a way that seems to make no sense. It makes sense once you notice what got hit. The person you would normally go to for comfort about a catastrophe is the source of this one, so the ordinary route to relief is closed at the exact moment you need it most.

It also explains the physical symptoms, which nobody warns you about. The interrupted sleep, the inability to eat, the involuntary replay several times a day, the sudden drops when something ordinary reminds you. Those are what an alarm system does when the thing it was built to protect is the thing that raised it.

## What Actually Moves This?

Two separate pieces of work, on two different timelines, and mixing them up is the most common way couples end up back here in two years.

The first is fast. Separating the fact from the story built on top of it can shift inside a single honest sitting, because it requires nothing at all from your partner. It is an audit of your own conclusion, and you can run it alone, today, whether he is cooperating or not.

The second is slow. If the relationship continues, something has to actually change so that whatever went unsupplied has a real vehicle inside the relationship, deliberately built rather than arrived at by accident or crisis. That is measured in months and it does not complete just because the two of you have stopped fighting about it. Expect the first shift to feel disproportionately fast next to the second, and be careful not to read it as the job being done. Settling the meaning while leaving the structure untouched is precisely how a couple gets a good year and then finds themselves back in the same room.

Nothing below is a validated protocol, and anyone offering you one for this is overselling something.

1. **Write the fact in one line, with no adjectives in it.** Just what happened, the way a stranger would report it. Adjectives are meaning wearing a disguise, and they smuggle the conclusion into the evidence.
2. **Write the sentence you have been treating as its meaning, in its exact wording.** Not the tidied version. The one that actually runs at three in the morning.
3. **Ask what would have to be true for that sentence to hold**, and what would count as evidence either way. Most of these sentences have never been asked to produce any.
4. **Ask your practical questions once, properly, and write down the answers.** Is it over, who knows, what do I need to know about my health and our money. Those are facts you are owed, and having them written down stops them being re-litigated every night.
5. **Then let the emotional question rest for a week.** Ask once whether he still loves you, hear the answer, and deliberately do not re-ask it for seven days. This will be extremely uncomfortable, and the discomfort is the mechanism rather than a side effect.
6. **Name the need, in plain words, on both sides.** What had stopped being supplied for him. What had stopped being supplied for you. Two sentences. Most couples have never once said these out loud.
7. **Separate the shame from the grief**, in two columns, and notice which one has been running the decision about who to tell.
8. **Decide nothing permanent for two weeks**, unless your safety requires otherwise. Week one is maximum pain and minimum information, which is the worst possible ratio for a decision this size.
9. **Say one true thing out loud to one person you trust.** Not the whole story, and not to everyone. Concealment is what keeps this at maximum weight, and a single witness reduces it more than a week of thinking will.

Is any of that comfortable? No. It is small, repetitive, freaking unglamorous work, and it is what people who came out of this well actually describe doing, usually with no idea at the time whether it was helping.

## How Long Does This Honestly Take?

The meaning can move in weeks. The structure takes months. The involuntary part takes as long as it takes, and it does not move in a straight line.

Separating the fact from the story is the fastest of the three, and people are often surprised by it. A conclusion held for six weeks can come apart in one afternoon of writing it down and looking at what it was actually resting on. That does not end the pain. It ends the second layer of pain, the part that was about what the event proved about you, which is frequently the heavier of the two.

The replay, the sleep, and the physical drops ease unevenly. Expect better weeks followed by a bad one for no reason you can identify, anniversaries that land harder than the days around them, and specific places or songs that stay difficult for a year. None of that is a sign of failure or of having made the wrong decision.

If the relationship continues, the rebuilding runs on its own clock, and it is longer than almost anyone hopes: many months at minimum, often considerably more, with two tracks that both have to hold. [The honest version of that timeline is set out in full on the repair page.](/patterns/how-to-rebuild-trust/) The thing worth watching for is the false ending, which arrives at around month four when the fighting stops and both people quietly decide it is finished. Stopping the fighting is a change in symptoms. Whether the need that went unsupplied now has somewhere real to go is a different question, and it is the one that decides whether you are here again at month twenty.

## What Does the Other End of This Look Like?

Both sentences standing at once, without either one needing to cancel the other out.

Every pattern like this has two ends, and describing only the wounded one leaves you with an accurate account of the problem and nowhere to walk. So here is the resolved version, concretely, whether the relationship continued or not.

| | The first weeks | The settled version |
|---|---|---|
| The two sentences | Cancel each other out, several times a day | Both stand. He loved you and he did this, and neither fact deletes the other |
| The audit | Running constantly, ledger updated hourly | Closed, because it was answering a question that was never about character |
| The word for what happened | Arrived on its own and was never examined | Chosen deliberately, and it might well be the same word |
| The checking | Same question, re-asked weekly, phrased differently | Asked once. The answer was allowed to stand |
| What the decision rests on | A verdict about whether the past was real | Whether this relationship can supply what you need going forward |
| Who knows | Nobody, and the silence is doing damage of its own | At least one person you chose to tell |
| The replay | Involuntary, several times a day | Occasional, and it passes without taking the afternoon |

What people describe at the far end of this is rarely relief and rarely closure. What keeps coming up instead is a strange sense of having been returned to themselves. One description of it, from someone whose marriage did not survive: *"For me, it's like a rebirth."* That sentence arrives months later or not at all, it cannot be rushed, and it should never be held up as something you ought to be feeling yet. Whether the relationship ends or continues, the thing that changes is that the decision stops being a referendum on your worth and becomes an ordinary, difficult, adult choice about the next several years.

Do not judge yourself if the middle stretch of this feels worse than the first week did. Shock is anesthetic. What comes after it is the actual feeling arriving, and that is usually the work happening rather than the work failing.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From four bodies of public work, none of them mine, all of them checkable without going through me.

Esther Perel, a psychotherapist writing for a general audience, made the central claim of this page publicly in *The State of Affairs: Rethinking Infidelity* in 2017, arguing that an affair is often bound up with the unfaithful partner's search for a lost sense of self or aliveness rather than functioning as a verdict on the relationship. Shirley Glass, a clinical psychologist, gave the transparency inversion its most durable vocabulary in *NOT "Just Friends"* in 2003, describing the wall that goes up between partners while a window opens to an outsider. Sue Johnson, the clinical psychologist who developed Emotionally Focused Therapy, named the attachment injury in a 2001 paper with Judy Makinen and John Millikin, describing a betrayal that damages a couple's sense of safety and treating its repair as a structured process separate from whether the bond has ended. The general principle that behavior organizes around which needs are being met has its most serious academic home in Self-Determination Theory, developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan at the University of Rochester, which identifies autonomy, competence, and relatedness as basic psychological needs whose satisfaction or frustration predicts wellbeing and behavior.

One honest note on vocabulary. The six drivers listed earlier are common currency in the personal development world and carry no peer-reviewed standing of their own. They are a useful map rather than a finding, and Deci and Ryan's work is the version with actual research behind it.

The rest of this is mine, and it carries no research weight at all. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, I am not a therapist, and none of the four people named above has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.
