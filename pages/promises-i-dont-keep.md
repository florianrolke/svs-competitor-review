# Why do I make promises I mean and then not keep them?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because the promise was made by a version of you in one state and comes due to a version of you in a completely different one. Neither of them is lying. The sincerity you felt was accurate about what you wanted and nearly worthless as a forecast. The only part of this you actually choose is what you do in the hour after you know you will miss it.

- "I say yes to things nine months out like they belong to somebody else."
- "I knew on Tuesday I was not going to make it and I said nothing until Friday."
- "They said no worries so fast that I could hear them already planning around me."
- "I do not understand how I can mean something that much and still not do it."

You are on the phone on a Tuesday. Somebody you care about is describing a bad month, a father in hospital, a job that has gone wrong, and near the end of it you say you will call them this week. You mean it. You can feel yourself meaning it, which is a specific physical sensation and not a figure of speech. There is a small warmth in it, and a small relief, and a sense of the thing already being half done.

Then the week happens. Wednesday has a deadline in it. Thursday you think about the call twice, once in the car and once at about eleven at night when calling would be strange. Friday you are tired. Saturday belongs to other people. On Sunday evening you are loading the dishwasher and it arrives, whole and cold, and you stand there holding a plate.

The second version is longer range. In March somebody asks whether you are coming to the thing in November, a week away somewhere, and you look at November and November is empty. You say yes. You mean that too. Then it is late October and the week has a flight in it, and a dog, and a handover, and the yes you gave in March reads like an arrangement made on your behalf by a stranger with a slightly better life.

Two explanations get offered for this and neither one fits. The first is that you were exaggerating, that the promise was social noise, that you did not really mean it. You did. The second is that you forgot. You did not forget. The call was in your head on Thursday at eleven at night, which is the opposite of forgetting.

What actually happened is stranger and much more ordinary, and it has been measured, and almost nobody has ever explained it to you without also implying something about your character. That is what this page is for.

## What Does It Feel Like to Mean a Promise Completely?

It feels like certainty, and the certainty is the part that later becomes impossible to explain to anybody.

At the moment you promise, several things are true at once. The other person is in front of you, or in your ear, and their situation is fully loaded in your mind. You can see what the call would do for them. You want to be the sort of person who makes it. The cost of making it is somewhere abstract and the benefit is right here in the room, arriving immediately, in the form of their voice getting easier.

Notice what is present and what is absent. Present: warmth, their face, your affection for them, a clear picture of the good version of yourself. Absent: Thursday. Not absent in the sense of forgotten, absent in the sense that Thursday has no sensory content. You cannot feel a Thursday you have not had yet. You can only feel now.

That asymmetry is the whole engine of this page, and it is not a flaw in you specifically. Everybody forecasts from the state they are standing in.

Here are the sentences people actually run in that moment, in the wording they use when nobody is listening.

- "I will definitely call this week."
- "Yes, count me in, I would not miss it."
- "Send it over, I will get back to you tonight."
- "Of course I will come, it is ages away."
- "I promise, this time I will."

And here are the ones that arrive later, usually alone.

- "How is it Sunday already."
- "It has been three weeks."
- "I will do it tomorrow and then it is done."
- "They have stopped asking."

The one to notice is whichever one produced a small physical drop as you read it, with no translation needed.

## Why Does the Week Run Out Before the Call Gets Made?

Because a promise addressed to a whole week has nowhere inside that week to happen.

Work through the mechanics rather than the morality. On Monday the call is available, so Monday is not the day. On Tuesday it is still available, so Tuesday is not the day either. Availability is what keeps a task permanently postponable, because nothing about any particular hour makes it that hour's job. The week does not run out. It fills, with things that did have hours attached to them, and the call loses every single one of those contests without ever being consciously declined.

This explains something that otherwise looks like inconsistency. The same person who cannot make a call promised for this week will reliably turn up for a call scheduled for Tuesday at seven. Nothing about their affection changed between those two cases. One promise had a location and the other did not.

There is a second mechanic underneath it. A promise made about a category rather than an event never produces the small flicker of dread that gets things done. Dread is a scheduling signal. Remove the schedule and you remove the signal, and what is left is a warm intention that keeps not being contradicted by anything until Sunday, when it is contradicted all at once.

If you have privately concluded that the missing calls prove something about how much you care, this is the first place to check, because the evidence for that conclusion is much weaker than it feels.

## What Do Researchers Call the Distance Between Meaning It and Doing It?

There are two names, they cover two different halves of this, and both of them are precise about the what while saying nothing at all about the why.

The first is the **hot-cold empathy gap**, sometimes just the empathy gap. It describes a documented failure to predict what a different internal state will feel like, and it runs in both directions. A person who is calm cannot accurately imagine being furious. A person who has just eaten cannot accurately imagine being hungry, and a person who is hungry cannot accurately imagine being full. The term entered the research literature in 1996, in a paper about visceral influences on behavior, and the studies that followed it are unusually easy to picture.

Read and van Leeuwen ran one of the cleanest in 1998. People chose a snack to be delivered a week later, and their choice depended heavily on whether they happened to be hungry at the moment of choosing. The delivery was a week out for everybody. The only thing that varied was the state of the person making the decision. Sayette and colleagues found the same shape in 2008 with smokers, where people who were not currently craving priced a future cigarette far below what people who were currently craving priced the identical future cigarette. Same person, same future, different price, and the only variable was which state they answered from.

Now apply that to a promise. The moment of promising is a hot state. Somebody's face is in front of you, the affection is live, the picture of yourself keeping the promise is vivid. The Thursday morning the promise comes due is a cold state, with a full inbox and a headache and none of the emotional material that produced the yes. The person who made the promise and the person who has to keep it are, as far as prediction is concerned, badly out of contact with each other.

The second name is the **intention-behavior gap**, which measures the outcome rather than the mechanism. Armitage and Conner pooled 185 studies in 2001 and reported that intention together with perceived control accounted for roughly a quarter of the variation in what people actually did. Sniehotta, Scholz and Schwarzer reported in 2005 that what closed most of the remaining distance was planning and in-the-moment control rather than any increase in how much people wanted the outcome. A sincere intention, on its own, is a poor predictor. This is a finding about human beings in general, and it is oddly steadying to have a number for it.

| What these two names genuinely give you | Where each one stops |
|---|---|
| A precise account of why the sincerity was real and the forecast was still wrong | It describes the failure and says nothing about what your particular yes was doing in that room |
| Evidence that state prediction is poor for everybody, in both directions | It shows the effect exists and cannot tell you how large yours is on a Tuesday |
| A measured size for the distance between intending and doing | The measurement comes almost entirely from health behavior, not from promises made to people you love |
| A reason to stop treating an unkept promise as a moral result | It offers no way to tell an honest forecasting error from a yes that was buying something |
| A finding that planning and control close the gap better than motivation does | It says nothing about why some promises get planned and others never do |
| Language you can use out loud with somebody who is hurt | Naming a mechanism to somebody who has been let down is not repair, and can be heard as an excuse |

Both names describe a what. Neither reaches the why, and the why is the part that decides what would actually help. Two people can miss the identical phone call on the identical Friday while the thing each of them was doing at the moment of promising has nothing in common with the other. One was genuinely mispredicting a Thursday. One was ending a conversation that had become unbearable. No published finding separates those two people, and until they are separated, any advice given to both of them will fit one and be useless to the other.

One boundary before going further. If what you recognize is the size of the promise going up while you are still speaking, the Friday named when you knew it was the Wednesday after, that belongs on [promising too much](/patterns/promising-too-much/) rather than here. This page is about the ordinary-sized promise you meant exactly as you said it.

## Which Version of This Needs More Than a Page Like This?

Five situations sit outside what any page can usefully address, and it is worth checking them first.

If the promises are to a child, and the pattern has run for years, and the child has visibly stopped expecting anything, that is worth taking to somebody qualified rather than working on privately. The stakes are different and the repair window has a shape you cannot see from inside it.

If alcohol or another substance is part of how the promises get made, or part of why they get missed, then the promise is the visible end of something else and adjusting your language will not touch it.

If you are regularly told you promised things you have no memory of promising, and this happens across different people rather than with one person, that is a different question from the one on this page and deserves a proper assessment rather than a self-directed fix.

If there is any thought of harming yourself, that belongs with a licensed professional now rather than later.

And one more, different in kind. If somebody in your life extracts promises from you and then punishes the miss, or keeps a ledger of them, or uses them to establish what you owe, then your yes was not freely given and none of what follows is the right frame for it. That is a problem about that relationship wearing the clothes of a problem about your reliability.

## Were You Lying When You Said It?

No, and getting the distinction right has a practical consequence rather than a moral one.

A lie requires that at the moment of speaking you knew the statement to be false. If you meant it, that condition is not met, and this holds even though the outcome looked identical from the outside. What you produced was an accurate report of your preferences delivered in the grammar of a prediction. Those two things use the same words and do completely different jobs, and English gives you no way to mark which one you are doing.

Most people who read a page like this have already privately settled on the harsher reading, usually years ago, because it is the only explanation that has ever been offered to them. The trouble with settling there is that it produces its own behavior. If you have concluded you are unreliable, you start avoiding the conversations where promises get made, or you over-commit to prove the conclusion wrong, and both of those generate more missed promises rather than fewer.

The distinction also matters to the other person, considerably more than most people realize. Schweitzer, Hershey and Bradlow reported in 2006 that trust damaged by an unreliable act recovered when the person apologized and then behaved consistently, while trust damaged by deception did not fully recover even with the same apology and the same consistency. Deception and unreliability are repaired at different rates, and only one of them is repairable at all in the ordinary way. If the person waiting on you has quietly filed you under the wrong one, that is worth knowing, because it is the kind of thing that gets corrected by evidence rather than by protest.

## Why Is the Person Who Promised Unreachable by Thursday?

Because your own decision-making treats a future version of you with something closer to the distance it uses for other people than the closeness it uses for now.

Pronin, Olivola and Kennedy reported a set of studies in 2008 in which participants were asked to allocate an unpleasant-tasting liquid for a taste experiment. People assigned their future selves substantially larger quantities than they assigned their present selves, at levels much closer to what they assigned to strangers. The same shape showed up with time: people volunteered more of their future hours than of their current ones. When you agree to something for November, part of what is happening is that you are spending a resource belonging to someone you are only loosely identified with.

Bartels and Urminsky pushed at the same joint from the other side in 2011. When people were led to see their own identity as likely to change substantially, they became noticeably more impatient in choices between smaller-sooner and larger-later rewards. When continuity was made salient instead, patience increased. How connected you feel to the person who has to do the thing turns out to be a live variable rather than a fixed fact about you.

Hershfield and colleagues added a neural version of this: thinking about one's distant future self produced activity patterns resembling those produced by thinking about another person, and the degree of that resemblance tracked real-world saving behavior. That description is reasonably well supported. The applied versions built on top of it, showing people age-progressed images of themselves to change their financial behavior, have a thinner and more mixed record in larger tests, and it is worth saying that rather than presenting the whole line of work as settled.

What all of this gives you is a correction rather than a technique. The sentence you have probably been saying to yourself, that a person who really meant it would have done it, does not survive the evidence. The evidence suggests something less flattering to everybody and much less specific to you. Meaning it happens here. Doing it happens over there, to somebody your own brain treats as partly a stranger.

## Why Does a Yes Nine Months Out Cost Nothing?

Because you are reading an empty calendar as a statement about your availability, when it is only a statement about how far ahead people have booked you so far.

The model is sometimes called time slack. It is a described model rather than one person's finding, and the plain version of it is that people forecast their future free time from the current visible state of the schedule instead of from the rate at which schedules fill. November looks open in March because nothing has landed in it yet. Nothing has landed in it yet because it is March.

You can audit this without any research at all. Pick any month in the past two years and look at what was actually in it. Then ask what that month looked like nine months before it happened. Every single one was empty at that distance and full when it arrived. The forecast has failed every time and has never once been revised, because nobody goes back and checks a prediction about November after November is over.

Distance does something else too, which is that it strips detail. A week away in November, viewed from March, is a word and a feeling. Viewed from late October it is a flight at an awkward hour, three days of handover, somebody to take the dog, and a conversation about money. None of those existed at the moment you said yes. They were not hidden from you. They had not been generated yet, because your mind does not build the logistics of something that far out, and it will not do so no matter how carefully you try.

This is why the nine-month yes and the this-week yes fail in slightly different ways and want slightly different handling. The first one fails because the future was underspecified. The second fails because the week had no slot in it.

## Why Do Two People Break the Identical Promise for Completely Different Reasons?

Because the promise is the visible end of something, and the something varies enormously between people who look identical from outside.

This matters practically rather than philosophically. Devise a fix based on the surface and you get a fix that works for whichever person happens to share your surface, which is not necessarily you. A rule about naming times will transform the week of somebody whose problem was genuinely a forecast, and will do nothing whatsoever for somebody whose yes was ending an unbearable moment, because that person will simply name a time in exactly the same unbearable moment for exactly the same reason.

Five roots follow. Most people find one lands considerably harder than the others, and a fair number find two running together in different parts of their life. Read them for the physical jolt rather than for the argument, and pay particular attention to the second one, which is the most common and the least shameful and the one people are least willing to accept about themselves.

## What If the Yes Was the Fastest Way Out of the Moment?

Sometimes the promise works as an exit from a conversation that had become difficult to stay in, with no plan inside it at all.

The shape is recognizable once described. Somebody is upset, or waiting, or looking at you with an expectation you can feel physically. The silence has gone on slightly too long. And the yes arrives, fast, before any part of you has consulted a calendar, and the moment immediately gets easier. That easing is the payoff, it arrives within about a second, and it is completely reliable.

Anything that ends a bad moment gets repeated. It does not need to work out later in order to get repeated. It only needs to work now, which it does, every time, without fail. That is the least controversial finding in the whole of behavior science and it is doing more work in this pattern than anything sophisticated.

Two tells separate this root from the others. The first is speed: the promise leaves your mouth ahead of any thought about whether it is possible. The second is what you feel immediately afterward, which is relief rather than warmth. A promise that was genuinely a plan tends to leave a small pleasant anticipation behind it. A promise that was an exit tends to leave a faint flatness, sometimes with a hint of dread underneath, and it usually gets changed subject within about ten seconds.

The promises produced this way also tend to be vague, because specificity would have required the very pause the yes existed to avoid. Soon. This week. Definitely. If your unkept promises are disproportionately the vague ones, that is worth noting.

The moment itself, and what the word is buying inside it, is covered properly on [saying yes when you mean no](/patterns/saying-yes-when-you-mean-no/). What belongs here is the consequence: a promise generated as an exit was never a forecast, so no improvement in your forecasting will touch it.

## What If You Just Misread Who You Would Be by Then?

This is the ordinary root, it is the most common one by a wide margin, and it deserves considerably more respect than it usually gets.

Here nothing is being bought and nothing is being avoided. You wanted to make the call. You looked at your week and formed an estimate. The estimate was made by a person who felt like calling, and by the time Thursday arrived the person who felt like calling was not available, and the person who was available had a different set of priorities that were also completely legitimate. That is a prediction error about internal states, of the kind everybody makes, in a domain where nobody has ever been trained to expect it.

The evidence supports treating this generously. State prediction is poor across the board and in both directions. Nobody looks at a hungry version of themselves from a full one and gets it right. What makes promises particularly exposed to it is that the two states are separated by days rather than minutes, and that the promise is spoken in the hotter of the two.

Two tells identify this root. The first is distribution: your misses are spread fairly evenly across people you like, people you owe, and people you barely know. There is no pattern by relationship, because the mechanism is not relational. The second is specificity: your promises tend to be detailed, with dates and plans attached, because you were genuinely planning rather than escaping. People whose yes is an exit do not produce detail.

If this is your root, the most expensive thing in the whole pattern is probably not the missed calls. It is the moral conclusion you drew from them. A forecasting error repeated for twenty years, interpreted as evidence about your character, produces a quiet and permanent low-grade self-assessment that costs far more than the handful of things you actually did not do.

## What If No Was Never Actually Available to You?

Some promises are not given. They are extracted, and the difference changes what the promise even was.

There are rooms in which a refusal is not a real option. A manager who does not receive no. A parent whose disappointment reorganizes an entire family for a fortnight. A friend in a bad enough state that any answer other than yes feels like abandonment. In those rooms the yes is a report on the power arrangement rather than a statement of intent, and treating it as a broken promise afterward misidentifies what happened.

The tell here is immediate and unmistakable. Ask yourself what would have happened if you had said no, and see how quickly a detailed answer arrives. People with a forecasting problem have to think about it and usually shrug. People with this root have the answer instantly, in full, including the tone of voice and the two weeks afterward.

The second tell is clustering. Misses concentrated around specific people, while your promises to everybody else hold, points here rather than at your calendar.

Say the consequence plainly: a promise that could not have been refused does not become keepable by trying harder, and every strategy aimed at improving your reliability will fail against it while quietly reinforcing the idea that the failure is yours. The work sits with that relationship. Where the yes was pulled out of you, the material on [agreeing when you disagree](/patterns/agreeing-when-you-disagree/) is closer to the actual problem than anything on this page.

## What If Every Yes Was Reasonable and Only the Sum Was Impossible?

This is the root that hides best, because it cannot be found by examining any individual promise.

Herrnstein and Prelec described the mechanism in 1991 under the name melioration: choices get allocated toward whichever option currently returns the most, one decision at a time, and the aggregate that results can be worse than an allocation nobody ever explicitly chose. Nothing about any single step is irrational. The whole is bad and no part of it is.

Applied to a calendar, it looks like this. Somebody asks for a coffee. Reasonable, and you say yes. Somebody asks whether you can look at their document. Half an hour, reasonable, yes. A weekend in June, a call with an old colleague, a favor for a neighbor. Every one of these, taken alone, is a thing a decent person with a normal week can absorb. There is no point at which the wrong decision is made. And then the week arrives with eleven of them in it and four of them do not happen.

The tell is diagnostic and quick. You can defend every commitment on your list individually, in detail, and you cannot explain the week. If a friend went through your calendar item by item asking which one you should have refused, you would have a good answer for each and no answer for the total.

Which means reviewing your promises one at a time will never find anything, and that is exactly what people with this root do, repeatedly, concluding each time that they simply need to be a bit more disciplined. The only place the problem is visible is the sum, written out on one page, in one sitting, and most people have never once done that.

The taking-on side of this, the hand that goes up before the week has been checked, is [volunteering for everything](/patterns/volunteering-for-everything/). What belongs here is the consequence for promises specifically: when the sum is impossible, something has to give, and what gives is always whatever had no time attached to it, which is almost always the promise made to somebody who loves you and will not chase.

## What If the Promise Was Aimed at Their Face Rather Than at Your Calendar?

This is the version that damages trust fastest, and it needs naming without contempt, because the contempt is what stops people recognizing it in themselves.

Here the promise is a device for changing an expression. Somebody's face falls, or tightens, or does the thing it does, and a sentence comes out of you that restores it. The content of the sentence is secondary. What mattered was that the face changed back. This is a real skill, it works extremely well, and it is usually learned somewhere young where somebody's disappointment was genuinely expensive to be around.

The tell is a memory gap of a very particular kind. You can remember clearly that they were upset and then were not. You cannot remember with any precision what you actually committed to. That combination is close to definitive, because a promise made as a plan leaves the plan behind in your memory and a promise made as a repair leaves only the repair.

The reason this erodes trust faster than the other four is that the content was never load-bearing, so no follow-through was ever scheduled, so the miss rate is close to total. The other person may not be able to describe what they are noticing, but they notice. People are good at detecting the difference between a promise made about a Thursday and a promise made about their expression, and the detection usually shows up as a slight flatness in how they respond rather than as anything they say.

What is worth knowing here is that the thing the other person actually needed in that moment was to be taken seriously, and that can be delivered without a promise attached. Saying that you can hear how bad it is, and that you do not know yet what you can do about it, changes a face too. It changes it less immediately, which is exactly why it is harder to do and why it lasts better.

## Which Root Is Yours, Judging Only From This Month?

Look at what you already have rather than at how you feel about yourself, which is the least reliable instrument available.

| What you notice about your own last month | The root it points at |
|---|---|
| The promise leaves your mouth before you have thought, and relief follows within seconds | The yes as an exit from the moment |
| Misses spread evenly across everybody, promises usually detailed and dated | A genuine misread of your future state |
| You can describe instantly and in detail what refusing would have cost you | No was never actually available |
| Every commitment is defensible alone and the week is indefensible | The sum, one reasonable yes at a time |
| You remember them being upset and then not, and not what you agreed to | The promise aimed at an expression |
| Vague promises fail and specific ones hold | The exit, or the missing slot in the week |
| Far-future promises fail and near ones hold | The empty calendar read as availability |
| You promise more after a period of missing things | The moral conclusion feeding the pattern |

Most people find two of these rows land. Where two land, they usually belong to different parts of life, work in one and family in the other, and they want different responses.

## What Happens in the Hour After You Know You Will Miss It?

This is the part of the whole pattern that you actually choose, and almost nobody treats it as a decision.

Separate the two events carefully, because they get collapsed together constantly and they have nothing in common. The first event is the promise, made in a state you did not select, by somebody who genuinely meant it, using a forecasting faculty that is unreliable in everyone. The second event is the moment you realize it will not happen. That usually arrives on a Tuesday, with plenty of time left, and it is fully conscious. You know. You have information the other person does not have. And what you do in the next hour is a choice made by somebody in a cold, ordinary state with full access to the facts.

Most people do nothing. Not out of malice, and not really out of cowardice either. Doing nothing preserves the possibility that you might still manage it, and that possibility is genuinely comfortable to hold for about forty-eight hours. Meanwhile the other person keeps arranging things around a call that is no longer coming, and the window in which telling them would have been a favor closes quietly.

Everything the other person will later describe as your reliability is decided in that hour rather than at the moment of promising. That is worth sitting with, because it relocates the whole problem from a place you have little control over to a place where you have almost complete control.

It is also harder than it sounds, and pretending otherwise helps nobody. Missing something is passive. Telling somebody is an act, with a specific unpleasant sentence in it, delivered while they still have every reason to be disappointed. Silence requires nothing at all. That asymmetry is why this hour goes the way it does for nearly everybody, and why it is worth deliberately arranging rather than left to willingness.

## Why Does Going Quiet Cost More Than the Miss Itself?

Because silence is not neutral, and the other person will fill it with something less flattering than what actually happened.

Ferrin and colleagues compared three responses to a trust violation in 2007: apology, denial, and saying nothing. Reticence performed poorly against both of the others. It left the other person's suspicion exactly where it had been, delivering neither the reassurance of an apology nor the counter-claim of a denial. Silence gets read as content, and the content it usually gets read as is that you either did not notice or did not mind.

There is a practical asymmetry underneath this that is easy to overlook. What the other person lost went beyond the call itself. They lost the ability to plan around you. Told on Tuesday, they still have Thursday and can do something else with it. Told on Sunday, or not told at all, they have already spent it waiting. The early version costs you one uncomfortable sentence and returns their week to them. The late version costs you a longer apology and returns nothing.

And the person is rarely evaluating your capacity. They are evaluating whether you know what happened. Somebody who says on Tuesday that they will not manage the call this week is legible. Somebody who reappears the following Wednesday with a full account of how busy things were is asking to be assessed on their week, which is not the question anybody was asking.

## Why Do People Nod and Then Quietly Make a Backup Plan?

Because they have stopped treating your promises as information and started treating them as a mood, and that shift happened without anybody deciding to make it.

Describe it from their side for a moment. They like you. They are not angry. Over some number of years they have collected data, and the data says that a certain proportion of what you say you will do does not arrive. So they began, at first unconsciously, applying a discount. They stopped putting your commitment into their plans as a fixed item and started putting it in as a possibility. They made an alternative arrangement and did not mention it, because mentioning it would have been an accusation and they did not want to have that conversation.

The signs are small and consistent. The no worries that arrives slightly too fast. Not being asked a second time about something they clearly still want. A conversation where they mention something you would obviously have been invited to, and were not, and neither of you remarks on it. Being told about plans rather than consulted about them.

Nobody in this situation thinks of themselves as having lost faith in you. They think of themselves as being realistic, which is exactly what makes it durable. Calibration formed from evidence does not respond to protest and does not respond to a particularly good apology. It responds to new evidence, at the rate that new evidence can be produced, which is one promise at a time.

That is the honest and slightly bleak part. It is also the reason the distinction from earlier matters so much. Schweitzer, Hershey and Bradlow found that trust damaged by unreliability did recover with apology and consistent behavior, while trust damaged by deception did not fully recover. If you are being read as unreliable, the discount is reversible. If you are being read as somebody who says things they do not mean, it is a different and much slower repair, and it is worth finding out which one you are living in rather than assuming.

## Where Does This Hide When You Would Not Call It a Promise?

In most of what functions as a commitment without ever carrying the word, which is where the majority of the volume sits.

I will look at it tonight. Send it over and I will get back to you. Let me check and I will let you know. We should do this more often. Yes, definitely, let us sort something out. Nodding while somebody describes a plan that involves you. Not objecting when a date is proposed, which everybody present correctly reads as agreement. Saying you will think about it when what you have actually done is close a subject.

Two of these deserve separate mention. The first is the reply, which is the single most commonly broken commitment there is and the one least likely to be described as a promise. Getting back to somebody is a promise. It is generally the smallest one available and the one that erodes most steadily, because there is no moment at which it visibly fails.

The second is promises to yourself, which for many people are the ones that have been broken longest and hurt most. The mechanism is identical. The version of you who decides at eleven on Sunday night to get up at six has no access to the version of you standing in the dark at six. The difference is that nobody else is there to notice, so the evidence accumulates entirely in private and gets read entirely as character.

## Is a Promise Kept Three Weeks Late Still Broken?

It depends entirely on what the promise was for, and no research decides this one, so it is worth reasoning about directly.

Where the content was the point, late delivery is mostly repair. A document, a lift to the airport that got rearranged, a piece of information somebody needed: these have a use that survives delay, and turning up with them three weeks late costs you something in reputation and returns most of the value. Apologize once, deliver, move on.

Where the timing was the point, late is a different object entirely. A call promised during the week somebody's father was in hospital had a job, and the job was that week. Making it a month later does not deliver the thing late. It delivers proof that you remembered, which can land worse than silence, because it establishes that the omission was not forgetting.

Most people apply the first logic to promises of the second kind, which is why the very late call so often goes worse than expected. If what you are about to deliver has expired, the honest move is to say what you noticed rather than to perform the original promise after the fact. That the week happened and you were not in it, and that you have thought about it since, is a truer sentence than a phone call arriving as if nothing has passed.

## Why Has Deciding to Be More Careful Never Held?

Because the decision gets made in one state and the promise gets made in another, and the first has no way to reach the second.

Picture the resolution honestly. It arrives on a Sunday evening, alone, calm, with the whole miserable week visible behind you. In that state the correct behavior is entirely obvious and the resolve is completely genuine. Then on Wednesday somebody is upset in front of you and the resolution is nowhere in the room, because it was formed by a person who is not currently present and who had no idea what this specific moment would feel like.

There is a second problem, and it is close to a joke. Resolving to be more careful with promises is itself a promise. It is made by the same machinery, in the same conditions, with the same warmth and the same certainty and the same lack of a slot in any particular week. Whatever explanation you accept for the first kind applies unchanged to the second.

This is why the repairs that hold are structural and slightly boring, and why the ones that fail are motivational. Nothing on this page suggests wanting it more. What is worth changing is the shape of the sentence, so that a promise made in a hot state carries something that survives into a cold one: a time, a smaller unit, or a visible checkpoint before the deadline. That is a change to the instrument rather than to the person holding it.

It is also worth saying plainly that it is not as simple as somebody telling you to just think before you speak. There is more underneath most of these than the visible half, which is why the five roots come before any of the practical material rather than after it.

## How Good Is the Evidence Behind Any of This?

Mixed, and unevenly distributed across the claims, so it is worth being specific about which parts are load-bearing.

The intention-behavior work is the strongest of it. There are large meta-analyses, the effect is stable, and the general finding that sincere intention explains a minority of the variation in behavior has held up across decades. The limitation is domain: nearly all of it studies health behaviors, exercise and screening and diet, chosen because those are easy to measure. Whether a number derived from gym attendance transfers cleanly to a phone call promised to a friend is an assumption rather than a finding, and it should be held loosely.

The state-prediction studies are more vivid and thinner. They are mostly small, laboratory-based, and conducted in narrow domains such as hunger, craving and pain. The direction of the effect is consistent and the mechanism is plausible. The size of it in ordinary life has not been established at anything like the scale the popular versions imply, and some of the best-known consumer-behavior demonstrations of hot-state decision making have had serious data problems that had nothing to do with the underlying idea.

The future-self findings sit in between. The descriptive work replicates reasonably well. The interventions built on top of it have a much weaker record, and anybody presenting the age-progressed-image studies as a settled way to change behavior is running ahead of the evidence.

The planning fallacy, which is the vocabulary most often thrown at this pattern, is treated properly on [always running late](/patterns/always-running-late/), where it belongs. It is worth noting only that the popular version of it circulates in a far stronger form than the literature supports, and that underestimation turns out to vary heavily by task type rather than being a universal law.

The trust-repair work comes from economic games, scenarios and vignettes rather than from marriages and friendships. That is a real limit. It is still the best available evidence on the specific question of whether unreliability and deception repair at different rates, and the answer to that question is useful enough to include with the caveat attached.

## What Does a Promise You Can Keep Sound Like Out Loud?

It has three properties, and none of them involve promising less.

**It names a time.** Not this week. Thursday evening. The time can be wrong. A wrong time still converts a promise with no location into a promise with a location, and the location is what generates the small useful pressure that gets things done. A promise with a time in it also fails visibly, on Thursday, while there is still a week left to fix it, instead of invisibly on Sunday when there is not.

**It names a smaller unit than the whole thing.** Not I will read your manuscript. I will read the first thirty pages and tell you by Friday what I think of them. The smaller unit is a complete deliverable rather than a reduced one, which is what separates it from simply promising less. The person gets something real on a known date instead of something large on an unknown one.

**It is checkable without asking you.** The best commitments produce evidence on their own. A calendar invitation sent while you are still on the phone. A message with the date in it. A shared document. Anything that means the other person does not have to decide whether it would be rude to follow up, which is a cost they are currently paying that you cannot see.

And an honest limit, because the section would otherwise be dishonest. None of this improves your forecasting. The person who has to make the call on Thursday is exactly as unreachable as before. What changes is that the failure now has a date attached to it, which means you find out on Tuesday instead of on Sunday, and Tuesday is where the useful hour lives.

## How Do You Repair One You Already Missed?

Four parts, in order, and the order does most of the work.

**Name it before they raise it.** Whoever names it first sets what kind of event it was. If they raise it, it is a complaint and you are responding to an accusation. If you raise it, it is an account, and it is a considerably easier conversation for both of you.

**Say what happened in one sentence, without building a story around it.** The week got away from me and I did not call. That is the whole thing. The instinct to explain in detail comes from wanting to be understood, and the effect is the opposite, because a long explanation asks the other person to assess whether your reasons were good enough, which puts them in a position nobody enjoys occupying.

**Do not attach a new promise you have not checked.** This is where most repairs go wrong, and there is a real tension in the research here worth naming. Schweitzer, Hershey and Bradlow found that a promise to change behavior sped up trust recovery, which is a genuine argument for making one. The same work found that deception is the thing that does not recover. A repair promise made on the spot, in the warm state that repair conversations produce, is generated by exactly the mechanism this page has been describing, and missing it is far more expensive than the original.

**Offer the smallest thing you are certain of.** Not I will definitely call this week. Ten minutes now, if they have it, or a specific time you have already looked at. Small and delivered beats large and hoped for by a margin most people badly underestimate.

## What Changes for the Person Who Was Waiting?

What they lost has less to do with the call than with being able to plan around you, and that is the thing that comes back first when anything changes.

Consider what an unreliable commitment does to somebody's week. They cannot count it, so they have to hold two versions of Thursday, one where you call and one where you do not. Holding two versions of something is genuinely tiring and it is invisible to everybody except the person doing it. Over years, most people stop doing it. They collapse to the single version where you do not, which is the moment your promise stops being load-bearing in their life, and it is usually the moment they also stop mentioning it.

So the first thing that returns when this shifts has nothing to do with warmth or with forgiveness. They start putting you into plans as a fixed item again. That change is small, unannounced, and easy to miss, and it is the actual measure of whether anything has moved.

The second thing that returns is that they begin to ask you for things again. People stop asking long before they stop liking you, and the asking is a better indicator than anything either of you would say out loud.

Neither of those responds to a good conversation about it. Both respond to a sequence of small kept commitments with dates on them, which is unglamorous, and which works.

## How Long Does This Take to Shift, Honestly?

It runs at two speeds, and confusing them is the usual reason people give up in the third week.

Your own side moves fast. Catching the moment of promising takes a couple of weeks, because the moment is concrete and has a physical signature you can learn to notice. Changing the sentence you say is faster still, since it is a script rather than an insight and can be practiced. Within a month most people can tell, in the moment, whether the yes they just produced was a plan or an exit.

The other side moves slowly, and it should. The person waiting on you is holding evidence collected over years, and evidence does not answer to explanation. It updates at the rate you produce new evidence, which is one kept commitment at a time, and the first few will barely register because a small number of data points does not overturn a large one. Expect months rather than weeks before you notice them planning around you again, and expect that shift to arrive without comment.

Expect it back under specific conditions, too. When somebody is upset directly in front of you. When the commitment is far enough out that the details have not been generated. When you are tired enough that ending the moment is worth more than getting it right. Those are the conditions the pattern was built for, and their return is the return of the conditions.

It takes time and repetition to build anything that lasts, and there is no way to compress the part where somebody else watches you and slowly changes their mind.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

The hot-cold empathy gap entered the literature in 1996 in a paper on visceral influences on behavior. Read and van Leeuwen published the snack-choice studies in 1998 in Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, finding that current hunger shaped choices made for a week later. Sayette and colleagues published the cold-to-hot work with smokers in 2008 in Psychological Science. Badger and colleagues reported a related finding in 2007 in the Journal of Health Economics, showing that current craving substantially changed how people valued a future dose.

On the distance between intending and doing, Armitage and Conner published their meta-analysis of 185 studies in 2001 in the British Journal of Social Psychology, reporting that intention and perceived control accounted for roughly a quarter of the variance in behavior. Sniehotta, Scholz and Schwarzer published the bridging work in 2005 in Psychology and Health, finding that planning and action control carried most of the remaining distance.

On the future self, Pronin, Olivola and Kennedy published the allocation studies in 2008 in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin. Bartels and Urminsky published the identity-continuity work in 2011 in the Journal of Consumer Research. Hershfield and colleagues published the neural work on future-self representation in 2009 in Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience; the applied interventions built on that line have a weaker record and are described here with that caveat.

Herrnstein and Prelec described melioration in 1991 in the Journal of Economic Perspectives, showing how a series of locally sound choices can produce an aggregate nobody would have chosen. On trust, Schweitzer, Hershey and Bradlow published the promises-and-lies experiments in 2006 in Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, and Ferrin and colleagues published the comparison of apology, denial and silence in 2007 in the Journal of Applied Psychology.

## What Is Worth Doing Before Your Next Promise?

One measurement, small enough to actually run, and it is closer to data collection than to change.

For the next two weeks, every time you make any commitment at all, including the small ones that do not carry the word promise, write down three things in your phone. What you said. What the other person's face or voice was doing immediately before you said it. And whether you felt relief or anticipation in the two seconds afterward.

Do nothing else. Do not try to promise less, do not try to be more careful, and do not attempt any of the sentence changes from further up the page. You are collecting evidence about which root is running, and adjusting your behavior while collecting it will contaminate the only useful data you have.

At the end of two weeks read the list in one sitting. If the relief column is mostly full, your yes is an exit. If the faces column keeps showing somebody upset, the promise was aimed at an expression. If neither pattern appears and the commitments were detailed and evenly spread, you have a forecasting problem and you can stop looking for a character flaw, because there is not one there.

I am not going to tell you that five days makes you somebody who returns every message, because that outcome is less in my control than I would want it to be. What the week is actually for is finding which of these five roots is producing your yes, then building a plan around that specific finding, and then holding you to it once you are home with the same phone and the same people.
