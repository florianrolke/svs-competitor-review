- a: Humor is the most socially rewarded exit available. Nobody at a table objects

    without being named as a departure from the conversation. It works by keeping
    the exchange away from the depth where the real problem would have to be stated
    plainly, and it leaves everyone feeling the moment went well.
  q: Why do I use humor to change the subject when something serious comes up?
- a: Because acknowledging its real size would require acting on it, and the acting
    is what the reflex is protecting you from. As long as the relationship stays filed
    as a rough patch, no decision is owed to anybody. This is one of the clearest
    forms the pattern takes, and it holds longest where the alternative involves a
    conversation you have already imagined going badly a hundred times.

- a: 'The calm can be manufactured. Composure that comes from having looked at something

- a: Minimizing the severity of past harm is common, and it usually does two jobs

    respond to the same slow work of gathering evidence that acknowledgment is survivable.

    situation, which takes energy that was already scarce, and which nobody around

- a: It is extremely common, and it is learned rather than innate. Avoidance shows

    a well practiced one, built at a time when looking really did cost more than not
    looking. That history is why willpower has such a poor record against it. The
    reflex answers to accumulated evidence that acknowledgment is survivable, gathered
    slowly, in low stakes rooms.

- a: 'Surface coping handles the logistics of a problem while leaving its weight untouched.
    The signs are specific: the bill gets paid promptly and the spending behind it
    never gets discussed, the appointment happens and the result gets filed without
    any reaction, the argument gets smoothed over by nine in the evening every single
    time. Ask yourself what you actually felt about the last hard thing you handled
    well. A blank there is the signal.'

- a: It is the move where you grant that something happened and refuse it any consequences.
    Yes, that was said to me, and it was fine. Yes, that year was rough, and it did
    not really affect me. The event survives the retelling intact while the effect
    on you gets edited out. In conversation it usually appears as arguing about whether
    you were entitled to be hurt, rather than simply saying that you were.

- a: Because looking at something does change it, though not in the direction the
    fear predicts. What changes is that you can no longer treat it as possibly smaller
    than it is, and losing that option registers as things having got worse. The problem
    itself carries on at exactly the size it already was. Naming it accurately is
    simply the first moment its actual size becomes available to work with.
  q: Why do I feel like acknowledging a problem will make it worse?
- a: Usually because there is no known floor. Somebody who has spent years routing
    feeling away has no evidence about what happens if they stop, so the honest expectation
    is something bottomless, and a check-in feels like opening that door. Start with
    a question smaller than how you feel. What was the worst ten minutes of today,
    and what was the best. Answers arrive for that when they will not arrive for anything
    larger.
  q: Why do I avoid checking in on myself emotionally?
- a: 'Because worry needs somewhere to go, and small safe problems are where it is
    permitted to land. A throwaway remark can be turned over for three days at no
    real cost to anything. The serious thing gets downgraded on contact, so the concern
    it should be carrying gets displaced onto whatever is nearby and harmless. That
    disproportion is a clue worth following: the item you feel least about is often
    the one to look at.'
  q: Why does a small comment from a friend spiral me into overthinking while a serious
    problem barely registers?
- a: Yes, and predictably so in three places. Money problems compound while the app
    stays closed. Physical symptoms get harder to deal with the longer they stay unexamined.
    Relationships harden as the other person draws their own conclusions from your
    silence. The logic of the pattern guarantees it, because sizing something down
    exists to postpone the response, and postponement is exactly what those three
    charge for.
  q: Can minimizing problems actually make them worse?
- a: Calling your own reaction an overreaction is one of the quietest ways to dismiss
    the problem behind it. It works by moving the conversation onto you and away from
    the thing that happened, and it was usually taught by somebody who found your
    reactions inconvenient. A more useful question is what a person with no stake
    in the situation would say about it, decided before you rule on whether your response
    was warranted.

# Why do I avoid problems instead of dealing with them?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because looking at a problem was, at some earlier point, more expensive than the problem itself, so what got learned was a way of not looking. The reflex sizes a thing down before you consciously decide anything. From inside it registers as being fine, staying positive, or keeping perspective, which is why it can run for twenty years without once being questioned.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
None of these sound like avoidance while you are saying them. Go slowly, and notice which ones are already yours word for word.

- "Dealing with this issue will only bring up more problems."
- "Problems are just negative energy; I choose to focus on the positive."
- "I'm not the type to face problems head-on; I prefer to let them sort themselves out."
- "My problems aren't as bad as others', so I shouldn't complain."
- "I'm just not ready to face the reality of this situation."

Each one sizes a problem down before you have properly looked at it, and the thing that got sized down carries on growing at its own rate.

</div>

A voicemail from a doctor's office asks you to call back to discuss your results. You tell yourself next week, and then it is next month, and by then the not-calling has stopped registering as a decision at all.

There is usually a second one running alongside it. You open the banking app, look at the balance, and close it again without touching the transaction list, because some part of you already knows roughly what the transaction list would say. Both moments feel, from inside, like being busy, being sensible, being someone who does not make a drama out of things. Avoidance would be nobody's first description of them, including yours.

That gap between what the behavior does and what it feels like is the whole difficulty here. Almost nobody living inside this pattern experiences themselves as avoiding anything. They experience themselves as coping well, and a fair number of them are being praised for it.

What follows travels the whole distance: the sentences running underneath, where the reflex was built, what it is quietly holding shut, the places it hides where you would never think to look for it, and what the far end of it looks like in an ordinary week. That far end has a name duller than it deserves. Letting a problem be its actual size.

## What Does Avoiding a Problem Sound Like From the Inside?

Reasonable. That is the part that catches people off guard.

The sentences below are the lines people run in their own heads, in their own phrasing rather than a tidied version of it. They recur with an unnerving consistency across people who have never met and whose lives look nothing alike, which is the first clue that this is something installed rather than something anyone is.

The first group is about consequences, and it is the engine of everything else on this page:

- "I'm worried that addressing this issue will only lead to more problems."
- "I worry that dealing with this issue will uncover more problems I'm not ready to face."
- "I fear that confronting this issue will only lead to more intense problems."
- "Confronting problems always leads to more drama."

The second group is the disguise. Every line in it could be said out loud at a dinner table and collect agreement:

- "I prefer to live in the moment and not worry about problems."
- "I prefer to focus on the now; thinking about problems feels like borrowing trouble from the future."
- "I prefer to focus on the present, even if it means ignoring looming problems."
- "Maybe it's just not in my nature to confront problems directly."

The third group runs underneath the other two, and it is about worth rather than strategy:

- "Problems are a sign of failure; I must appear successful."
- "I don't want to burden others with my problems."
- "I'm not worthy of having my problems resolved."
- "It's just my luck to have problems I can't solve."

And a fourth group arrives only when the pattern gets caught looking at itself, usually late at night, usually said to nobody:

- "I don't want to face the truth."
- "It's easier to be in denial than to face the pain."
- "I'm not sure I can handle the truth behind this issue."
- "I prefer the illusion of harmony to the reality of conflict."
- "I'm afraid of facing the reality that things might not get better."
- "Avoiding this issue is my way of coping."

Read the second group again, because that group does the most damage. Three of those lines could appear on a meditation app tomorrow morning and nobody would blink. A person can run this pattern for a decade while being complimented on their perspective, their positivity, and how little they let things get to them. Nobody goes looking for a way out of being easy to be around.

What matters is the small physical jolt when one of these turns out to be yours word for word, with no translation needed. That jolt is more useful than any definition on this page.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, before anything practical, because several versions of this need more than an article.

If what is being minimized is a physical symptom, get it looked at by a doctor rather than by a search engine. The most expensive version of this pattern is a symptom that stayed theoretical for two years because it never got examined, and no amount of understanding why you avoid the appointment substitutes for the appointment.

If alcohol, other substances, or eating are doing the smoothing over, that belongs with a professional too. Using something to get out of contact with a problem sits squarely inside this pattern, and it is the version most likely to get quietly worse while everything else looks handled.

If what is being minimized is current danger, someone whose moods you organize your week around, a household where raising an issue has consequences, that is not a self-directed project. Bring in people who can genuinely help, including people who can help you leave if that is what the situation calls for.

And if you are carrying a hopelessness that has not lifted for months, or any thought of harming yourself, however quickly it passes, that belongs with a clinician today rather than with a practice you run alone. Asking in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve.

Everything below is written for the everyday version: the unopened statement, the conversation that keeps not happening, the job that has been "fine" for three years.

## Is This Procrastination, or Something Else?

Something else, though the two overlap so constantly that most people use one word for both.

Procrastination works at the level of the task. A specific action gets delayed past the point it should have been done. Minimization works at the level of the size. It decides whether the problem is being weighed correctly at all, regardless of whether anything ever gets done about it.

The distinction earns its keep because the two need different responses. Someone procrastinating usually knows exactly what they are avoiding and can be moved by a deadline, a system, or five minutes of momentum. [The mechanics of that are worth reading on their own.](/insights/how-to-stop-procrastinating/) Someone minimizing can be extremely efficient. They pay the bill on time, book the appointment, answer the email, and still walk away with the problem filed at a fraction of its actual weight, so the part that needed acknowledging never gets acknowledged by anyone.

You can watch the two come apart inside a single sentence. "I finally cleared the card, so that whole thing is behind me now" handles a logistics problem promptly and leaves the three years of spending that produced it completely unexamined.

| The thing it gets called | What that word actually describes | What separates it from minimizing |
|---|---|---|
| Procrastination | A specific task delayed past when it should have been done | Procrastination postpones the doing and leaves the problem correctly sized in your head. Minimizing shrinks the problem and leaves the doing perfectly possible |
| Optimism | A general expectation that things tend to work out | Optimism applies to everything, including the things going well. This positivity is aimed, and it collapses the hour the avoided problem forces itself into view |
| Resilience | Carrying real weight without falling over | Resilience knows what it is carrying and roughly what it weighs. Minimizing has never put the thing on the scale |
| Acceptance | "This is real and I am no longer fighting it" | Acceptance grants the problem its full size and stops struggling with it. Minimizing makes a smaller and different claim: that the thing is less real than it looks |
| Being low maintenance | A genuine preference for less fuss | A low maintenance person can still tell you plainly when something is wrong. This pattern cannot, and the inability shows up only under pressure |
| Emotional maturity | Feeling something fully and staying steady anyway | Maturity of that kind survives being looked at directly. Manufactured calm requires that you keep not looking, which is a different arrangement entirely |

Pick the row that matches the last six months rather than the row that matches your best week. Most people already know which one it is by the second read.

## Where Did the Not-Looking Get Learned?

Somewhere that contact with a problem cost more than the problem itself, and the lesson held.

This rarely traces back to a single decision to start avoiding things. It forms as a rule, learned early enough that it feels like temperament by the time anyone thinks to question it, and it gets installed by two fairly different routes.

The first route runs through what problems themselves came to mean. Looking at something made it real, and people describe that fear in almost exactly those words: "I worry that acknowledging the problem will make it more real and terrifying." Admitting a problem meant admitting failure, said just as plainly: "Admitting there's a problem means admitting failure." Needing help exposed weakness. In a house where any of those held, a child does not learn to solve. They learn to not-look, which is a genuinely effective skill for as long as the looking is what carries the punishment.

The second route runs through homes marked by violence, frequent conflict between the adults, or the long aftermath of infidelity, where harm arrived without warning from people who were supposed to be safe. Minimization forms there as a survival calculation rather than a preference. Peace is fragile. A raised issue can precede real harm. And your own read of a situation cannot be fully trusted, which is what being deceived, or being told that what you plainly saw did not happen, does to a person's confidence in their own judgment over time. [What that environment builds into an adult has its own page](/patterns/growing-up-with-violence/), as does [the scanning that keeps running long after the room is safe](/patterns/walking-on-eggshells/) and [what happens to trust in your own perception](/patterns/trust-issues/).

Both routes arrive at the same adult rule. A problem, once acknowledged, will not stay contained to itself, and it will cost more than staying quiet costs. That calculation got made once, in a smaller and less safe room, by someone doing the best arithmetic available to them at the time. It has been running ever since without review, which is the ordinary fate of any rule that works well enough to stop drawing attention to itself.

## Why Does Confronting One Thing Feel Like It Will Open a Hundred?

Because the fear underneath this pattern is about spread rather than about the problem in front of you.

People say it in nearly identical language, which is how you know it is structural rather than personal. "If I start dealing with this, I'll have to face other issues too." "What if resolving this problem opens up a Pandora's box?" "I worry that dealing with this issue will uncover more problems I'm not ready to face." The prediction is chain reaction: touch one thing and the whole arrangement comes apart at once.

There is a version of that fear that is simply correct, which is exactly why arguing with it has never worked for anybody. Look honestly at why the finances are what they are and you will end up looking at the job, and then at the relationship, and possibly at twenty years of using purchases to manage a feeling. Problems in a life are connected, so acknowledgment does travel. The fear gets two things wrong, and both of them matter.

It gets the speed wrong. Acknowledgment travels at the pace you allow it to, over months, and mostly it surfaces one adjacent thing at a time rather than all of them in one evening.

It gets the obligation wrong. Naming a problem's real size commits you to nothing. You can say "this job has been quietly wrong for two years" out loud, write it down, and then go and make dinner. The mind treats naming and fixing as a single indivisible act, which is why it guards the naming so fiercely. Prising those two apart is one of the most useful moves available in this whole pattern, and it costs nothing to try.

There is a second fear stacked underneath the first, and it shows up wherever emotional fallout is the thing being avoided rather than a practical task. Emotional pain gets treated as a bottomless pit: something that, once entered, might never end. Anybody who has spent decades routing feeling away has no evidence to the contrary, which makes the belief entirely rational from where they are standing. [What that routing eventually turns into is worth reading separately](/patterns/emotional-numbness/), as is [what it actually takes to feel something rather than think about it](/insights/how-to-feel-your-emotions/).

## What Else Is the Minimizing Protecting?

Four things, and it defends all four at once, efficiently, which is why it has survived this long.

1. **Self-image and competence.** Admitting a problem risks admitting failure, so the problem gets undersized instead of the self-image getting revised. This is why the pattern is often strongest in capable people with a track record they feel obliged to keep intact. [The fear sitting under that is its own subject.](/patterns/fear-of-failure/)
2. **The felt sense of being in control.** Minimizing keeps your sense of having a handle on things intact, because full acknowledgment would disturb it. A problem allowed to be its real size makes the future less predictable for a while, and for some people that is the least tolerable part of the whole exchange. [Where the need for that predictability comes from has its own page.](/patterns/need-for-certainty/)
3. **Belonging, and other people's comfort.** "I don't want to burden others with my problems" is one of the most common lines in this pattern. Naming a problem's real size risks being too much, or, in the version shaped by conflict at home, risks the disagreement that once came before real harm. Peace gets protected in place of the truth. [How that protection plays out in the moment a disagreement arrives is covered here.](/patterns/why-do-i-avoid-conflict/)
4. **Against pain with no visible floor.** Covered above, and worth repeating because it is the layer people find last in themselves.

Reading all of that as laziness is why so many people in this pattern are quietly harsh with themselves about it. Containment is the accurate word. It describes a strategy built to keep one problem from becoming every problem, learned in conditions where that fear was frequently accurate, and never updated once the conditions changed.

## Why Does This Look Like Competence From the Outside?

Because in adult life this rarely looks like avoidance. It looks like being fine.

Concretely, it looks like staying compulsively busy so there is genuinely no window in which to look at the thing. It looks like researching and planning endlessly without ever converting any of it to action, and using perfectionism as a permission slip for the inaction. [That particular exit deserves its own reading.](/patterns/how-to-stop-being-a-perfectionist/) It looks like changing jobs, or letting a relationship end, at precisely the point where staying would require something real. It looks like a bank account left unopened, a symptom left unchecked, a diagnosis left unconfirmed.

It also lives in language. Something serious gets described in flattened terms, "it's not a big deal", "it could be worse", in words that plainly do not match the weight of the thing. Humor arrives on cue whenever a conversation gets close to landing. Conversations drift toward the abstract and the theoretical whenever they threaten to become specific. Other people's problems get an enormous amount of attention, which is generous, useful, and also a very effective way of never arriving at your own.

Then there is the version that reads as wisdom. Emotional detachment gets mistaken for maturity. Composure and a kind of emotional minimalism get mistaken for resolution. Analysis gets used in place of feeling, and analysis is the most respectable exit in the entire catalog, because it looks exactly like doing the work. [The wider map of how people avoid a feeling covers the rest of these.](/insights/ways-we-avoid-our-emotions/)

Where the pattern was shaped by earlier violence or chronic conflict, it can also show up as literally downgrading what happened, describing serious harm in mild language, sometimes as a coping mechanism and sometimes out of shame, alongside a hypervigilance about keeping the current peace intact.

The tell across every version is the same. Somebody highly functional and quietly exhausted, with a life arranged so that no situation ever forces them to find out what they can actually handle.

## Where Does It Hide When You Would Not Call It Avoidance?

In six arenas, and most people recognize themselves in one or two before they recognize the rest.

| Where it shows up | What it looks like from outside | The sentence underneath |
|---|---|---|
| Health | An appointment that keeps not getting booked, a symptom researched at midnight instead of examined in daylight | "I'm not sure I can handle the truth behind this issue." |
| Money | Statements unopened, the app closed before the transaction list loads, a rough figure carried in your head for years | "I prefer to focus on the now; thinking about problems feels like borrowing trouble from the future." |
| A relationship | The thing you stopped raising because the last few times it just caused more drama | "Confronting problems always leads to more drama." |
| Work | A calendar with no gaps in it, or a move to a new role at exactly the point the current one would ask for something real | "I'm not ready to step out of my comfort zone." |
| Conversation | Humor arriving on cue, subjects steered toward the general, an unusual reluctance to tell your own story | "I prefer the illusion of harmony to the reality of conflict." |
| Your own inner state | Check-ins skipped, feelings analyzed rather than felt, flatness read as being level-headed | "Avoiding this issue is my way of coping." |

The health row is the one to act on first if it applies, since it is the only row where the cost of another six months is measured in something other than time.

The work row is worth sitting with if your calendar has no gaps in it. Overcommitment is the most defensible form this takes, because every individual yes was generous and each one is easy to justify to anyone who asks. Look at which commitments get accepted. Usually they arrive from other people, which makes them far easier to say yes to than the one thing nobody is asking you to face.

The conversation row hides in plain sight for the sociable. Steering toward the abstract, taking an intense interest in everybody else's situation, and being warmly present without ever once being specific about your own year, all read as good company. [The version of this that shows up as distance from feeling itself has a page of its own.](/patterns/emotional-suppression/)

The body sometimes files a complaint the mind refuses to. Tension headaches, a stomach that acts up in the weeks around something unavoidable, sleep that gets worse the moment the lights go out and the day stops supplying distraction. Get symptoms checked medically rather than assuming they are psychological. The point here is narrower: a body under sustained pressure from something unaddressed will often say so before you are willing to.

## Is Choosing to Focus on the Positive the Same as Minimizing?

Sometimes, and the test is whether the positivity is general or aimed.

A genuinely positive disposition covers everything. It shows up in traffic, in a delayed flight, at a difficult meeting, and it holds roughly steady across the whole surface of a life. The version that belongs to this pattern is targeted. It arrives with unusual force around three or four specific subjects, the ones with the most actually at stake, and it stays notably absent from the small irritations of an ordinary Tuesday.

The second test is what happens under pressure. A general disposition bends. Aimed positivity collapses, and it collapses suddenly, usually at the moment the avoided problem stops being avoidable. People who have never seen you rattled see you rattled, which is confusing for them and often humiliating for you.

There is a whole vocabulary available for making this respectable, and it borrows heavily from genuinely useful traditions. Presence gets used to justify never planning for a consequence. Non-attachment gets used to justify never naming a loss. Gratitude gets used to close a conversation that had barely started. [The specific practice of using spiritual language to skip past something unresolved is covered in full here.](/insights/what-is-spiritual-bypassing/)

The same trick runs on the comparison side. Reasoning that others have it worse invalidates your own experience so efficiently that nothing has to be done about it, and it sounds like humility while it works. If you have ever finished describing something genuinely hard and found yourself adding "but honestly, people have it much worse", watch what that sentence accomplishes. It ends the topic. [Learning that your own reaction counts as data has a longer history behind it for most people.](/patterns/emotional-invalidation/)

## What Does Letting a Problem Be Its Real Size Actually Look Like?

Considerably quieter than most people imagine, and nothing like living in a permanent state of alarm.

Every pattern like this is a polarity with two ends, and describing only the painful end leaves you with an accurate account of your situation and nowhere to walk. The other end has a plain name: acknowledgment of challenges. Naming a problem at its actual size and taking action proportionate to it. Here it is translated out of abstraction and into an ordinary week.

| The minimizing end | The acknowledging end | What it looks like on an ordinary Tuesday |
|---|---|---|
| Denial of reality | Acceptance of reality | The banking app gets opened in a bad month, not only in the good ones |
| Downplaying the size | Naming the actual size | "This has stopped working" gets said about the job in month three rather than in year two |
| Comfort chosen over truth | Truth valued above comfort | The appointment happens instead of another evening reading symptom forums |
| Surface coping | Actually metabolizing it | The feeling gets felt on the day it arrives rather than managed away by nine in the evening |
| Over-simplification | Room for the actual complexity | A situation is allowed to be genuinely good and genuinely a problem at the same time |
| Fear of the truth | Honesty valued for its own sake | A friend asks how work is going and gets "honestly, pretty rough right now", with nothing added to soften it |
| Manufactured calm | Calm that survives looking | You can hold the full weight of the thing and still be steady in the room with it |
| "Others have it worse" | Your own experience counts | The comparison stops functioning as a reason to say nothing |
| Impact denied | Impact acknowledged | You can say that something hurt without also arguing for why it should not have |

Read the right-hand column carefully and notice how little distress there is in it. None of this asks you to become anxious, or vigilant, or the person who brings the mood down. It asks that the difference between a problem and a crisis stay visible, which happens naturally once problems get looked at while they are still problem-sized instead of compounding in the dark for a year.

The calm survives too. What changes is where it comes from. Calm produced by not looking has to be maintained. Calm available while you look directly at something hard needs no maintenance at all, and it is the version that holds up when somebody asks you a direct question.

Don't judge yourself if that column reads as slightly out of reach today. The distance between the two took decades to build, and nobody crosses it in an afternoon.

## What Actually Moves This?

Instruction fails here, which is worth saying before the list starts. Telling yourself to just face it assumes minimization is a decision made fresh each morning, when what is actually running is a reflex, built at a time when looking really was more dangerous than not looking. Reflexes respond to accumulated evidence rather than to instruction, and everything below is a way of manufacturing that evidence cheaply.

1. **Notice where the reflex fires hardest.** Make a short list of everything you would describe as fine if somebody asked casually. The items where "it's not a big deal" arrives fastest and most emphatically are usually the items with the most at stake. That correlation is diagnostic rather than coincidental, and it is the cheapest piece of information available to you this week.
2. **Say one problem's real size out loud, to one person.** Start somewhere low stakes, deliberately. The point is lived evidence that acknowledgment does not detonate anything, and evidence accumulates only through repetition. One accurate sentence to one trusted person, then watch what actually happens over the following days, which in almost every case is nothing.
3. **Separate the naming from the fixing.** Say what is true, then do nothing about it for a week. This sounds irresponsible and is the opposite. It teaches your system the thing it most needs to learn: that acknowledging a problem and being obligated to solve it immediately are two separate events that only felt fused together.
4. **Sit through the ten minutes after.** The urge to reach for a coping behavior will arrive within minutes of any honest acknowledgment. Scroll, snack, drink, a burst of tidying, a sudden idea for a new project. Set the bar low enough that you will actually clear it: ten minutes without the smoothing move. The urge stays present the whole time, and moving past it means going while it is still there rather than waiting for it to quiet down first.
5. **Write the sentence you would not say out loud.** In a private note, in whatever words arrive, however unfair or dramatic they sound. Written honesty is easier than spoken honesty by a wide margin, and it is a genuine first repetition. [There is a fuller treatment of what looking at something honestly actually involves.](/insights/what-is-truth/)
6. **Convert one plan into one action.** If planning is where your version lives, pick the smallest line item from any plan you have been refining and do it this week, badly, before the plan is finished. The exposure that acting brings is the exact thing the planning was arranged to postpone.
7. **If the pattern was built around danger, safety comes first.** Where minimization formed under violence or chronic conflict, acknowledgment cannot stop feeling like an invitation to harm until safety is re-established as a baseline fact of your current life. That is slower work, usually done with support, and it is not a technique you apply on a Tuesday afternoon.

Is any of this comfortable? No. It is small and freaking repetitive, and it works for exactly that reason, in the way that a single dramatic reckoning almost never does.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Longer than a week, and the honest answer runs at two different speeds.

The behavior moves fast. Actually naming a problem at its true size, out loud, can shift within weeks once somebody starts practicing it on purpose. People are frequently surprised by this. The mechanics were never the hard part.

The fear underneath moves slowly. The prediction that acknowledgment triggers catastrophe was rehearsed for years and it fades at the pace of accumulated experience rather than understanding, which is why reading this page changes remarkably little by itself. Fifty ordinary acknowledgments, made in rooms where nothing much was at stake, followed by nothing bad happening, change a great deal. For most people the fear fades rather than fully disappearing. You end up able to feel the pull to minimize, recognize it, and name the thing accurately anyway.

There is a trap worth naming here, because this pattern is unusually good at setting it. Understanding why you avoid problems is itself an extremely comfortable place to sit. It feels like progress, it involves no exposure whatsoever, and it can absorb years. [Why insight so reliably fails to change behavior is worth reading before you spend another month on it](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/), alongside [the mechanism underneath knowing exactly what to do and doing none of it](/patterns/why-cant-i-change/).

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. The thing that took me longest to accept was how ordinary the mechanism turned out to be. I had assumed the avoided things were avoided because they were enormous. Most of them were avoided because looking had been expensive once, a long time ago, in a room I had not thought about in decades. Where it sits for me now is worth saying plainly: the reflex to say "it's fine" has weakened a lot, and it has not gone, and I would rather tell you that than sell a finished version of something nobody finishes.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Decide Anything?

Take one situation and write two sentences about it.

The first is the sentence you actually use out loud when somebody asks. "Work's fine, just busy." "We're good, just a rough patch." "It's probably nothing, probably stress." Write it exactly as you say it, including the word "just" if it is in there, because that word does an enormous amount of the compressing.

The second is the sentence somebody looking at the whole situation from outside would write. Someone with no stake in it, no reason to be unkind and no reason to be kind either. One line. What would they put down.

Then read the distance between the two. That distance is the measurement, and it is more useful than any amount of deciding whether this pattern applies to you. Do it for three situations and something usually becomes obvious: one where the two sentences nearly match, which tells you the reflex is not running everywhere, and one where the second sentence is genuinely difficult to write. The difficulty is the answer. That is the one.
