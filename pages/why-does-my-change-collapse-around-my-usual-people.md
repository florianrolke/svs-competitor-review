  a: "This is close to universal. Family and long-running friend groups are the highest-cue environments most people ever spend time in, so reversion there sits nearer the norm than the exception.   a: "Yes, and the useful move is additive.   a: "Because a boundary is a rule, and the reversion happens before any rule gets consulted. # Why does the change hold everywhere except at home?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

A change holds where it was practiced and collapses where it was not. The old setting is the cue: a specific room, a tone of voice, a chair, a role, all handing the original pattern its trigger before any decision gets made. The people there also still expect the old version of you, out of familiarity rather than malice. Both are workable, and neither one requires cutting anybody off.

You are forty minutes into the visit, in the same chair you always end up in, and you hear your own voice come out at a pitch it has not used anywhere else in years. Nothing has happened yet. Nobody has said the thing they always say. The tightness arrived ahead of the conversation, and the person you have been for the last three months is already somewhere else in the building.

Most people land on a page like this having tried the two available explanations and found both of them useless. The first is that the change was never genuine, which does not survive contact with the eleven weeks it held perfectly well at work, on a Tuesday, under pressure, with nobody watching. The second is that these particular people are the problem, which explains the evening and prescribes nothing you can actually do, since you are going back at Christmas either way. There is a third account, it is duller than both, and it has the advantage of pointing at something.

One plain line before any of it. If what happens in that house involves violence, if you are frightened of somebody there, if you are being controlled through money or movement or who you are allowed to see, or if you are having thoughts of harming yourself, that belongs with a licensed professional and with people who can help you get somewhere safe, ahead of anything written here.

## What Does This Look Like on an Ordinary Visit Home?

Almost entirely unremarkable from outside, which is why nobody in the room registers it happening.

- Sitting down in the same chair at the same table where an old argument used to start, and feeling the tightness arrive before anybody has said a word.
- Three months into a genuinely new way of handling conflict, and losing all of it inside the first hour of a holiday visit.
- Catching yourself using a different voice the moment a parent calls, quieter and more defensive and roughly fifteen years younger, and only noticing once the call has ended.
- Hearing yourself agree to something with an old friend group that you would decline anywhere else in your life without a second thought.
- Walking into a childhood bedroom and reaching for the same old comfort, the phone or the snack or the door closing, before you have put the bag down.
- Driving home replaying the whole evening and wondering whether the last few months were ever real.

Read that list looking for the moment you decided anything. There is no such moment, and that absence is the entire finding. Every item on it describes a response that arrived complete, ahead of deliberation, in a setting where it has been arriving complete since you were a teenager. The version of you that has been steady since March had nothing loaded, because it has never once been run in that kitchen.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, near the top, because this boundary matters more than every practice further down put together.

If closeness or distance is being used against you, warmth restored the moment you back down and withdrawn the moment you assert something, silence used to make you smaller, or somebody holding control over your money, your movements, or who you are allowed to see, none of that gets solved by rehearsing a better response. That needs a licensed professional and, where there is any risk to your safety, people whose job is helping you get somewhere safe. The same holds for any thought of harming yourself, however quickly it passes.

The same applies from the inside. If a low mood has not lifted for months, if there is an addiction in the picture, or if you are currently managing a diagnosed condition, that assessment belongs with a clinician who can see the whole situation rather than one evening of it. And one sentence said once and then left alone: if difficulty with attention, memory, mood or energy has been persistent for you across every setting rather than showing up around particular people, that is worth raising with a doctor or another qualified professional, and this page is about the pattern rather than about any medical question. Asking for help in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve.

## Why Does an Old Setting Have This Much Power Over a New Pattern?

Because a pattern gets stored together with the conditions it formed in, and walking back into those conditions re-supplies them.

The conditions are more specific than most people expect. Not the abstraction of family, but the seat you take, the height of the counter, the tone that used to precede an argument, the order in which people arrive at the table, the exact phrase somebody uses to summon everybody to dinner. Each of those was present during a few thousand repetitions of the old response. Encountering the set again makes that response the easiest thing in the room to reach, and easy things get reached first, before any of the slower machinery involving intention has finished starting up.

Kurt Lewin, a German-American psychologist writing in the 1930s, put this as a formula that has outlived most of what surrounded it: behavior is a function of the person and their environment, taken together. The practical consequence, and the one people skip, is that a description of you that leaves out the room you are standing in is an incomplete description. You have been assessing your own change using exactly that incomplete description.

The cleanest public demonstration is deliberately strange. In 1975, Godden and Baddeley, two researchers at the University of Stirling, had deep-sea divers learn lists of words either on dry land or fifteen feet underwater, then tested recall in the matching environment and the mismatched one. Words learned underwater came back better underwater, words learned on land came back better on land, and recall dropped substantially whenever the two failed to match. Be careful how much weight you put on it. The same pair found in 1980 that the effect largely disappeared when they tested recognition rather than free recall, and the size of context effects in the wider literature has always been modest and inconsistent. What the diver study is good for is proving that the effect exists at all, which is enough to break the assumption that behavior travels with the person and nothing else.

Habit research points at the same thing from a more ordinary angle. Wendy Wood, a psychologist at the University of Southern California who set her work out for general readers in *Good Habits, Bad Habits* in 2019, has argued across decades of studies that most habitual behavior is triggered by stable features of a context, a location, a time of day, a preceding action, rather than by conscious intention renewed each time. Work she published with colleagues in 2005 followed students who transferred to a different university and found their habits shifting when the surroundings that supported them changed. Bas Verplanken and colleagues built a related idea into what they called a habit discontinuity hypothesis in 2008, using people who had recently moved house: a genuine break in context opens a window where behavior is unusually responsive.

| The same person, two rooms | The office on a Tuesday | The kitchen you grew up in |
|---|---|---|
| Repetitions of the new response | Eighty or ninety, most of them recent | Zero |
| Repetitions of the old response | A handful, all of them old | Several thousand, over twenty years |
| Cues present | Almost none of the original set | Nearly all of it, arriving at once |
| Role you are handed on arrival | The one you built for yourself | The one assigned before you could read |
| How fast the response arrives | Slowly enough to choose | Ahead of the first sentence |
| What a slip there would mean | An off day | Proof, apparently, about your whole character |

Look at the bottom row for a moment. The same behavior gets scored completely differently depending on where it happens, and it gets scored hardest in the one place where the deck is most heavily stacked.

## What Does This Sound Like From the Inside?

Like somebody arguing with themselves in language that has been worn smooth by repetition.

People in this pattern say it in almost identical phrasing. These belong to the pull of the old place, the part that knows exactly what is happening and stays anyway:

- "I know I need to be pushed out or push myself out but I just, it’s, it’s a strong hold."
- "I know if I, I move out or you know, get financial independence, you know, my mom’s always there. I know I can always come back. It’s not a problem."
- "No, I enjoy being a child, and I wish to stay a child but I want to grow toward other people as being a man, independent."

A second set runs underneath, and it has a different job. This half is about what the arrangement costs and why it keeps getting paid:

- "I had too little boundaries so I let others steal my time. I used to accept life conditions that were dirty and broken. because this is how I felt."
- "It is easier to be the one who carries it than the one who asks."
- "I do not want to be the person who needs help with this."

Read them slowly. What matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word, with no translation required.

Two things about those sentences are worth noticing before going further. Every one of them is spoken by somebody with full insight into their own situation, which is a useful corrective to the idea that understanding is the missing ingredient. And the last one explains a great deal about why this goes unaddressed for years, since a problem you have decided is beneath asking about is a problem you will keep solving alone, badly, on the drive home.

## Why Does the Group Around You Set the Level You Settle At?

Because ongoing behavior tends to settle at whatever the people immediately around you expect and model, and a family or a twenty-year friend group has been modeling one particular version of you for a very long time.

This runs through channels nobody chooses. The joke that assumes the old you. The task handed over without discussion because you are the one who always does it. The question asked in a tone that expects a particular answer, and gets it. The story retold at the table about something you did at nineteen, which is a small, affectionate act of maintenance on a picture that has stopped being accurate. None of that is aimed at holding you down. It is what familiarity looks like when it has had decades to settle.

The public research here is genuinely interesting and genuinely contested, and both halves belong on the page. Nicholas Christakis and James Fowler, working with data from the long-running Framingham Heart Study, published a series of papers in the late 2000s and a general-audience book, *Connected*, in 2009, reporting that behaviors and states including weight gain, smoking cessation and self-reported happiness appeared to spread through social ties, sometimes out to friends of friends. The findings were challenged hard and fast. Ethan Cohen-Cole and Jason Fletcher argued in 2008 that the same statistical signature can be produced by people choosing similar friends and sharing environments rather than by anything spreading, and later critics raised further problems with the models. The strong version of social contagion should be held loosely. The weak version, that the people you spend your ordinary weeks with shape your ordinary behavior, is about as well established as anything in this area gets, and it is all this page needs.

Consider somebody who noticed that around one particular old friend group they became a more cynical, dismissive version of themselves, one they had otherwise grown out of years earlier. The first conclusion was that the friendship had become fake, or that the group resented who they were becoming. Neither reading survived a closer look. The group's picture of them had settled around a person who no longer existed, and every ordinary interaction kept addressing that person, out of habit and out of a wish not to lose somebody they were fond of. Naming it as attachment rather than as resistance dissolved the resentment that had been quietly accumulating for a year, and it opened an option that had nothing to do with the group at all.

## Why Doesn't a Decision Made in One Room Survive a Different Room?

Because a decision gets made under one set of conditions and tested under a completely different one, and nothing about the making rehearsed it against the testing.

This is the part that feels like betrayal. The decision was real. You can remember exactly how it felt, what you understood, the clarity of it. Then it fails to appear on the one evening it was made for, and the failure reads as evidence that the clarity was self-deception. The clarity was genuine and undertrained at the same time, which is an ordinary combination and a workable one.

| Where the change was made | Where the change gets tested |
|---|---|
| A calm room, chosen by you | A room you did not choose, at a time set by somebody else |
| Nobody playing a role | Everybody playing a role, including you |
| The old cue set entirely absent | The old cue set arriving in full, within seconds |
| Time to think between the trigger and the response | The response already underway before the sentence ends |
| No audience with a fixed picture of you | An audience with a picture twenty years old |
| Success measured against the intention | Success measured against a lifetime of prior evidence |

Two lines of public work sit directly on this gap. Peter Gollwitzer, a psychologist working in Germany and later at New York University, drew a distinction in a 1999 paper between a goal intention, which names what you want, and an implementation intention, which names the specific situation and the specific response: if this happens in that place, I will do this. A 2006 review he published with Paschal Sheeran, pooling ninety-four independent tests, reported a medium-sized advantage for the situation-specific version. Nothing mystical is going on. Naming the situation is what allows the plan to be cued by the situation, which is the same mechanism that makes the old pattern so hard to beat, pointed the other way.

The second line comes from relapse research. Marlatt, a psychologist working at the University of Washington from the late 1970s, built a model of relapse around high-risk situations rather than around weak character, and named a trap he called the abstinence violation effect: after a single slip, the person concludes the whole project has failed, and that conclusion does far more damage than the slip did. Anybody who has driven home from a family dinner deciding that the last three months were fake has met that mechanism personally. The evening cost you one bad hour. The verdict costs you the next three months.

Consider somebody who restructured a costly habit at work and held the new version for the better part of a year, then watched it return completely on every visit to their parents. The habit had been interrupted and replaced everywhere it was actually practiced, which had never included that house. So the house still held the untouched original: the same kitchen, the same tone, the same seat, the same unspoken role expected of them there. What moved it was treating that house as its own separate context with its own separate rehearsal, rather than as a place where an existing change ought to have transferred automatically.

## Does Reversion Happen at Random, or on a Schedule?

On a schedule, and the schedule is exposure.

An unsupported change does not decay evenly over time like a battery running down. It sits intact until it meets a cue-dense environment, then reverts on contact. Which means the dates are largely knowable in advance.

| The exposure | Roughly when it arrives | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| The first visit home | Usually within the first two months | The full original cue set, all at once |
| The first holiday | On a date already in the calendar | Cues plus roles plus an audience plus alcohol plus tiredness |
| The first old-friend catch-up | Whenever somebody suggests it | The peer standard, without much of the physical setting |
| The first phone call with the person who does the tone | Often within days | One relationship functioning as its own context |
| The first bad week after the change | Unpredictable | Whether the new response survives a low state |
| The return to ordinary routine after a trip | Day three or four back | Whether anything besides the trip actually changed |

The predictability is the useful part, and it is worth pausing on how differently it reads once you have it. Of course it happened at Christmas becomes a sentence about a calendar rather than a sentence about your character. Of course it happened on the first call becomes a note about which scene to rehearse next. A pattern that reverts on a schedule can be met at the door. A pattern that reverts randomly can only be regretted afterward, which is where most people have been living with this.

## Why Doesn't More Discipline Fix This?

Because discipline operates on the wrong layer.

Willpower is a state-level lever. It works on physiology, attention and self-talk in the moment, and it can genuinely hold for a few minutes inside a difficult scene. What it cannot do is supply repetitions it never had. Inside that kitchen, an intention with a handful of rehearsals behind it is competing against a response with thousands, in the environment where all of those thousands happened. It loses more often than it wins, and the loss then gets filed as insufficient resolve, which is both wrong and expensive.

Notice what the discipline theory predicts and check it against your own last twelve months. If the problem were resolve, the collapse would be scattered: some bad weeks at work, some slips with new friends, a general unreliability. Instead it is concentrated in one or two places and absent everywhere else, on the same days, in the same person. A lever that works everywhere except one specific location is not the lever that is failing. [Whether a given problem is really about discipline at all is worth settling separately](/patterns/is-this-a-discipline-problem-or-something-else/), and so is [the more general question of why change keeps failing to take](/patterns/why-cant-i-change/).

There is also a well-known model of how change actually proceeds that builds slips in rather than treating them as exits. James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente, working from the early 1980s on how people give up smoking, described change as a set of stages people cycle through repeatedly, with relapse an ordinary station on the route rather than a departure from it. Their model has been criticized for being difficult to test and for the arbitrariness of its stage boundaries, and the part that matters here has survived the criticism intact: people who expect to cycle behave very differently after a slip than people who expected a straight line.

## Do You Have to Cut Anybody Off to Make This Hold?

No, and a page that implied otherwise would be selling you something expensive that does not work.

Removing the exposure does remove the trigger, which is why it sounds so appealing at eleven at night on the drive home. Three things go wrong with it. It leaves the mechanism completely untouched, so the same reversion turns up in the next cue-dense environment you enter, whether that is a new job, a new relationship, or a new friend group with its own settled expectations. It treats people who are pulling on you through ordinary attachment as though they were acting with intent, which is usually false and always corrosive. And for most readers it is neither available nor wanted, since the whole difficulty is that you love these people and want to be around them without losing yourself in the process.

Boundaries deserve a more careful word than the internet usually gives them. A boundary is a rule, and rules get consulted after a decision point. The reversion described on this page happens before the decision point, which is why people can hold a clear, well-reasoned limit in theory and watch it evaporate in practice inside the first twenty minutes. [Setting a limit and having it hold is its own skill with its own page](/patterns/how-to-set-boundaries/), and it works considerably better once the response has been rehearsed in the room where it is needed. Sequence matters here. Rehearsal first, then the rule has something to stand on.

There is a genuinely separate situation where distance is the right call, and it deserves naming rather than burying. Where contact is doing active harm, [the decision to step back from a parent is a real one with real reasoning behind it](/patterns/should-i-go-no-contact-with-a-parent/), and it belongs on its own terms rather than as a side effect of a bad Christmas. This page is written for the much more common reader, who is going back, wants to go back, and would like to arrive as themselves.

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because several different situations produce the same drive home and only one of them is the one this page decodes.

| What it feels like | What it might actually be | The thing that tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| The change was never real | An unrehearsed response meeting its full cue set | It held for months in every setting where it was actually practiced |
| They are pulling me down deliberately | A picture of you that nobody has updated | The pull continues unchanged on visits where you are doing well |
| I am two different people | One person meeting two retrieval contexts | Each version is stable and consistent inside its own setting |
| I have to get away from these people | One context that has never been rehearsed | Whether the same reversion appears with new people in new places |
| Everything I built collapsed | One context collapsed | Score the last month setting by setting rather than as a whole |
| This is a discipline problem | A location problem | Discipline held fine everywhere else during the same week |
| I am the family scapegoat | A role assigned early and never renegotiated | Whether the role is handed to you before you have said anything |

Two of those have better pages than this one. [A whole family system organized so that individual members cannot separate cleanly is a different structure with a different way out](/patterns/enmeshed-family/), and [the emotional baseline you keep returning to regardless of circumstance is its own mechanism entirely](/patterns/emotional-home/). If the reversion follows you everywhere rather than clustering in one or two places, the second of those is the page to read, since it describes a setpoint that travels rather than a context that waits.

## Why Does a Peak Experience Wear Off So Fast at Home?

Because intensity and repetition are separate ingredients, and a peak supplies only one of them.

The pattern is familiar to anybody who has come home from anything intense: a week away, a course, a hard conversation that opened something, a genuinely different few days. The change is real while it lasts. It is also, in repetition terms, almost weightless. A powerful few days can hold five or ten instances of a new response, in a setting purpose-built to make that response easy, watched by people who expect it of you. Then it goes back into a house holding several thousand instances of the old one, in front of people who expect that.

| What an intense week actually supplies | What it leaves exactly as it was |
|---|---|
| A vivid, memorable emotional peak | The number of repetitions the new response has |
| Clarity about what specifically needs to change | Any rehearsal against the rooms where it will be tested |
| A setting engineered to make the new response easy | The cue stack waiting in the kitchen at home |
| A group that expects the new version of you | The group at home, still expecting the previous one |
| Genuine relief and a period of real momentum | The ordinary Wednesday, three weeks later |

Somebody comes home from an intensive program genuinely different, calmer and more direct and less reactive, and is fully back inside the old pattern within three weeks of ordinary life. The usual conclusion is that the program was theater, or that they are the one person it fails to work on. Neither holds. The change had a peak behind it and almost no repetitions, none of them inside the specific rooms, voices and times of day that cue the old response at home, while everybody around them was still quietly rewarding the previous version. What moved it was structural: naming the specific rooms and situations where the old response fires, rehearsing the new one against those named situations, checking in briefly each day rather than relying on resolve, and accepting that the honest timeline is months and several exposures. [What happens in the weeks after any intense experience is a subject in its own right](/insights/what-is-integration/), and it decides more than the experience does.

## What Actually Moves It?

Two structural moves run together, rather than one motivational one, and both can be done alone without anybody's cooperation or awareness.

Nothing below is a validated protocol, and anybody offering you one for your own family is overselling something. What follows is built from how the reversion works, and it is designed so that you can start before the next exposure arrives.

1. **Name the next three exposures, by date.** Open a calendar and find them: the visit, the holiday, the call, the catch-up somebody has been suggesting for a month. Write the dates down. This single step converts a vague dread into three appointments, and appointments can be prepared for.
2. **Walk the first thirty minutes through before you arrive.** Where you will be standing. Who speaks first. The comment that reliably lands. The precise moment the old response arrives. Do this in enough detail that you can see the room, because a plan tied to a specific scene gets cued by that scene, which is the whole point of doing it this way rather than resolving to do better.
3. **Decide your first sentence in advance.** Not a speech and not a confrontation. One line, in the tone you would use with a colleague you like, ready for the moment you already know is coming. Having it prepared matters more than having it be clever.
4. **Change one concrete cue you actually control.** A different seat at the table. Arriving in your own car. A five-minute walk before dinner. Staying somewhere else for one of the nights. Answering the call standing up in a different room from the one you usually take it in. One altered cue keeps the old trigger stack from reassembling completely, and none of these require anybody else to agree to anything.
5. **Build one exit that costs nobody anything.** A short walk, a dish to wash, a phone call you need to return. The point is a legitimate ninety seconds outside the cue set, available on demand, decided in advance so you do not have to invent it while flooded.
6. **Add one person or setting that reflects the new version back.** This is additive rather than subtractive. One regular contact who expects the newer you gives you a second reference point instead of a single old one, and standards move with exposure. Nobody has to be removed for this to work.
7. **Score the month setting by setting.** Write the list: work, the gym, your partner, close friends, the family table, that one friend group. Mark where the new response held and where it did not. Most people discover it held in five places out of six, then discover they had been grading the whole month by the sixth.
8. **Debrief within an hour of leaving, in writing, with no verdict attached.** What actually happened, in factual lines. Which cue preceded it. What you did in the ten seconds afterward. No conclusions about yourself. The debrief is for locating the next rehearsal target, and a verdict written while tired is worth nothing except the damage it does.

Is any of that comfortable? No. Rehearsing a conversation with your own mother, in advance, in writing, like somebody preparing for a presentation, is freaking undignified and it works better than the dignified version, which is arriving with good intentions and hoping. What it costs is twenty minutes before a visit you were already dreading.

## What Does the Other End of This Look Like?

Considerably less dramatic than the word breakthrough suggests, and made almost entirely of small things that happen in the first half hour.

| Inside the reversion | The other end of it | What it looks like on an ordinary visit |
|---|---|---|
| The response arrives before you do | A gap opens between the cue and the reply | You notice the tightness, and the sentence takes a second longer to come out |
| Every setting graded as one verdict | Each setting scored separately | You can say the new response holds at work and not yet here, without either fact canceling the other |
| The pull-back read as resistance | The pull-back read as an old picture | The joke about who you were at nineteen lands as affection rather than as a trap |
| Arriving with good intentions | Arriving with a rehearsed first line | You already know what you are saying when the comment comes |
| No way out of the room | One small exit, prepared | You wash up, walk to the end of the road, and come back |
| A slip means the change was fake | A slip names the next rehearsal | You write down which cue got you, and you use it in January |
| Somebody else has to change first | The only moves are yours | Nothing you did required anyone's agreement or even their notice |
| Distance as the only option | Distance as one option among several | You go, you stay yourself for most of it, and you leave when you planned to |

Nothing in the middle column is impressive, and nobody in the room will comment on any of it. That is what durable change actually looks like from outside, which is to say invisible.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Months and several repeated exposures, and the first one or two will probably still produce a slip.

Two speeds are worth separating, because confusing them is what makes people quit after one bad Christmas. Recognition is fast. Within a visit or two of paying deliberate attention you will be able to name the cue that gets you, often while it is happening, and naming it while it is happening counts as progress even on the evenings you go ahead and run the old response anyway. Watching yourself do it, knowing exactly what you are doing, feels like failure and is genuinely a step.

Access is slow. The new response has to become reachable inside that specific room, and reachable is built from instances rather than from understanding. The honest marker sits later than most people put it: whether the response comes slightly more easily on the third and fourth exposure to the same setting than it did on the first. That curve is what tells you the work is landing, and it is visible only if you write the exposures down, since memory of a difficult evening is unreliable and biased toward the worst ten minutes.

Expect uneven results across settings, and expect a bad exposure after a run of good ones. Illness, a hard week, a job loss, or somebody at the table having a difficult year of their own all put the newer response under load it has not been tested against. The old sequence is still installed. It simply has less of your life than it used to.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From public research on memory, habit, planning and social influence, most of it decades old and all of it checkable without going through me.

Kurt Lewin put behavior as a function of the person and the environment together in the 1930s, which is the frame the whole page sits inside. Godden and Baddeley, at the University of Stirling in 1975, ran the underwater and dry-land recall study with deep-sea divers that made context effects concrete, and the same pair reported in 1980 that the effect largely vanished under recognition testing, so the honest version is a real but modest and inconsistent effect rather than a law. Wendy Wood at the University of Southern California has spent decades on how stable contexts trigger habitual behavior, summarized for general readers in *Good Habits, Bad Habits* in 2019, with related work on students transferring universities published in 2005; Bas Verplanken and colleagues developed the neighboring habit discontinuity idea in 2008 using people who had recently moved house. Peter Gollwitzer's 1999 distinction between goal intentions and situation-specific implementation intentions, and the ninety-four-study review he published with Paschal Sheeran in 2006, are the basis for rehearsing a response against a named scene rather than resolving in general. Marlatt's relapse work at the University of Washington from the late 1970s supplies both the focus on high-risk situations and the abstinence violation effect, the trap where one slip gets read as total failure. James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente's stages of change, from the early 1980s and criticized since for testability, contribute the expectation that people cycle rather than proceed in a line. Nicholas Christakis and James Fowler's Framingham papers and their 2009 book *Connected* are where the social-spread claims come from, and those were contested seriously by Ethan Cohen-Cole and Jason Fletcher in 2008 and by others afterward, so the modest reading is the one used here.

The rest of it is mine and carries no research weight at all. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, and none of the researchers or writers named above has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, theirs is the one worth checking.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before Your Next Visit Home?

Write the next visit before it happens, on one page, and put a date on it. Open a note and describe the first thirty minutes as you honestly expect them to go: the room you will walk into, where you will put your bag, who speaks first, the sentence somebody reliably says, the seat you will end up in, and the exact moment your voice changes. Then add two lines underneath. One naming the cue you think does it. One naming what you will say or do in the ten seconds after it arrives, in ordinary language you would actually use.

Afterward, on the drive home or the next morning, open the same note and mark what actually happened next to what you predicted. Two things usually land. The first is how accurate the prediction was, which tells you this was never mysterious and never random. The second is that having written it down changes the evening slightly even when the old response still wins, because a cue you have named in advance arrives with a fraction of a second attached to it that it never used to have. That costs you twenty minutes and nothing else, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever go anywhere or tell anybody you did it.
