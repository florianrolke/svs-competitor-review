Using it as the explanation of why your week looks that way is where it runs out."

The silence around you reads as indifference from the inside and is usually an information problem instead."

# Why am I still doing every part of it myself?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Usually because a handover has never been possible, rather than because one was tried and failed. Four things have to be true before work can move to another person, and at least one of them has probably never been true for you. Almost all advice about this assumes all four and then works on your willingness, which is rarely what is missing.
</div>

Somebody asks what you do all day and you cannot answer it properly.

Not because it is secret, and not because you are being modest. You start to answer, and about eight seconds in you notice that the honest version would take twenty minutes and would still leave out the part that takes the time. So you give the short version, which is the name of the thing rather than the thing, and they nod, and the conversation moves on, and you go back to it.

That exchange is worth staying with, because it contains the whole mechanism. The work you do has never been described out loud to anybody. Not once, not in full, not even to yourself. It exists as a set of conditions you have learned, thresholds you check without noticing that you check them, decisions you make so fast that they do not register as decisions. Something in that state cannot be given to anybody, because there is nothing there to give. There is no object. There is you, doing it.

That is a very different situation from the one nearly every article about this is written for.

## What Makes This Different From Giving Something Away and Taking It Back?

The order of events, and it changes everything that follows.

There is a well described version of this problem in which the handover happened. You gave somebody a piece of work, it came back in a condition you could not use, and you redid it yourself rather than explain what was wrong with it. That is a real and expensive pattern with an arithmetic of its own, and if that scene is the one you recognize, [the page on taking work back after you have already given it away](/patterns/doing-it-myself-because-its-faster/) is the accurate one for you and this one is not.

There is a second version in which an offer was made. Somebody said, out loud, let me take that, and the answer was out of your mouth before they finished the sentence. That has its own machinery and [its own page](/patterns/refusing-help/). The offer is the event there. Something was proposed and something was declined.

Neither of those has happened to you.

Nobody has taken a piece of this and brought it back wrong, because nobody has ever had a piece of it. Nobody has offered, because nobody knows there is anything here to offer for. There has been no conversation, no attempt, no failed experiment, no rough first version, no awkward moment where you had to say it was not right. There is the work, and you, and a stretch of years.

| The situation this page is not about | The situation this page is about |
|---|---|
| You gave it to somebody and it came back wrong | It has never been given to anybody |
| An offer was made and you declined it | No offer has ever been made, by anyone |
| You could name three people who could do it | You could not name one, and have not looked |
| The problem is the cost of correcting | The problem is that nothing exists yet to correct |
| Somebody knows roughly what you do | Nobody has ever heard the full description |
| You are protecting a standard | No second version exists to hold a standard against |

Read the right column and notice how little of it is about you as a person. Four of those six entries describe an absence in the situation rather than a preference in you, which is why advice aimed at your willingness slides straight off. You are not standing at the top of a diving board refusing to jump. There is no water.

One more boundary before the mechanism, because it is the one people most often arrive here carrying. If what you actually feel is that accepting anything creates a debt you would then owe, [that is a separate mechanism with its own page](/patterns/why-cant-i-ask-for-help/), and it can run alongside this one in the same person without either explaining the other.

## What Has to Be True Before Anything Can Move to Another Person?

Four things, all at once, and the number is the useful part.

Almost everything written about giving work away treats it as a single act with a single obstacle, which is your reluctance. Watch an actual handover happen and it looks nothing like that. It is a stack of conditions, and if any one of them is missing the whole thing fails, usually in a way that gets blamed on the last condition rather than on the missing one.

**One. The work has to have a form outside your own memory.** Not a perfect document. A form. Something a second person could look at and act on without you standing next to them. Most of what you do has never had one, because it never needed one. You were always going to be the person doing it, so no version of it was ever written down.

**Two. A person has to exist who could take it.** Not in principle. In your actual life, this month, reachable. For a great many people carrying this, that person genuinely does not exist yet, and the search for them has never started, for reasons that turn out to be about condition one.

**Three. Some currency has to exist to pay them in.** Money is the obvious one and it is not the only one. Time you could trade back, a favor you could return, a working relationship with enough in it that an ask is not an imposition. Where all three are empty, the handover has no medium, however willing everybody involved happens to be.

**Four. You have to be able to survive the version that comes back.** Not enjoy it. Survive it. Something slower, or shaped differently, or eighty percent as good, arriving in a week where the missing twenty percent is a genuine problem.

| The precondition | What it looks like when this is the missing one | What ordinary advice assumes instead |
|---|---|---|
| A form outside your memory | You cannot write the description of the job, and you have quietly stopped trying | That the task is obvious and needs only assigning |
| A person who could take it | The search has never started and you would not know where it starts | That a queue of capable people is waiting |
| A currency to pay them in | The sums have been run more than once and have never worked | That the money is there and you are being cautious |
| Tolerance for the returned version | You have imagined it arriving wrong and felt the week collapse | That fear is the only obstacle and confidence fixes it |

The reason this matters more than it sounds is that all four failures produce an identical visible outcome, which is one person doing everything. From outside, and often from inside, they look like the same problem. They are four different problems, and what fixes one of them does nothing whatsoever for the others.

Somebody whose missing precondition is the third can spend two years working on their relationship with control and change nothing at all, because reluctance was never the constraint. Somebody whose missing precondition is the first can bring a person in and watch the situation get measurably worse for five months, because a person arriving into a job that exists only inside one memory generates work rather than absorbing it.

## What Do People Call This, and Where Does the Name Stop?

There is one name in common use and it is a folk phrase rather than a research term. It is worth naming anyway, because it is what people actually say when they describe this out loud.

**Wearing too many hats.** One person covering several separate functions at once, switching between them within a single hour, and belonging fully to none of them. It appears in ordinary speech constantly, in workplaces and households and volunteer groups, and people recognize themselves in it immediately. One of the ways it arrives is almost apologetic: *"we have to wear so many different hats all the different time"*, said as an aside, as though it were a fact of the weather rather than a description of a life.

The phrase is accurate about the load. It is a headcount. It tells you that one person is carrying several jobs and it tells anybody listening roughly how heavy that is, which is genuinely useful information and more than most people manage to convey about their week.

Where it stops is the part that decides whether anything changes. The phrase describes how many things you are doing. It says nothing at all about why not one of them has ever moved to somebody else, and those are separate questions with separate answers. Two people can be wearing exactly the same number of hats, for exactly the same number of years, and be in situations with nothing in common: one of them has nobody to give anything to, the other has three willing people and no way to describe the work. The phrase covers both without distinguishing them.

It also carries a small piece of misdirection that is worth naming. The image suggests the fix is to take some hats off, which frames the problem as excess. Sometimes it is. Very often the problem is that the hats have no other head available, and telling somebody with no other head to wear fewer hats is not advice.

| What the phrase genuinely gives you | Where it stops |
|---|---|
| An accurate headcount of the separate functions one person is covering | It counts the functions and says nothing about why none has moved |
| A shared piece of language other people understand instantly | It is understood as a complaint about volume rather than a description of a structure |
| Permission to say the load is unusual without sounding self-important | It suggests the remedy is fewer hats, which assumes another head exists |
| A way of naming the switching cost, which is real and rarely counted | It leaves the switching cost as a feeling rather than as something anybody measures |
| Recognition, which matters and is not nothing | No mechanism, no route in, and no way to tell two very different situations apart |

Said plainly: this is a description, and the page you are reading is about the mechanism underneath the description. The description is where most conversations about this both start and end, which is why so many of them leave everybody feeling understood and nothing different on Monday.

## When Does This Belong With Somebody Qualified Rather Than a Page Like This?

Before anything practical, because it decides whether the rest of this applies to you at all.

This assumes a specific and ordinary situation: you are carrying more than one person should carry, it has gone on for years, and what it is costing you is time, closeness and a certain flatness rather than your health. If that assumption is wrong, most of what follows is the wrong instrument and reading further is a way of postponing the right one.

So read this part properly even if you are confident none of it fits. If the load has started reaching your body, and sleep has stopped working, or something physical has begun that you are managing around rather than looking at, that belongs with a doctor before it belongs with anything else. If the tiredness has flattened into something that no longer lifts on a holiday or after a week away, the difference between being tired and something needing treatment is not a distinction to make on your own. If you cannot stop working even when nothing is urgent, and an unoccupied evening produces something closer to dread than to relief, that is worth taking to somebody qualified rather than reading about.

If your own judgment has started to feel unreliable to you, in the sense that you can no longer tell whether a week was bad or ordinary, that is a signal on its own. And if what you are carrying includes any thought of harming yourself, that belongs with a licensed professional now rather than later, and nothing on this page should delay it by an hour.

One more, because it hides inside this particular shape. If the reason nobody helps is that the people around you actively benefit from you carrying it, and any attempt to hand something over has been met with something that made you smaller, then what you are looking at sits between you and them rather than inside you, and the useful work concerns those relationships rather than your own arrangements.

The rest of this is written for the ordinary, stable, expensive version. You are competent, the thing you run works, and you have not had a genuinely free week in a long time.

## Why Do Two People Doing Everything Themselves Need Completely Different Things?

Because the visible result carries no information about what produced it.

One person is doing all of it because there has genuinely never been anybody. Another is doing all of it because the work has no describable form and therefore cannot be moved, even though two capable people are sitting ten feet away. A third has both of those solved and no money. From outside, all three are the same person: the one who does everything, who looks tired, who says they should really get some help at some point.

The general instruction aimed at all three is identical and it is aimed at willingness, which for at least two of them was never the constraint. Advice that bounces off somebody often enough teaches them that they must be the problem, since it works for other people and has never once worked for them.

Five roots follow. They produce the same week. They need different things, and the most common way somebody loses a year on this is by working hard on a root they do not have.

| The root | What has actually happened | The sentence you catch yourself saying |
|---|---|---|
| Every piece arrived when there was nobody | Work went in through a valve that only opens inward, and nothing scheduled a review | It made sense at the time and I never went back to it |
| Nobody knows the work exists | It has never been described out loud, so no offer has ever been possible | People think I just sit there |
| The arithmetic does not close | The sums have been run and there is no money, no time, and no favor to call in | I would love to, and with what |
| There is nothing to hand across | The work is held as conditions and judgments rather than as an object | It would take longer to explain than to do, forever |
| You have never watched anybody do it | The move has no model, because nobody in your history ever made it | I do not really know how that is supposed to work |

Read all five. Most people find one lands harder than the rest, and a fair number find that two are running at once and that one of them is quietly funding the other.

## What If Every Piece Arrived at a Moment When There Was Genuinely Nobody?

Then nothing about the current arrangement was ever decided, and that is worth taking seriously rather than as an excuse.

Reconstruct how it actually happened, one item at a time, because the reconstruction is where the surprise is. A thing needed doing. On the day it arrived there was no other person, or no money for another person, or no time to find one before the deadline, and you absorbed it. That was the correct call. It was not a compromise or an avoidance. Given the conditions on that specific afternoon, absorbing it was the only available move and it worked.

Then the same thing happened with the next item. And the one after that. Each absorption was correct on its own day, none of them was a decision about the future, and no single one of them was large enough to be worth a conversation.

What is missing from that sequence is any mechanism that runs in the other direction. Work goes in through a valve that only opens inward. Nothing in the arrangement is watching for the moment when a person appears, or the money appears, or the deadline pressure lifts, because watching for that would require somebody to be tracking the conditions under which each item was absorbed, and nobody has ever written those conditions down. The absorption is remembered. The condition attached to it is not.

So the pile is a record of every constraint you have ever been under, most of which have since expired quietly. The volunteer group whose founder is still the treasurer eleven years after the group could easily afford one has usually nothing to do with anybody clinging to the accounts. That person took them in year one because there was nobody, and nothing since has ever prompted a review.

The tell for this root is that you can date almost every item and none of the dates are recent. Go through the list and ask, for each one, why did I start doing this, and the answers arrive fast and specific: because so-and-so left, because we could not afford it then, because it was one afternoon and now it is Tuesdays. Then ask the second question, which almost nobody asks: is that still true. A meaningful proportion of the time the answer is no, and has been no for years, and the discovery is genuinely disorienting.

This root is the cheapest of the five to work on and the one people most reliably skip, because a list of expired conditions does not feel like personal work. It feels like admin. It also removes more from the pile in an hour than most of the deeper work removes in a season.

## What If Nobody Around You Knows the Work Exists?

Then the absence of offers is an information problem, and reading it as indifference has probably been costing you something on top of the work itself.

Start with what other people can actually see. They see outputs: the thing that got shipped, the event that happened, the household that runs, the accounts that balanced. Outputs are visible by design, because an output is the part meant to be seen. Everything upstream of an output is invisible unless somebody narrates it, and you have never narrated any of it, because narrating it while doing it would take longer than doing it.

Star and Strauss made this argument carefully in 1999, under the heading of visible and invisible work. Their point is that visibility is a property of where the observer is standing and of how the surrounding system counts things, rather than a property of the effort itself. The same work is fully visible to the person doing it and structurally invisible to everybody else, and no bad faith is required anywhere for that to be true. Two honest limits: it is a conceptual and observational argument rather than a measured finding, and it names a category rather than establishing a magnitude. What it does well is describe the exact mechanism by which real effort becomes uncountable, which is that nobody is present at the moment the effort happens.

Now put that together with how offers get made. Nobody offers to take on something they have not noticed. There is no reservoir of people around you declining to help. There is a group of people who believe that whatever it is, it is handled, because it always is, and the evidence for that belief is overwhelming and comes from you.

This produces a loop that seals itself, and the sealing is the reason it can run for a decade:

- The work is not described, so nobody sees it.
- Nobody sees it, so nobody offers.
- Nobody offers, so there is no occasion that would require describing it.
- With no occasion to describe it, it stays undescribed.

Nothing inside that circuit generates the interruption. Every step is a consequence of the step before it, and none of the steps involves anybody behaving badly or thinking anything unkind.

The tell for this root is a particular flavor of irritation that arrives without a target. You are not angry at a specific person for a specific failure to help. You are carrying a low, general resentment at nobody in particular, which never resolves because there is nothing at the other end of it. The other tell is the answer you get when you finally describe one piece of it out loud. It is almost never *I know*. It is almost always some version of *I had no idea you were doing that*, delivered with real surprise, and that surprise is the most useful thing on this page if you can get somebody to say it to you.

## What If the Arithmetic Genuinely Does Not Close?

Then this root is real, it is not a story you invented, and it deserves to be stated precisely rather than either dismissed or surrendered to.

There is a version of this advice that assumes the money is there and you are being cautious with it. Sometimes that is true. Often it is nowhere near true, and being told otherwise is why so many people carrying this stop reading anything about it. Somebody describing this out loud put the whole bind in one sentence: *"that drains me. I personally, that, that drains me because I would like to spend more time trying to bring money in or doing. I've that bring money in, not the work, but I haven't got the money to pay somebody else to do it"*.

Read the shape of that rather than the words. The thing that would free the resource requires the resource. Paying somebody needs money the work has not made yet, and making more of it needs the time the paying would have freed. It is a closed loop, and unlike most binds people describe, this one is arithmetic rather than psychology. No amount of clarity about your own motivations opens it.

Two things are worth saying about it, and they point in opposite directions, which is why this root gets handled so badly.

The first is that the loop is often genuinely closed and pretending otherwise is not help. Where the numbers do not work, they do not work. One person put the whole calculation in a sentence: *"I kind of afraid to hire a manager that will speak with the local people [...] Because, you know, a manager should get paid, and if he should get paid, I need to pay it from somewhere."* That is correct arithmetic rather than an avoided feeling, and treating it as the second thing is how the conversation stops being useful.

The second is that the money is usually the only precondition actually missing, and the other three have been left untouched for years on the grounds that the money is missing. That is the expensive part. Describing the work costs nothing. Finding out who in your reach could take a piece of it costs nothing. Deciding in advance what an acceptable returned version looks like costs nothing. None of those requires a budget, all three take a few hours in total, and all three are almost always still undone on the day the money finally appears. Which is how somebody arrives at the moment they can afford help, hires, and discovers the situation gets worse for a season, because the only precondition they had solved was the one that got solved for them.

There is a second currency worth checking before concluding the loop is shut. Money is one medium. Reciprocity is another, and most people carrying this have a favor account that has only ever run in one direction. If you have spent fifteen years doing things for people and have never once called anything in, the balance available to you is much larger than you think, and it has never been tested because testing it would require describing the work, which returns us to the first precondition again.

The tell for this root is that you can produce the numbers immediately. Ask somebody with a different root why they have not brought somebody in and you get a vague answer. Ask somebody with this root and you get a figure, a rate, and a monthly shortfall, because they have run it more than once and can do it from memory.

## What If There Is Nothing to Hand Across, Even With Somebody Willing Standing There?

Then the constraint is the form of the work rather than anybody's willingness, and this is the root that most often hides inside all the others.

Picture the actual moment of transfer, because most advice skips it. Somebody capable is standing there with an hour. You want to give them a piece of it. What do you say? For a task that has ever been written down, this is easy. For a task that has only ever been done, you find yourself starting three sentences and abandoning all three, because the thing you are trying to describe has no beginning, no edges, and no separable steps. It is a set of conditions and thresholds you assembled over years and have never had to state.

Groups solve this without anybody planning it. There is a described model of how it works, usually called a transactive memory system: individuals in a group specialize, and the group carries a rough directory of who knows what, so nobody has to hold everything and any member can find what they lack by asking the right person. The model has an experimental literature behind it, developed by Hollingshead among others, showing that groups with a working directory recall and perform differently from groups without one. I am naming the model rather than a single finding, because the useful part for this page is structural. A person running everything alone has no directory. There is no entry pointing at anybody but them, so there is no route by which a piece of the work could be located, let alone taken.

Then there is the estimating problem sitting on top of it, which explains why the first attempt fails so reliably. Knowing something makes it extremely difficult to model not knowing it. Newton reported a study in 1990 in which people tapped out the rhythm of a familiar tune and estimated how many listeners would name it. Tappers expected around half. Roughly one listener in forty got it. Handle that number carefully: it comes from an unpublished dissertation study, it is retold in business writing far more often than it is examined, and the precise figures should not be leaned on. The direction is what matters, and the direction is that the person who knows the thing has almost no ability to feel how little of it is arriving.

Applied to your afternoon: when you estimate that explaining would take longer than doing, you are estimating from inside the knowledge. That estimate is wrong in a predictable direction, and it is wrong in the same direction every single time, which is how a repeatedly wrong estimate ends up governing fifteen years of decisions without ever being checked once.

The tell for this root is a specific and slightly embarrassing experience: you have tried to explain a piece of it, out loud, to somebody willing, and stopped part way through because you could not find the thread. Not because they were slow. Because you discovered, in the middle of the sentence, that you did not have the description either.

## What If You Have Never Once Watched Anybody Hand Anything Over?

Then the move has no model, and a move with no model is much harder to make than anybody who has seen it done realizes.

This root gets missed because it does not look like anything. There is no wound in it, no scarcity, no bad experience. There is an absence, and absences are invisible from the inside by definition. You cannot notice that you have never seen something.

Look at the houses and rooms you formed in. In a great many of them, one person did everything and nobody discussed it. The parent who ran the household ran all of it, including the parts nobody named. The relative who organized the family organized every occasion for thirty years and was thanked at funerals. The first workplace you took seriously was run by somebody who was in at seven and left at eight and answered every question personally. What you absorbed was a picture of what competence looks like rather than an instruction, and in that picture, competence and doing all of it are the same thing.

What you never saw was the ordinary, unremarkable, slightly awkward version: somebody explaining a task to another person, badly, at length; the second person doing it wrong; a short conversation; the second person doing it better; and then the first person genuinely not touching it again. That sequence is boring and nobody narrates it, so it does not travel. If you have never watched it happen, then handing something over is a skill you have never seen performed rather than one you have declined to use.

The consequence is that the first attempt gets improvised, and improvised handovers fail in a specific way. Too much gets given at once, or too little. The check-in is either constant or absent. Nobody agreed in advance what finished means. The result comes back and there is no established way to say what was wrong with it, so nothing is said, and the whole experiment quietly ends without ever being called an experiment.

There is a fifth root's worth of good news here, which is that this one moves faster than any of the others once it is named, because what is missing is a procedure rather than a disposition. Procedures can be copied. If there is one person in your reach who does this well, watching them do it twice teaches more than a year of intending to be better at it.

The tell is that you find the whole subject slightly unreal. Other people talk about delegating the way they talk about a foreign city they have visited and you have not. You can follow the sentences and none of the pictures arrive.

## How Do You Tell Which of the Five Is Yours?

By checking what has actually happened, rather than by asking yourself how you feel about handing things over.

| The question | What each answer points at |
|---|---|
| Why did you start doing each item, and is that reason still true? | Old reasons still running means the first root. Current reasons means look further down |
| Has anybody offered anything in the past year? | Nobody offering, and no ill feeling anywhere, points hard at the second root |
| Could you say the monthly figure for one hire right now, from memory? | A figure arriving instantly points at the third root. Vagueness points elsewhere |
| Have you ever tried to explain a piece of it and stopped mid-sentence? | That is the fourth root, and the stopping is the whole diagnosis |
| Can you picture anybody in your life ever handing something over properly? | If no picture arrives at all, the fifth root is live |
| When you imagine it done by somebody else, what goes wrong first in the picture? | A quality failure points at tolerance. A blank picture points at form |

Two or three of these will point the same way, which is enough to start on. It is also worth knowing which ones are cheap. The first root and the second root are hours of work, not months. The third is arithmetic. The fourth is a document. The fifth is watching somebody. None of the five requires you to become a different person before anything can move, which is the assumption most advice about this quietly builds in.

## Why Does the Load Not Shrink When the Hours Do?

Because two separate things are being carried and only one of them is made of hours.

The first is the doing. That is the visible half, it is measurable, and it responds to ordinary remedies. Fewer items, better tools, a longer working day, an efficient week. The doing goes up and down and everybody, including you, treats it as the whole load because it is the part that can be counted.

The second is the holding. Being the only place where the whole shape exists. Knowing which of forty things is currently at risk, what the state of each one is, what happens if a particular date moves, which decision is waiting on which other decision. The holding does not stop at the end of a working day, because it has no working day. It has no unit, so nobody counts it, including you when you are estimating your own week.

| The doing | The holding |
|---|---|
| Made of hours, and countable | Made of state, and countable by nobody |
| Stops when you stop | Runs while you are asleep and on a holiday |
| Reduced by a good week, a fast tool, a clear morning | Unchanged by any of those |
| Visible to anybody in the room | Visible to nobody, including the people who live with you |
| Somebody else can take one item of it | Cannot be taken in pieces, because it has no pieces |
| A weekend restores what it costs | A weekend restores nothing, because sleep was never what it spent |

This is the specific reason a genuinely quiet week produces a Monday indistinguishable from the Friday before it, which almost everybody reads as evidence that something is wrong with them. Rest works on the doing. It has never once touched the holding, and nothing that treats the load as hours ever will.

There is a related reason this rarely gets recognized as overload by anybody, including doctors, managers and the people closest to you. Most descriptions of work strain assume you have little authority over how the work gets done. Karasek set out the demand and control model in 1979, dividing jobs by how heavy the demands are and how much say the person has over meeting them, and the quadrant everybody worries about is heavy demand with little authority. Your job sits in a different quadrant entirely. The demands are very heavy and the authority is total, since every decision is yours by definition. That combination reads to everybody as a good job, an enviable job, the kind of independence people say they want.

Two limits on that model, stated because they matter here. The two separate effects are well supported across a large literature: heavy demands predict strain, and having authority over your own work predicts better outcomes. The multiplicative claim, that control buffers demand and therefore protects you, has had weak and inconsistent support in later reviews, and it is repeated with much more confidence than the evidence carries. So the model explains cleanly why nobody around you reads your situation as a load. It does not license the conclusion that being in charge makes the demand harmless.

## Where Does This Hide When You Would Not Call It Doing Everything?

In a great many places that have nothing to do with a business, which is where it goes unaddressed longest.

| The setting | What it looks like from outside | Who else could actually name what happens |
|---|---|---|
| A household | Somebody who is very organized and never seems to mind | Nobody. The appointments, renewals and thresholds live in one head |
| An extended family | The one who arranges things, thanked warmly at every gathering | Nobody knows which documents are where, or when anything falls due |
| A volunteer group | A founder still doing the accounts eleven years in | One other person, roughly, and only for the last two years |
| A small team inside a large place | The person everything routes through, described as indispensable | Their manager could name three of forty items |
| A project of your own | Something you have been working on quietly for a long time | Nobody has read it from the inside, ever |
| Care of another person | A relative managing everything, who says it is fine | Nobody. Not the other relatives, and often not the doctors |
| A craft or a trade | One person doing the work, the quoting, the invoices and the van | Nobody, and that is normal enough in the trade to be invisible |

Look down the third column. That is the actual finding, and it is the same in every row regardless of how different the settings look. The load is not what makes these situations similar. What makes them similar is that in each one, there is exactly one person who could say what happens next, and no record of it anywhere else.

The version people write about most is the one where a whole structure sits on a single person and the org chart has one box doing the work of nine. That version has its own page: [what happens when an entire operation rests on one person, and what a week away does and does not fix](/retreats/founder-burnout-retreat/). It is one setting among several and it is not the only one, and treating it as the whole subject is why a person running a household on the same mechanism never finds anything written for them.

There is a test that works in every row, and it takes a minute. Imagine you are unreachable for twelve days. Not dramatic, and not permanent. A bad flu and a phone left in a drawer. Now ask what actually stops, and then ask something harder: could anybody else even work out what has stopped? For most people carrying this, the first answer is uncomfortable and the second answer is the one that changes their afternoon.

## Why Does the First Attempt Make Everything Worse Before It Makes Anything Better?

Because for a while you carry the old load and the new one at the same time, and almost nobody is warned about this.

Run through what actually happens the first time you give a real piece of it away. You still know the work, so you are still holding it. On top of that you now have to describe it, which you have never done. Then you have to check what comes back, which is a new task. Then you have to repair or re-explain the parts that missed, which is another. Meanwhile the other person is slower than you, because everybody is slower at something in their first three attempts, and their slowness has become your problem.

Total load in week one: higher. Week two: higher. Week three: usually still higher, and this is where most attempts end.

The curve is real, and it says nothing at all about the other person. It is what transferring anything costs, and it is paid entirely up front while the return arrives entirely later. For most ordinary tasks the crossover sits somewhere between three and eight repetitions, which in real life means weeks. The exact number matters far less than knowing there is one, because a person who expects the rise can sit through it and a person who does not will read week two as proof.

What people conclude in week two is almost always the same sentence, and it feels like hard-won evidence: it is faster if I just do it. In that specific week it is completely true. It will also be true in week three and false forever afterward, and there is no signal at the moment it flips, so somebody who stops during the rise never finds out that they stopped one round short.

One practical consequence worth naming. Because the cost is front-loaded, the worst possible time to attempt a first handover is a week already at capacity, which unfortunately describes almost every week you have. Postponing indefinitely does not follow from that. What follows is picking something small enough that the rise is survivable rather than something big enough to be worth the effort, which is the instinct almost everybody follows and the reason most first attempts are chosen badly.

## Why Does Just Hire Someone Fail as Advice?

Because it answers the second precondition and quietly assumes the other three.

Somebody arriving does not produce a describable task. It does not produce the money, which is why the advice lands as slightly insulting when the money is the missing piece. And it does nothing at all about your ability to tolerate a version you would not have made, which is the precondition people discover last and usually in public.

The sequence when only the second precondition is solved is predictable enough to write down in advance. A person arrives. There is nothing written, so the first fortnight is spent with them shadowing you, which slows you down and produces no artifact. They begin taking pieces, and every piece requires a conversation, because the conditions live in your memory. Their versions come back at seventy percent, correctly, because that is what a third attempt looks like. You repair them yourself, quietly, because the deadline is real. Three months in, your hours have not fallen, there is now a salary going out, and the honest internal summary is that it did not work.

That summary is wrong in an expensive way, because the experiment that failed was never the one people think. What failed was an attempt to move undescribed work into somebody else's hands. Nothing was learned about whether the person was capable, and nothing was learned about whether you can let go, although both will be blamed.

The related advice that fails for the same reason is the instruction to be less of a perfectionist about it. In a great many of these situations the standard is genuinely the thing that keeps the work working, and being told to lower it reads, correctly, as being told to be worse at the thing you are good at. What actually needs to happen is more boring and more specific than either instruction: the standard needs to be written down once so that it exists somewhere other than in your judgment. That is a document rather than a personality change, and it is why this whole subject responds better to an hour with a blank page than to a year of resolutions.

There is a third piece of standard advice worth retiring here. The suggestion to simply do less assumes that the pile is made of optional things. Some of it is, and the deleting is genuinely the fastest single reduction available. But a large part of what routes through you holds something up in the plain sense that something breaks if it stops, and telling somebody in that position to do less is an instruction with no available action attached to it.

## What Does This Cost, Given That Nobody Ever Sends a Bill?

Nothing that arrives as a single event, which is exactly why it runs for so long without being addressed.

The first cost is an upper bound on what can exist. Not on what you can do, which is impressive and which you have been pressing against for years. On what can exist at all, since anything that must pass through one person is limited to what one person can personally touch. That number is reached earlier than anybody expects and does not move much with effort.

The second cost is more specific and gets misread constantly. Work that is genuinely too large for one person tends to be abandoned close to the end rather than at the start, because the last stretch is where the volume becomes undeniable and where there is nobody to share it with. If that has happened to you repeatedly, [the pattern of things stopping at ninety percent has its own page](/patterns/abandoning-things-at-ninety-percent/) and the two mechanisms feed each other in a way worth understanding separately.

The third cost is that nobody worries about you, and this one is quiet enough to miss for a decade. People do not check on the person who handles everything, because handling everything is the information they have, and their inattention is an accurate response to what you have shown them for fifteen years. The result is a life in which you are surrounded by people who like you, would help instantly if asked, and have no idea that anything is required.

The fourth is the one that shows up in the body eventually. Not dramatically, and not as an event. As a gradual reduction in the number of things you can be interested in, because interest requires spare capacity and there is none. People describe this as having become boring, or as having lost something, and it is more accurately described as having nothing left over.

The fifth is a risk rather than a cost, and it is the one that would appear in any competent review of an arrangement like yours. Everything sits on one person, and that person is subject to ordinary life. A bad flu. A family emergency. A week in bed. Nothing in the arrangement survives that, and nobody has ever looked at it, because looking at it is itself one more thing that routes through you.

## What Actually Moves This?

The preconditions, in order, and the order is not the one people reach for.

Almost everybody starts at precondition two, because it is the one that feels like action. You look for a person. What happens next is the sequence described above, where a capable person arrives into a job that has no form and the whole thing gets read as a failed hire. Starting at two while one is missing is the single most common way this gets attempted and the single most reliable way it fails.

**Start with the inventory, because it is cheap and nothing else works without it.** One sitting, an hour, one line per item. Everything that routes through you, including the small ones that never take long and the ones that only come round twice a year, which are the likeliest to be invisible even to you. Against each line, one column: could anybody else currently name that this exists.

That hour does three things at once, and only one of them is the obvious one. It produces a list, which is the obvious one. It converts a single undifferentiated weight into a countable set, which changes how the load feels within about a day, because a countable thing can be reasoned about and an uncountable one can only be endured. And it reliably surfaces a group of items, usually somewhere between a fifth and a third, that no longer have any reason to exist and can simply be stopped. That last group is the fastest reduction available to anybody in this position, and it requires no other person, no money, and no conversation.

**Then describe exactly one thing.** Not the system. One recurring task, written down as though somebody else would be doing it next month. Half a page. What triggers it, what finished looks like, what the two or three judgment calls are and how you make them.

This is the step people skip because it feels like preparation rather than progress, and it is the actual work. It is also where the estimating error shows up in a way you can see. Time it. Compare the real figure with the number you would have guessed. The gap between those two numbers has been governing this for years and almost nobody has ever looked at it.

**Then look for the receiver, with the description in your hand.** This is a completely different search from the one that has never started. You are now able to say what the person would do, which means you can ask people whether they know somebody, which means the question is answerable. A search for somebody to help with everything has no answer and no first move. A search for somebody to do the specific written thing has both.

**Then decide the tolerance in advance, in writing.** Before anything comes back, write down what an acceptable version looks like, including what you will let stand that you would have done differently. Decide that on a calm morning rather than in the moment the version arrives, because the moment it arrives is exactly when you will be least able to judge it.

**Then expect the rise and count the repetitions.** Three to eight. Write the number down so that week two has something to be measured against other than your mood.

One thing worth saying about the emotional half, since a page about mechanisms can imply there is nothing else here. For a good number of people, working through those five steps is straightforward until the fourth, at which point something arrives that has nothing to do with logistics. If handing over a small piece produces a reaction out of all proportion to the piece, the preconditions are solved and the thing in the way is elsewhere, and that is worth knowing rather than pushing through.

## Which Parts of This Are Research, and Which Parts Are Mine?

Worth separating, since the two carry very different weight and this subject attracts a lot of confident writing built on very little.

On work that goes uncounted, Star and Strauss published the argument about visible and invisible work in 1999, in the journal Computer Supported Cooperative Work. It is a conceptual and observational paper rather than a study with a measured result, it establishes a category rather than a magnitude, and its original setting is systems and organizations rather than households. Both authors are on one paper, so it counts here as a single line of evidence rather than as two. It is used as a lens and should not be read as a measurement of anything.

On how groups avoid one person carrying everything, the transactive memory model has been developed experimentally since the mid-1980s, including a well-known line of work by Hollingshead on how members retrieve what other members know. I am naming the model rather than resting the argument on one person's finding, partly because no single result carries it and partly because the useful part here is the structure it describes rather than any measured effect size.

On the difficulty of estimating what another person does not know, Newton reported the tapping study in a 1990 doctoral dissertation. It is unpublished, small, and retold in business writing far more often than it is read. The specific figures should be handled with care. The direction of the error has held up in other work on how badly people model the state of somebody else's knowledge, and the direction is the only part this page uses.

On why a heavy load with total authority does not read as strain, Karasek published the demand and control model in 1979 in Administrative Science Quarterly. The two main effects are supported by a large literature. The interaction claim at the center of the model, that authority buffers demand, has repeatedly failed to replicate cleanly and is quoted with far more confidence than it has earned. That limitation is stated here rather than left out, because the model is being used for the part it does establish.

Two things this page deliberately does not claim. It does not claim that the state you are in has a name, because the folk phrase names the load and nothing else, and no research term covers the situation described here. And it does not claim to know which of the five roots is yours, because nothing written down can establish that, and any page that tells you otherwise is selling the certainty rather than earning it.

The rest is mine and carries no research weight at all. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind and I am not a therapist. None of the researchers named here has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my reading of it disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Faster than you would guess for four of the five roots, and the speeds are wildly different, which is why a single answer is useless.

The inventory is an hour and it pays out the same day. That is not a figure of speech about how it feels. The deleting alone usually removes real items from a real week inside the first week of the list existing.

The description of a single task is two hours and it is the highest return per hour available anywhere in this. Most people find it takes about a quarter of what they expected, and the discovery that it was a two-hour job rather than a two-week job does more to move this than any amount of resolve.

The receiver search runs on the description and takes as long as your particular circumstances take, which nobody can promise you. What can be said is that it becomes a real search rather than an intention on the day the description exists.

The first handover takes three to eight repetitions to stop costing more than it saves. In ordinary life that is four to ten weeks. Almost every abandoned attempt stops inside that window.

And the holding, which is the part that actually costs you, takes longest and moves in a way that surprises people. It does not shrink when the doing shrinks. It shrinks when the shape exists somewhere other than in your own memory, which is why the writing steps do more for the exhaustion than the transferring steps do. People routinely report the load feeling lighter before a single task has moved, and then distrust that because nothing has visibly changed.

Expect it to come back under pressure. A bad month, somebody leaving, a period where the arithmetic gets tight again, and the whole thing reassembles quickly. That is the conditions returning rather than everything you built being lost, and the second recovery is much faster than the first because the documents still exist.

## What Does the Resolved Version Actually Look Like?

Not an empty week, and any description involving an empty week should be treated with suspicion.

You still do a lot of it. Some of it you do because you are the right person for it and you want to, and naming that out loud is part of the resolution rather than a failure of it. A version of this where you have handed over everything you are good at would be worse than where you are now and nobody sensible is aiming at it.

What changes is the holding. The whole shape exists somewhere other than one memory: on paper, in a shared place, in the heads of two other people who could describe roughly what happens next. Nothing about that is impressive to look at, and it is the entire difference between a load that runs while you sleep and one that stops when you stop.

The second change is smaller and stranger. Offers start arriving. Not because anybody has become more generous, but because for the first time there is something visible for an offer to attach to. People who have known you for fifteen years say some version of *I had no idea you were doing that*, and then, sometimes within the same conversation, offer to take a piece. That sequence is common enough to expect and it is disorienting the first time, because it appears to contradict fifteen years of evidence that nobody was interested.

The third is that the twelve-day test stops being frightening. Something would still slow down. Something would go wrong. And somebody other than you could describe what has stopped, which is the whole of what makes an arrangement survivable rather than an arrangement waiting for a bad month.

## What Is One Thing to Do This Week, Before You Give Anything to Anybody?

One hour, one page, and nobody needs to be told you are doing it.

Sit down with one sheet of paper and write everything that routes through you. Not organized, not prioritized, just written, including the small ones, the ones that come round twice a year, and the ones you would be embarrassed to admit take up space. Then go down the list once and mark each line with a single letter: **D** if it could simply stop, **W** if somebody else could do it once it was written down, **M** if it needs money that does not exist, and **Y** if you want to keep it and would fight to.

Most people expect the list to be about thirty items and find it is closer to seventy. Most people expect the D column to be empty and find it holds a tenth to a third of the page. And almost everybody discovers that the Y column, the things they actually want, is short, specific, and has been buried under everything else for years.

That sheet costs you an hour and gives you the one thing your situation has never had, which is a description of the work that exists somewhere other than in your own head. Nothing else on this page can start until it does.
