# Why does my child act exactly like me when I'm at my worst?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because a child builds their working model of relationships from what a parent does under pressure, and that is the only version of you happening in real time when something goes wrong. Stated values get heard. Stress responses get copied. The accuracy of the copy is what makes it frightening, and it also means your child is reading who you are now rather than keeping a permanent score.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
Parents who end up reading something like this are usually carrying a few of these already, and most of them are about their own childhood rather than their child's.

- "I felt like with my mom, I had to be the parent."
- "I was a little girl. I shouldn't have to raise myself."
- "I'm already bored of being strong."
- "Yeah, I'm broken. But I function."
- "I don't want to be stronger."

The hardness that carried you through all of that is the same hardness your child is now practicing back at you, and it is workable.

[How the seminar works with a stress response your child has already learned](https://florianrolke.com/the-soulsearching-experience/)
</div>

Your eight-year-old slams a door and storms off down the hall, and the posture is exactly yours. Same set of the shoulders, same clipped phrase you use about them once your patience has run out, arriving back at you in a voice three decades younger.

If you have seen that once you already know what this page is about, and you are probably carrying the harder question underneath it. You watched them pick it up from you. What you want to know is whether it is now permanent.

One plain line before anything else, because everything here is written for the ordinary version of this. If there is violence in the house in any direction, if your child has talked about hurting themselves or has hurt themselves, if anyone is being hit, or if you are carrying a hopelessness that has not lifted for months or any thought of harming yourself, that belongs today with a licensed professional and with people who can help you get somewhere safe. A page cannot hold that, and nothing below is a substitute for it.

What follows is written for the parent, which makes it slightly unusual. Almost everything published about a difficult family dynamic is addressed to whoever was the child in it. You are quite likely both at once: the adult running the household now, and the person who learned what to do under pressure inside a house you did not choose. Both halves tend to show up in the same afternoon. If the half that pulls harder is the one looking backward, [the version of this written for the person on the receiving end of a controlling parent](/patterns/controlling-parents/) covers that side properly, and it is a better use of an hour than reading this one twice.

Most parents arrive at this question after a specific incident. Sometimes it is small and repetitive: a tone, a slammed cupboard, an eye-roll that could have been photographed off your own face. Sometimes it is one large frightening thing that people around you have already begun describing as a problem with your child. The mechanism is the same in both cases, and the large version is often the small one after months of nobody responding to it.

## What Is Your Child Actually Copying?

Your stress response, in real time, rather than your values.

This is the part that catches people, because most parents are doing a genuinely good job on the values. You have said the right things about honesty, about mistakes being fine, about being able to come to you with anything. Your child heard all of it. Then something went wrong on a Wednesday evening, and for four seconds your child got to watch what you actually do when a situation stops going your way, which is the only footage available of how relationships work under load.

| What you have told them | What gets demonstrated when something goes wrong | What the child files as the working rule |
|---|---|---|
| "We talk about things in this family" | A raised voice that ends the discussion in four seconds | Volume ends discussions |
| "It is fine to make mistakes" | A spilled drink met with a sharp intake of breath and a lecture | Mistakes are dangerous |
| "You can always come to me" | A phone checked mid-sentence and a distracted nod | Reaching for someone costs you something |
| "I want you to be honest with me" | Honesty met with a flare of temper, then a long silence | Honesty gets punished, and managing people is safer |
| "Nobody pushes me around" | An hour of cold quiet until somebody gives in | Withdrawal is how you win |
| "I am completely calm" | A locked jaw, a raised shoulder, a very level voice | Calm is something you perform over the top of something else |

Read the middle column rather than the left one. The left column is what you believe. The middle column is what got demonstrated, and demonstration is the only channel with the bandwidth to carry this.

Albert Bandura ran the experiments that put this beyond argument, at Stanford in the early 1960s. Nursery-age children watched an adult behave either aggressively or mildly toward an inflatable doll, then went into a room containing the same toys. The children reproduced what they had watched, including specific gestures and specific phrases, with nobody instructing them and nobody rewarding them for it. The finding that made his name is that observation on its own is sufficient. Nothing has to be taught for it to be learned.

Attachment researchers describe the same channel from the inside of the child. John Bowlby's term for it is an internal working model: a template a child assembles for how relationships operate, built out of what repeatedly happens between them and a caregiver rather than from what the caregiver states is true. Mary Ainsworth turned it into something observable in a laboratory. Both names are worth searching if you want the primary literature, and both point at the same unglamorous fact. Your child is running on your demonstrated behavior, and the file updates live.

Don't take that as a verdict on you. Every parent alive is modeling something under pressure, most of it unchosen, and the ones who look effortless are usually the ones whose own stress response happens to be gentler for reasons they had nothing to do with. [The baseline atmosphere a household actually runs on has its own page](/patterns/emotional-home/), and it is worth reading separately if what you recognize is the temperature of the house rather than a specific behavior.

## When Does This Need a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Sooner than most parents think, and the boundary is worth stating plainly rather than leaving at the bottom.

Bring in a professional now if your child has hurt themselves, has talked about wanting to, or has made any kind of threat toward themselves or anybody else. The same holds if there is physical violence in the household in any direction, if anybody is frightened of anybody else in a way that governs how they move around the house, or if a substance is involved in the escalations. Those situations need somebody who can see your family over time, and they need it regardless of how accurately anything on this page describes the underlying dynamic.

The same applies to you rather than your child. If you are carrying hopelessness that has not lifted in months, an addiction, a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, or any thought of harming yourself, that comes first and it comes before any parenting project. Asking in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve, and a parent who gets support is doing the single most useful structural thing available to them, since your state is the material your child is working from.

There is also a middle case worth naming. Plenty of parents get a referral, feel it landed on the wrong person, and either accept it silently or refuse it out of embarrassment. Both are avoidable. A clinician sees your child, because your child is the person in front of them. What you know about the pattern between the two of you under stress is information they do not otherwise have, and taking it into the room with you tends to produce a far more useful assessment than either accepting a label or fighting one.

## Why Does the Mirror Only Come Out at Home?

Because you are the person your child can afford to stop performing for.

Teachers routinely describe a child their parent does not recognize: helpful, easy, well-liked. Grandparents report the same, and so does the friend's house where your child apparently clears the table without being asked. That gap is one of the most common sources of private shame in parenting, since it seems to prove the problem is specific to you. Structurally it points the other way. Public settings get the managed version. The unmanaged version comes home, to the person whose response is most predictable and whose attention is worth the most.

There is a second reason, and it is the one this page is built on. Your child learned the pattern from watching you, so you are the person it was calibrated against. A stress response absorbed from a parent gets tested on that parent first, because that is the relationship it was derived from and the one where it has the best chance of working. A child does not run your escalation at their piano teacher, because it was only ever a specific answer to a specific person under a specific set of conditions.

Which means the behavior you find least tolerable is, awkwardly, evidence of the closeness you are worried you have damaged. Nobody de-masks in front of someone who does not matter to them. [Households where the atmosphere shifted without warning](/patterns/growing-up-with-an-unpredictable-parent/) produce a sharper version of this again, because the child has to keep reading the parent continuously to know which version of the room they are in.

## Who Is Really Steering a Standoff Between You and Your Child?

Your child, in most standoffs that end with you raising your voice, and this is the single most useful reversal on the page.

From the inside, matching a child's escalation feels like taking control back. You held the line. The behavior stopped. Order returned to the kitchen. Look at the sequence from outside and the causality runs the other way: your child produced a behavior, and your response arrived reliably, at a volume they could have predicted, within seconds. Something that can be called up on cue is being operated rather than doing the operating.

| The moment | How it feels from where you are standing | What is structurally happening |
|---|---|---|
| Your child escalates | An unprovoked attack on an ordinary evening | A lever gets pulled that has worked before |
| You match the volume | Establishing that this stops here | The lever produces its result, on schedule |
| Your child escalates again | Deliberate defiance | Confirmation that escalation is a working method |
| You go further, or go cold | Regaining control of the situation | The demonstration gets more vivid |
| Your child backs down | You won, and relief arrives | A loop your child opened has closed |
| Nobody mentions it at breakfast | It blew over | Both of you learned the same lesson again |

Notice what neither of you learned in that sequence. Nothing about the actual disagreement got resolved, and both people practiced the belief that the loudest available response is how a relationship gets steered. Your child has now rehearsed it once more, with you as the model and the sparring partner in the same body.

The reversal is uncomfortable enough that it usually needs saying out loud before it registers. Something close to: my child can produce my response whenever they want it. Parents tend to argue with that sentence for a while and then go quiet, because the evidence is sitting in the last six months of their own kitchen. Seeing it clearly changes what happens next without requiring any technique, since it is very difficult to keep performing a move once you have watched yourself being cued into it.

None of this makes the escalation acceptable or means you have to absorb it. It relocates the intervention. [Wanting the situation to be under control is its own pattern with its own mechanism](/patterns/why-do-i-need-to-control-everything/), and it usually turns out to be about the parent rather than about the child.

## Is Something Wrong With My Child, or Are They Starving for Me?

In the large majority of cases the second, and the reason the first reading wins so often is that it is the easier one for a parent to hold.

That deserves saying flatly rather than surveyed, because the hedged version helps nobody at eleven at night. An alarming incident, damaged property, a note somebody found, a threat, a public scene that everyone is still talking about, has two readings that fit the same facts. One says something is wrong with this child. The other says this child has been escalating a request that nobody has answered, and has reached the volume where it finally gets a response. The second reading asks more of you, which is exactly why the first one arrives faster.

| The incident | The frightening reading | The more common reading | What tells them apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| Something got broken during a fight | Destructive, possibly dangerous | An escalation aimed at a specific person in the house | Whether it happens with everyone or mainly with one of you |
| A frightening note or statement | Evidence of something clinical | A bid that grew after quieter versions failed | What the quieter versions looked like, and when they stopped |
| A public scene | A child who cannot regulate anywhere | A child who de-masks with you and holds it together elsewhere | What the school actually reports |
| Refusing to speak for hours | Manipulation | A copy of a withdrawal they have watched work | Whether anybody else in the house does exactly that |
| An outburst that came from nowhere | Unpredictable behavior | A response to a change in the household nobody explained | What changed at home in the previous fortnight |

Both readings can also be true at once, which is the part that gets lost when a parent is asked to pick a side. A behavior can be an accurate copy of your stress response and a bid for your attention in the same act. Responding only to the copy leaves the hunger running. Responding only to the hunger leaves the demonstration running. The two motives are not competing theories to choose between, and treating them as a menu is one of the more reliable ways to stay stuck for a year.

Hold on to the accuracy point, since it is the one that quiets the worst of the fear. A child who was not watching you closely could never reproduce your pattern this precisely. The precision of the copy is the evidence that attention was being paid, and attention paid to a parent is not a symptom.

## What Does This Sound Like From the Inside?

Almost identical, from one parent to the next, and almost never about the child.

These are the sentences that run underneath, in the exact phrasing they arrive in rather than smoothed into a description of a mood. Most of them are about a childhood rather than a household:

- "I can blame my mother for teaching me polarity and that there's strength in weakness."
- "My mom doesn't accept growth."
- "Actually, probably my mum wouldn't have been so weak if I hadn't stepped up."
- "My dad left and I had to be a parent with my mom."
- "That's what being a single mum is. There's nothing else."

Read them slowly and see whether one of them is yours word for word, with no translation required. That small physical jolt is the useful part, and it tends to arrive on the sentence you would least want to admit to.

Two things are worth noticing about that list. Almost none of it concerns the child in the house right now, which tells you where the material actually lives. And the last one is a definition rather than a feeling. Somewhere a description of a situation became a description of a person, at which point it stopped being something that could be revised.

## Why Does Seeing Yourself in Your Child Land So Hard?

Because you recognize it, and recognition arrives faster than any thought about it.

A stranger's child having a tantrum in a supermarket produces mild sympathy and a quiet gratitude that it is not your turn. Your own child using your posture, your volume, and occasionally your exact sentence produces something much hotter, and the extra heat has a specific source. Two things are firing simultaneously: the ordinary irritation of the moment, and the private information that you are watching something of yours played back at you with the lighting turned up.

That second reaction is shame, and shame is expensive because of what it does next. A parent flooded with it gets fewer options, faster, and the option that stays available is usually the loudest one. Which produces another demonstration, which is what the child copies, which produces more of the thing that triggers the shame. [Reactions that seem out of proportion to what actually happened](/patterns/why-do-i-overreact/) run on this circuit almost every time, and the size of the reaction is measuring the meaning rather than the event.

There is a version of this that is worth separating out, because it is the good news hiding in the bad. Recognition is also information. You cannot change a pattern you have never seen, and most parents spend years unable to see their own stress response at all, since it happens too fast and feels like a reasonable reaction to circumstances. A child running your pattern back at you is the clearest footage you will ever get. Painful, accurate, and free.

## Where Did the Toughness Come From, and Is It Still Needed?

From a period when it was genuinely required, which is exactly why it has been so hard to put down.

A lot of parents reading this built their capability during something real. Single parenting with no support. A stretch of years where nobody was coming to help and the only available option was to become the person who handles it. A financial emergency, an illness in the family, a household where being soft was not survivable. The hardness that came out of that was not a distortion. It was competence, it worked, and it is probably the reason several things in your life did not collapse.

What happened afterward is that the conditions changed and the strategy did not. Murray Bowen's family systems work has a formal name for the shape this makes across generations, the multigenerational transmission process, describing how patterns of emotional functioning move down a family line and reappear in different surface forms. The surface can look completely reversed while the underlying structure repeats. A parent who was overwhelmed can produce a child who becomes a parent who is dominant, and both households run on the same rule about who is responsible for managing whom.

Aimed at a genuine emergency, that hardness is competence. Aimed at a seven-year-old who spilled something, it lands as threat. The strategy did not become wrong. It is being pointed at people who were never the danger, by a system that has no way of telling the difference on its own.

Which is why attacking it does not work. Parents who decide their toughness is the problem and set out to dismantle it usually find it back within a fortnight, harder than before, because a survival response that is under attack defends itself. What tends to lower the defensiveness is the opposite move: crediting the thing. Naming what it actually did for you, out loud, in specifics, and only then asking whether the conditions that required it still apply. A strategy that has been thanked goes considerably more quietly than one that has been condemned. [The mechanics of forgiving yourself for something you genuinely did are worth a separate look](/patterns/how-to-forgive-yourself/), because the guilt underneath this is usually the part keeping it running.

## Can Love Be Real and Still Fail to Arrive?

Yes, and this is the most quietly painful item on the page.

You can love your child completely and have that love fail to register as love, if the register it is delivered in is consistently curt, impatient, or edged with control. The reason is unfair and simple: people receive delivery, since delivery is the only part that is observable. Your intent is invisible. Your tone is not. A child cannot check the feeling underneath a sentence, so the sentence is what gets stored, and what gets stored is what gets copied.

Parents usually assume the love is self-evident. Of course they know. I work six days a week for this family. That assumption is doing a lot of load-bearing work, and it tends to hold right up until a teenager says something that makes it clear the message never landed at all, which arrives as one of the worse evenings a parent has.

The workable part is that delivery is a skill and feelings are not. Nothing about how much you love your child has to change, which is a relief, because that part was never the problem and could not have been fixed by effort anyway. What changes is the register: whether affection arrives with an instruction attached, whether praise gets delivered on the way past, whether the only sustained attention available in the house is the kind that follows something going wrong. [Warmth that never quite reaches the person it is aimed at has its own page](/patterns/emotional-invalidation/) from the receiving end, and reading it from that side is uncomfortable in a productive way.

## Are You Handing Your Child the Role You Had?

Possibly, and the tell is whether your child is managing your state rather than their own.

The structure worth checking is a child doing emotional labor that belongs to an adult. Reading your mood before deciding what to say. Softening bad news, or hiding it. Refereeing between grown-ups. Comforting you. Making themselves smaller or larger depending on what the room appears to need. That job description reproduces across generations even when the style flips completely, which is why a parent who spent their own childhood managing an overwhelmed adult can end up with a child managing a dominant one.

Both are the same assignment. In each case the child is doing the tracking, and the adult's state is the weather everyone else has to dress for. If that describes your household from either direction, [the pattern where a child becomes responsible for a parent has its own page](/patterns/parentification/), and [the adult version, looking back at a childhood spent as the responsible one](/patterns/i-had-to-grow-up-too-fast/), covers the side you may recognize from your own history.

The reason this matters here rather than sitting in a separate discussion is that the two mechanisms run together. A child managing your state is watching your state very closely, which means they are also copying it very accurately. The role and the mirroring feed each other. Reduce the amount of your weather the household has to forecast, and both loosen at once.

## Why Does a Spilled Drink Become a Catastrophe in Four Seconds?

Because the size of the reaction is set by what the event means to you, and meaning bypasses assessment entirely.

The chain is worth writing out, because parents rarely see it in full until it is on paper. A teenager comes home drunk and is sick in the bathroom. Within seconds: this becomes a habit, she ends up unconscious somewhere, somebody takes advantage of her, she dies. Four steps, under a second each, arriving as a single lump of dread rather than as a sequence anybody could inspect. Then a response comes out of you calibrated to step four rather than step one, and your child, who is at step one, experiences it as wildly disproportionate, because from where they are standing it is.

Here is the distinction that does the work. Stress is making something bigger than it actually is. Failure, in this specific sense, is making something smaller than it actually is. Most parents in this pattern are running the first one continuously while worrying that they are guilty of the second, which is why the amplification feels responsible rather than reactive. Naming the size of the thing accurately, out loud, before responding to it, is most of the intervention.

What matters for this page is what a child absorbs from living inside that. A household where ordinary disruptions reliably trigger instant amplification teaches its children that instant amplification is what disruption calls for. Which is how a nine-year-old ends up catastrophizing about a forgotten homework sheet in a way that is recognizably, uncomfortably, yours. [The full mechanism of worst-case chains has its own page](/patterns/catastrophizing/), and it is the single most contagious item in this whole cluster.

## Can You Keep Your Standards and Drop the Need to Control the Outcome?

Yes, and separating those two is the most practical relief available in this material.

Parents hear letting go of control as being asked to give up their values, which is a reasonable misreading and completely wrong. There are two different acts inside what feels like one. Expressing what you believe, what you expect, and what frightens you is available to you at all times, regardless of what your child does with it. Requiring that your child comply, on your timeline, was never actually on offer, and no amount of force converts one into the other.

| The act | Who it depends on | What it costs you | What it teaches your child |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stating clearly what you value and why | You alone | Very little, and it can be repeated calmly | That you are knowable, and that positions can be held without force |
| Naming a fear plainly, once, without pressure | You alone | A moment of vulnerability | That fear can be spoken rather than converted into volume |
| Setting a consequence and applying it consistently | You alone | Consistency, which is harder than intensity | That the world has reliable structure |
| Requiring agreement | Your child | An escalating series of standoffs | That relationships are won and lost |
| Requiring it on your schedule | Your child, plus luck | Chronic disappointment, aimed at the child | That love arrives with conditions and deadlines |

The bottom two rows are where the disappointment lives, and they were doomed from the start rather than failing because you have not pushed hard enough. Handing them back reads as a concession and works out as a discovery, since you have been paying an enormous price for something you never actually owned. [Expectations placed on a child that were never really about the child](/patterns/unrealistic-parental-expectations/) tend to sit right underneath this, and the two are worth untangling separately.

Is it easy to hold a standard while genuinely releasing the outcome? Honestly, no. It requires you to say the thing, mean it, and then watch your child do something else with it, on an ordinary Tuesday, without escalating. That is real, freaking discipline, and it is the part that actually changes what your child sees.

## What Actually Moves It?

What you do the next several times something does not go your way, repeated across months, since that is the only material your child is working from.

Nothing here is a validated protocol and anybody offering you one for this is overselling something. What follows is worked out from how the transmission runs, and it is built to be practiced while your household is actually running rather than understood on a quiet evening when nothing is at stake.

1. **Watch the first five seconds, not the conversation afterward.** The calm debrief matters far less than what happened before it, since the debrief is teaching and the first five seconds are demonstrating. Whichever of the two comes second is the one that overwrites.
2. **Name the size of the thing out loud before responding.** Something close to: this is a spilled drink, and I noticed my whole body treat it as an emergency. Saying it in front of your child does two jobs at once, interrupting your own chain and demonstrating that a reaction can be inspected rather than obeyed.
3. **Say the reversal to yourself once, plainly.** My child can produce my response whenever they want it. Do not do anything with the sentence for a week. Simply let it be true and notice how often the evidence turns up.
4. **Credit the toughness before retiring it.** Name what it did for you, specifically, out loud or on paper. A survival strategy that has been thanked goes quietly. One that has been attacked digs in and comes back harder.
5. **Answer the quiet version of a request.** The escalated behavior usually had a smaller predecessor that nobody answered. Find out what it was and respond to that one next time, before the volume becomes necessary.
6. **Repair out loud, and do it fast.** Say what you did, without a defense attached and without a lecture bolted onto the end. Repair is a demonstration too, and it is the demonstration most parents never received, which is why it is the one most worth handing on.
7. **Separate the standard from the compliance.** State your value once, clearly. Then let your child do whatever they are going to do with it, and hold the consequence rather than the argument.
8. **Fix your own state before addressing the behavior.** Food, sleep, ten minutes outside, a delayed conversation. A parent operating at their own limit is running the pattern by default, and there is no technique that beats sleep.
9. **Feel it rather than analyzing it.** Building a theory about your child is the most respectable exit available and it is still an exit. When you notice yourself constructing an explanation, drop into the body and find where it actually sits: chest, throat, jaw. [There is a separate page on how to do that.](/insights/how-to-feel-your-emotions/)

The uncomfortable part is that none of these are conversations, and conversations are what most parents reach for. A talk about how things are going to be different feels substantial, gets an emotional response from everyone, and transmits almost nothing, because it is a statement of values and the values were never the channel. [Why insight so reliably fails to change behavior](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/) applies to parenting more brutally than almost anywhere else.

## Is It Too Late If Your Child Is Already a Teenager?

No, and the reason has better evidence behind it than most reassurance of this kind.

Children track a caregiver's current state, continuously, rather than maintaining a running verdict. Edward Tronick's still-face work, first presented in the mid-1970s, demonstrated how immediately even infants respond to a caregiver's affect: a parent who goes blank produces distress within seconds, and a parent who re-engages produces recovery almost as fast. What came out of that line of research is more useful than the dramatic part everybody quotes. Ordinary interaction between a parent and a child is out of step a great deal of the time, and the outcomes that hold up are predicted by how reliably the mismatch gets repaired rather than by how rarely it occurs.

Which means the target was never a household without rupture, and it is worth pausing on that, since the fantasy of the calm parent has probably been costing you something. Rupture and repair is the mechanism. A parent who loses it, comes back, says plainly what happened, and does not do it again that evening is running the process correctly. Your child gets to learn that things break and get fixed, which is a considerably more useful working model than one built on nothing ever going wrong.

The strongest version of this comes from the adult attachment work of Mary Main, Erik Hesse and colleagues at Berkeley. Their interview research found that what predicted a child's security was not the content of the parent's own childhood but whether that parent could make coherent sense of it. Adults with genuinely difficult histories who had done the work of understanding them went on to raise secure children at rates comparable to adults with easy histories. The term that came out of it is earned security, and it is the most concrete piece of evidence available that history is not the deciding variable here.

None of that means an established pattern reverses in one exchange. Years of demonstration take a long series of counter-demonstrations to revise, and expecting otherwise produces its own disappointment cycle: a parent changes for eleven days, sees no difference, concludes it does not work, and returns to the old response with the added conviction that they tried. Your child has new material to copy immediately. They will not trust it for a while, and that caution is reasonable rather than a verdict on your effort.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Months to years, unevenly, with the noticing arriving long before the behavior changes.

Progress runs in a predictable order and it is worth knowing the order so you can recognize the early stages as progress rather than as failure. First you notice an hour afterward, usually while lying in bed feeling terrible. Then twenty minutes afterward. Then during, which is the worst stage, because you are fully aware of what you are doing and doing it anyway. Then, eventually, early enough that a choice exists. Each of those transitions takes a stack of real instances rather than any amount of resolve.

Watching yourself escalate while knowing exactly what you are doing feels like failure and is genuinely a step forward. Don't judge yourself for the during stage. It is the point at which most parents quit, and it is the last stage before the one that works.

Expect the old response to return under load. Illness, a job loss, a bad month financially, a bereavement, or simply a run of nights without enough sleep will bring back the full original version, sometimes long after you were confident it had been retired. That is what an installed pattern does. The measure that actually tells you something is how fast you notice and how quickly the household settles afterward, rather than whether the response arrived at all.

Your child changes on their own schedule, which will not match yours. A younger child often shifts quickly, within weeks, because their template is still being written. A teenager takes longer and usually tests the change several times before relying on it, which reads as ingratitude and is closer to due diligence. [Rebuilding trust after a long run of the same behavior](/patterns/how-to-rebuild-trust/) works the same way with a child as it does with anybody else: slowly, through repetition, and with the person who did the damage carrying the cost of proving it.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From five bodies of published research, all of which can be checked without going through me.

Albert Bandura's social learning experiments at Stanford in the early 1960s established that children reproduce a modeled behavior after observation alone, without instruction and without reward, which is the direct grounding for this page's central claim. John Bowlby's concept of the internal working model, set out across the three volumes of *Attachment and Loss* published between 1969 and 1980, and Mary Ainsworth's observational work reported in *Patterns of Attachment* in 1978, describe how a child assembles a relational template out of what actually happens rather than out of what is said. Murray Bowen's family systems theory supplies the multigenerational transmission process, the formal name for a pattern of emotional functioning moving down a family line and reappearing in a different surface form. Edward Tronick's still-face paradigm, first presented in the mid-1970s, demonstrated that young children respond to a caregiver's state in real time, and the research line that followed it made repair after rupture, rather than the absence of rupture, the thing that predicts a healthy outcome. Mary Main, Erik Hesse and colleagues at Berkeley developed the Adult Attachment Interview in the mid-1980s, and the earned security finding that came out of that work is the reason anybody can say with a straight face that a difficult childhood does not determine what a parent hands on.

The rest of this is mine and carries no research weight whatsoever. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, I am not a therapist, and nothing here is an assessment of your child. None of the researchers named has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, go and check theirs.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Say Anything to Your Child?

Run a five-second log for one week, and say nothing to anybody about it while you do.

Every time something does not go your way, and the bar for that should be low, a spilled drink, a late message, a plan changing, write down three things in a note on your phone. First, what actually happened, in one factual line with no adjectives in it. Second, what your body did in the first five seconds: jaw, shoulders, breath, volume, the specific words if there were any. Third, what your child did within the following ten minutes, if they were in the room. Do not attempt to change anything yet. Changing it while you are still gathering the information tends to give you a week of performed calm and no data.

At the end of the week, read the second and third columns side by side and ignore the first. Two things usually surprise people. There are fewer distinct responses than expected, often three or four, running on repeat for decades in nearly identical form. And the resemblance between column two and column three, in the same week, on the same day, is difficult to argue with once it is written down in your own handwriting. That costs you a week of noticing and nothing else, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever go anywhere.
