# What do I do when my partner won't work on any of this?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Most partners who refuse therapy, books, and every conversation about growth are refusing the format rather than refusing to ever change. Direct correction gets resisted on delivery, however accurate it is, and pressing harder produces more withdrawal. What moves it is asking instead of telling, naming the specific unmet need, and accepting that the decision itself is theirs.

## Is Your Partner Refusing the Format, or Refusing to Change?

Almost always the format, and telling the two apart changes everything you do next.

The formats are the visible, named things. A therapist's room. A book you left on their side of the bed. A podcast. A weekend away. A conversation that opens with we need to talk about us. Somebody can decline every one of those and still be a person who moves, slowly, in their own vocabulary and on their own schedule. Somebody else says yes to all of it, attends, nods, uses the words correctly, and drives home running the identical pattern. Visible participation is a much weaker signal than it looks.

Here is how to tell which one is in front of you. No single row settles it. Read the whole column.

| What to look at | Refusing the format | Refusing to change at all |
|---|---|---|
| One plain complaint, raised once, calmly | Gets a real answer eventually, sometimes days later | Gets deflected, mocked, or turned back onto you every time |
| Anything they have changed in the last two years | You can name at least one thing, however small | Nothing comes to mind, in any area of their life |
| A direct question about their own experience | Awkward, short, sometimes irritated, and answered | Refused, ended, or converted into an argument about you |
| Movement outside your formats | A habit quietly dropped, a routine started, an old friendship repaired | No movement anywhere, over years |
| What happens when you drop the subject for a month | They occasionally raise something themselves | Total silence, and visible relief |
| Curiosity about you | Still there, in ordinary moments, unprompted | Gone, or only present when they want something |
| The word therapy specifically | Refused, while other conversations stay possible | One refusal among a general refusal of everything |

Two honest cautions about that table. Most people find themselves split across rows rather than sitting cleanly in one column, which is ordinary and slightly annoying, since it means the answer is a mixture. And a genuine, total refusal to ever change anything is rarer than it looks from inside month eighteen of the same argument. The refusal in front of you has usually been shaped, over years, by how the request keeps arriving.

If the person you are describing cannot get near a feeling at all, in any format, with anyone, including themselves, then [the distance itself is the more accurate address](/patterns/emotionally-unavailable/) and that page is written for it. This one is for the narrower and far more common thing: they will not do it your way, in your language, on your timeline.

## What Happens Inside a Partner When You Raise It?

Something physical, and it starts before they have processed a single word of your point.

Conflict researchers describe a state called flooding, where a person's heart rate climbs, attention narrows, and the capacity for taking in new information drops sharply. In that state, the thing that reliably follows is the shutdown: the flat face, the short answers, the retreat into the phone or the garage or a suddenly urgent task. It looks like contempt from the other chair. From inside, it is closer to an overload with no available exit except leaving the conversation.

Layered on top of that is something more specific to being corrected. Clinicians who work on behavior change have a name for the urge to fix somebody with accurate information: the righting reflex. The finding that made it worth naming is that acting on it increases resistance rather than reducing it. Argue somebody toward a change and they will argue the other side, out loud, and get better at it each time. Being right is not what is being contested in that moment.

Put those two together and the daily experience makes more sense. You raise something true. Their body goes into a state where nothing new can land. Your accuracy makes the position harder to concede, so they defend it. You watch a person refusing an obviously good idea and conclude they do not care enough to try. They watch a person telling them what is wrong with them again and conclude nothing they say will be heard anyway.

Neither read is malicious. Both are wrong. And both get more entrenched every time the same conversation runs.

## Why Does Pressing Harder Produce Less Movement Every Time?

Because the pressing and the withdrawing are one machine, and each half feeds the other.

Marital researchers have documented this for decades under the name demand and withdraw: one partner pursues change by raising the issue, pressing, criticizing, while the other deflects, shuts down, and disengages. The part that matters practically is that neither role is the cause. Each response makes the other more likely, so the sequence tightens on its own without either person choosing to tighten it.

Read that from inside your own week and the loop is uncomfortably recognizable.

1. **You raise it.** Reasonably, at first, with a specific example and no accusation attached.
2. **They go flat.** Short answers, no eye contact, the conversation ends by attrition rather than by resolution.
3. **You read the flatness as indifference.** Which it very rarely is, and which reliably produces a stronger version of step one, later that week or later that year.
4. **They read the stronger version as proof.** That any conversation about the relationship becomes a list of their failures, so the next one gets shut down earlier.
5. **The relief teaches both of you.** You feel briefly better for having finally said it. They feel briefly better for having ended it. Both of those reliefs install the sequence more deeply.

The loop has a further property that makes it hard to see. Your escalation is a response, so it never feels like escalation from where you are standing. It feels like finally being clear. And their withdrawal is a response, so it never feels like refusal from where they are standing. It feels like protecting the evening.

[The general mechanics of a two-person loop that runs on rails are set out here](/patterns/why-do-we-keep-having-the-same-fight/), including the base-rate check and the design-instead-of-negotiating move. This page stays on the narrower question underneath it: what to do when the thing they will not engage with is the idea of working on themselves at all.

## Why Does a Refusal Land as a Verdict About Love?

Because a refusal about something this important feels like it has to be about you, and from inside the hurt there is no other available reading.

The internal arithmetic is fast and it runs almost identically in everybody who does it. This matters more to me than anything. They know that. They are refusing anyway. Therefore I matter less than their comfort. Therefore I am not loved the way I thought I was. People say the conclusion out loud in almost exactly these words: "I also felt that I wasn't good enough and I wasn't very loved by Him."

Here is the checkable problem with that arithmetic. The same no would very often show up in response to being told anything, about anything, by anyone. It is aimed at the delivery structure rather than at the content or at the person delivering it. Watch how your partner responds to unsolicited advice from a parent, a boss, a friend who knows better about their car. If the shape is the same, you have a person who resists correction, and you have been reading a general trait as a specific message about your worth.

There is a second internal report that belongs beside the first, and it comes from the other chair. "I don't know how to communicate. Nothing to do with love, I just don't know how to communicate." A person can be entirely certain of their love and entirely without the equipment to demonstrate it in the register their partner is asking for. From outside, missing equipment and missing care look identical. [The specific version where affection is genuinely present and never lands has its own page](/patterns/feeling-unloved/).

None of that obliges you to be satisfied. It relocates the question from do they love me, which the refusal does not actually answer, to what would have to change for this to be liveable, which is a question you can work on.

## Why Does the Ultimatum Get Obeyed and Change Nothing?

Because it can only ever purchase the form of the demand, and the form was never the subject.

Therapy or we are done gets a session attended. Stop doing that or I am leaving gets the thing stopped, sometimes for years, honored exactly. And then nothing else moves, which is the part that catches people completely off guard. A compromise negotiated under pressure can be kept to the letter while the need it stood in for goes entirely unaddressed, because the rule was never really about the rule.

Someone spends a year keeping an agreed-on limit precisely, offers that as evidence of full commitment, and is genuinely bewildered when their partner is no happier than before. The limit was standing in for something that was never named out loud: attention, or being asked about, or being wanted rather than accommodated. Nobody itemized it, so nobody could aim at it. Both people end up simultaneously exhausted and unheard, both convinced they have already tried everything.

Compliance without a decision underneath it also has a distinctive fade. It holds for two weeks, sometimes for a year, and then quietly reverts, usually during a stressful stretch when maintaining it stops being possible. That revert then gets read as proof the person never meant it, which is unfair. They meant the compliance. Nobody ever asked them for the decision, and the two are different purchases.

There is one honest use for an ultimatum, and it looks different from the outside. Where you genuinely cannot continue as things are, saying so as a decision about your own life is real and sometimes necessary. That sentence names what you will do, without predicting what they will do. It is a boundary rather than a lever, and [holding one you can actually keep is a skill of its own](/patterns/how-to-set-boundaries/).

## Where Is Your Partner Actually Standing Right Now?

Somewhere on a path, and pressure aimed at the wrong point on it fails predictably.

Behavior change researchers describe a sequence of stages that people pass through: not yet considering it, weighing it, preparing, acting, and maintaining. The model is debated in the literature, and some researchers argue the stages describe what change looks like rather than explaining what causes it. Taken as a locator rather than as a mechanism, it earns its place here, because it explains why the identical request produces gratitude from one person and a wall from another.

| Where they are | What it sounds like | What pressure does here | What actually helps here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Not yet considering it | "There's nothing to work on." | Entrenches the position and adds an argument they now own | Ordinary curiosity, no agenda, no follow-up question dressed as one |
| Weighing it | "Maybe. I don't know." | Forces them to voice the case against, which strengthens it | Let them say both sides out loud without correcting either |
| Preparing | "What would it even involve?" | Rushes a decision into an obligation | One concrete, low-cost, easily reversible option |
| Acting | "I'll go once and see." | Turns a first step into a program they did not agree to | Notice it once, plainly, and then leave it alone |
| Maintaining | Nothing. It is just how the week runs now. | Reopening the old grievance restarts the whole sequence | Let the past round stay finished |

Most of the frustration in this territory comes from a mismatch of exactly one stage. The reader is in preparing, with a therapist researched and two evening slots identified. The partner is in not yet considering it, and has been asked, effectively, to skip three stages by Friday. The request makes perfect sense as content and remains unreachable from where they are standing.

Locating the stage honestly also tells you something you might not want to know. If your partner has been in the first row for six years, across every subject, that is different information from a person who oscillates between the first two rows depending on the month.

## Could They Be Trying in a Channel You Are Not Counting?

Frequently, and this is the single most common misread on both sides of the conversation.

Effort has categories, and people give it in the category they were taught counts. Somebody who grew up watching a parent provide will give hours, money, reliability, and a roof, and will experience that as an enormous and continuous act of love. Somebody who grew up starved of attention will register attention, questions, touch without an agenda, and being asked how the day actually went. Two people can each be pouring out effort at full volume and neither one receives anything, because the pouring and the receiving are happening in different rooms.

From inside the giving, more of the same is the obvious answer to try harder. So the provider provides more, works another Saturday, keeps the agreed rule more exactly, and cannot understand why the ledger will not balance. From the other chair, none of it registers as the thing that is missing, so it reads as no effort at all.

The move that breaks this is unglamorous and it works. Sit down and itemize, from their position rather than your own, exactly where their effort has been going for the last year. Not to be generous. To be accurate. Then ask them to do the same about yours. Most couples discover that both lists are long, both are real, and neither one is aimed at what the other person is actually short of.

Do not confuse this with excusing a genuine absence. Somebody who provides nothing, in any category, is a different situation. [The one-sided version, where one person carries the entire relationship and keeps a private tally of it, is covered here](/patterns/why-am-i-always-the-one-who-cares-more/), and reading it first is worth the twenty minutes if the ledger in your head has been running for years.

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because at least six different situations produce the sentence my partner will not work on themselves, and they need completely different responses.

| What it looks like | What it might actually be | The thing that tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Refuses therapy, books, and growth conversations | A refusal of your formats, with change still available | Whether anything has moved for them in two years, in any area |
| Refuses everything, including ordinary closeness | A pattern of distance that predates you | Whether they can name any feeling, to anyone, ever |
| Agrees to everything, attends everything, changes nothing | Compliance without a decision | What happens six weeks after the last session |
| Says they are trying and it does not register | Real effort aimed at the wrong category | Itemizing their year from their position, honestly |
| Shuts down only when you raise the relationship | Flooding, and a conversation that always becomes a verdict | Whether the same shutdown happens with other subjects |
| Withdrew from a specific point in time | Something that happened, unrepaired | Whether you can both date it to a month |
| Refuses, and you are also afraid of the reaction | Coercion, which is a different category entirely | Whether you edit your behavior to prevent a response |

Row seven points off this page and belongs with a professional. Row two belongs on [the distancing page](/patterns/emotionally-unavailable/). Row six often belongs with [the work of repairing a specific breach](/patterns/how-to-rebuild-trust/). The rest are the ordinary territory of this one, and most people find themselves in two rows at once.

There is a seventh possibility nobody enjoys examining. Sometimes the person described as refusing to work on themselves is being asked to fix something that would be better addressed as a shared problem, by somebody who has quietly cast themselves as the well one in the relationship. [The reflex to improve the people you love has its own page](/patterns/why-do-i-always-try-to-fix-everyone/), and it is the one to read if the sentence you always end up saying is a version of I only want what is best for you.

## What Does Asking Instead of Telling Actually Sound Like?

Like saying one true thing and then handing the conclusion back rather than landing it.

The structure is small and it changes what the other person's body does. State your own observation or experience, in one sentence, about yourself. Then stop, and ask them what they make of it. The ask has to be genuine, which means you have to be willing to receive an answer you did not write in advance. Anything else is a question with a verdict hidden inside it, and people detect that instantly.

| What gets said | Telling | Asking |
|---|---|---|
| About their withdrawal | "You always shut down when I bring anything up." | "I noticed I stopped talking last night. What was happening on your side?" |
| About therapy | "You need to see someone." | "I've been thinking about going to see someone. What do you think would actually help us?" |
| About a book | "Read this, it explains exactly what you do." | "This bit reminded me of me. Does any of it sound familiar to you?" |
| About the distance | "You've checked out of this marriage." | "I've been missing you. What would a good week between us look like to you?" |
| About a pattern | "You do this in every relationship you've had." | "Has this shown up for you before, with anyone else?" |
| About your own change | "I've done all this work and you've done nothing." | "This has been useful for me. I'm not asking you to do it." |

The right-hand column is harder than it looks, for one specific reason. Each of those questions can be delivered with the answer already loaded, in which case it functions exactly like the left-hand column and produces the same wall. A genuine question has a silence after it, and the silence has to be long enough to be uncomfortable.

This move is borrowed rather than invented. It comes out of a counseling method built on the finding that people are persuaded far more effectively by hearing their own reasons than by hearing yours, however good yours are. Two adjustments make it usable at home. Do not run it as a technique on your spouse, who will notice. And accept that the first honest answer you get back may be something you did not want, which is the price of having actually asked.

## Does the Shutdown Belong to Your Relationship, or Did It Arrive With Them?

Run the check, because the answer changes whose work this is.

One question does most of it, asked about both of you: has this move shown up before, with other people, at roughly the same point? A partner who has withdrawn at the moment of deepening closeness in every serious relationship they have ever had is running something portable. It traveled in with them, it would have shown up with anybody, and it is theirs to examine rather than yours to have caused.

That relocation does two useful things at once. It frees you from reading their withdrawal as a verdict on you, which is exhausting and inaccurate. And it hands them a problem they can actually work on, which is a considerable improvement on being the permanent defendant in a case they keep losing.

Two cautions. Run it on yourself first, and answer honestly, because the pursuing half of this is often just as portable as the withdrawing half. And portable does not mean permanent. [The fuller version of this check, and what to do with the answer, sits on the recurring-conflict page](/patterns/why-do-we-keep-having-the-same-fight/).

## Does Your Own Visible Growth Help or Make It Worse?

Both, and which one it does depends almost entirely on whether it is being offered or held up.

Take a composite that turns up constantly. One partner has spent years developing herself, has real language for her interior, and can describe the relationship's dynamics accurately. He will not come to any of it. Every attempt to draw him in reads to him as being coached by someone who has appointed herself the expert on him, so he disengages, and his disengagement reads to her as more proof that she has to try harder. Her effort and his resistance are the same machine, running faster each year.

What moved it was not a better argument. It was noticing out loud that her attempts to get him to participate had quietly become organized around being needed and being right rather than around connection. Once that was said, his attendance stopped being the measure, and a different question became available: what would actually make him feel loved, in his categories rather than hers. That is a question you can ask on a Tuesday.

So the practical rule. Growth offered without comparison sometimes opens a door. My week has been better since I started doing this, said once, with nothing attached, asks for nothing and threatens nothing. Growth held up as an implicit correction closes the door harder each time. Look, I have changed, and you have not, is a statement about them wearing a statement about yourself, and everybody in earshot can hear which one it is.

The tell is in what you do next. If you are watching for their reaction, it was aimed at them.

## What Does It Sound Like When Something Finally Moves?

Less dramatic than you would expect, and almost always in the person's own plain words rather than in any vocabulary you gave them.

- "It's not that the marriage isn't gonna work, and it won't if she changes. It will work, but it's not anything she can do. It's something I have to do."
- "I said, you know, I may never know what it is. It just doesn't matter anymore."
- "I remember when you said that. I've always done the best that I could, and he's always done the best he could do, but sometimes our best was just different. That was all. One wasn't right and one wasn't wrong, it was just different."
- "I was just treating him like he was this person in a war and I had to do everything myself, but no, he's a strong bloke."
- "I was very low on the list of priority, even though I would never admit it. But now it's completely switched around. So I just think it's amazing. We have a winner."

Read the first one slowly, because it is the whole page in three sentences. A man locating the only part of this that he can actually move, and stating plainly that his wife's changing was never the mechanism. Nobody argued him into that. It arrived as a decision, on his own terms, after years of being pushed and years of watching somebody model change in front of him, neither of which produced it directly and both of which were probably part of the ground it grew in.

The second one dismantles the most expensive assumption in this whole subject. Understanding is treated as the necessary first step, so couples spend years excavating why he is like this, mistaking the excavation for progress. The shift, when it comes, is frequently a decision that the reason no longer matters. If your partner cannot explain themselves, that is a fact about self-knowledge and carries no information at all about capacity.

The third and fourth are the same discovery from two angles, and they are the ones that end the tallying. Two people doing their genuine best in different, colliding forms, with neither version wrong. And a person who had been carrying the entire relationship on the assumption that her partner could not be relied on, discovering that the assumption was hers rather than his.

The fifth is worth including because of how ordinary it sounds. Somebody reporting that their place in another person's priorities had visibly changed, in a tone closer to relief than to triumph. That is what a result in this territory usually sounds like.

## Why Does a Breakthrough Fade So Fast?

Because a peak and an installed change are different events, and only one of them survives a normal week.

One honest hour genuinely changes something. It changes state: the softening, the eye contact, the sense that the person you married is in the room again. State is real and state is temporary. It resets within days, and by the second week the ordinary conditions have reasserted themselves and both people conclude the session meant nothing. It meant something. Nothing was built to hold it.

What holds a peak is almost embarrassingly small and specific. One repeated thing, chosen by the person themselves, that now happens on ordinary days. Fifteen minutes on a Wednesday with no agenda. One question asked at dinner and actually answered. A walk on Sunday that neither person cancels. The particular content matters far less than the repetition and the ownership, and a practice they invented will outlast a practice you assigned by an enormous margin.

This is also why expecting the one attended session to be the whole intervention sets up a predictable letdown. The session surfaces the material. Whether anything changes is decided by the fourteen ordinary days afterward, on the Tuesday when nothing feels significant and the practice is the last thing anybody wants to do. [What integration actually involves is worth reading separately](/insights/what-is-integration/), and so is [why understanding a pattern so rarely changes it](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/).

## What Actually Moves a Partner Who Will Not Engage?

A change in delivery, a change in target, and a lot of patience with a timeline you do not control.

Nothing below is a validated protocol, and anybody offering you one for a specific marriage is overselling something. What follows is built from how the loop runs, and it is designed to be done by one person, without the other person's agreement.

1. **Say it once, then stop.** One clear sentence, about your own experience, at a neutral hour when nothing has just gone wrong. Then weeks of ordinary behavior. Repetition converts a real request into weather, and weather gets waited out.
2. **Ask for the need, not the activity.** Therapy, books, and retreats are your formats. Underneath each one sits something specific you are short of. Name that instead, in plain words: I miss being asked about my day. I want to be wanted rather than accommodated. The format is negotiable. The need is the actual subject.
3. **Hand the conclusion back.** State the observation, then ask what they make of it, and let the silence run long enough to be uncomfortable. A question with the answer already loaded produces the same wall a statement does.
4. **Drop the correction entirely for one month.** No articles, no suggestions, no mid-argument diagnoses. This is harder than it sounds and it is the single most informative experiment available to you, because it tells you what the relationship does with no pressure in it.
5. **Separate the pattern from the person, out loud.** There is an enormous difference between you shut down and something shuts down in you when we get close. The second one is describable, workable, and does not require anybody to agree they are defective.
6. **Stop auditing what you are not getting, for one week.** A private ledger of who has read more and attended more organizes both your giving and your withholding around scorekeeping, and the account never balances. One week without it is a strange and useful experience.
7. **Ask what they are like outside the fight.** If you cannot list three things, that absence is the finding. Describing somebody entirely through their worst five minutes removes the version you actually chose, and they can feel that description in the room whether or not you ever say it.
8. **Go on your own.** Therapy, a group, a seminar, a practice, whatever the format is. It requires nobody's permission, it changes half of a two-person machine, and it removes the most common accusation available to a resistant partner, which is that this was always about fixing them.
9. **Anchor anything that does move.** After a good conversation, agree on one small repeated thing before the state fades. Let them choose it. Let it be smaller than you want.
10. **Let the decision be theirs.** This is the hardest one and the whole page depends on it. You can lower resistance, name the need, and make room. You cannot install a decision, and continuing to try is what keeps the loop supplied.

Is any of that comfortable? No. Point four in particular tends to produce a genuinely awful first month, in which nothing happens and you conclude you have given up on your own marriage. It is small, repetitive, and freaking unglamorous, which is exactly why it holds when a single overdue conversation does not.

## What Do You Owe Yourself If Nothing Moves?

An honest answer to a different question, and a date on which you will give it.

Some partners do not change within the timeframe of the relationship. That outcome is real and this page will not paper over it. What changes at that point is the subject: from how do I move them to what do I need, regardless. Those are separate questions and the second one has been waiting a long time in most cases.

Three things make the difference between waiting and drifting. Pick a review point, six months or a year out, and write down privately what you would need to have seen by then. Keep it specific and modest, because the version people write while hurt tends to be neither. And in the meantime, build the parts of your life that do not require their participation: the practice, the friendships, the work that means something, the room of your own. None of that is a consolation prize. It is the thing that makes the eventual decision a choice rather than a collapse.

Two pages carry the rest of this. [Setting a limit you can actually hold](/patterns/how-to-set-boundaries/) is the one to read if what you need is a change in what you tolerate rather than a change in them. And [the question of whether to stay at all](/patterns/should-i-leave-my-relationship/) is a different question with a different structure, and it is worth reading before you make any decision you cannot undo. If what is accumulating instead is a slow, quiet bitterness, [that has its own page too](/patterns/resentment/).

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Months of low-pressure consistency where the pattern is old, and sometimes it never happens at all.

Two speeds are worth separating. The delivery change is fast. Most people can move from telling to asking within a few weeks of paying deliberate attention, and catching yourself mid-correction counts as progress even on the days you finish the sentence anyway. Hearing your own voice say you need to work on yourself, in almost the exact words you used a year ago, and stopping halfway through, is a real step and it feels like nothing.

Their side is slower and it is not on your schedule. A withdrawal that predates the relationship has decades of practice behind it and does not reorganize because somebody found a better way to ask. What a better way to ask does is remove the pressure that was holding it in place, which is a necessary condition rather than a sufficient one. Expect months. Expect a period where the house is quieter and nothing appears to be happening, which is the most common point at which people conclude the approach failed.

Expect relapse under load, too. Illness, a job loss, a bereavement, a bad year at work, small children. The old sequence stays installed in both of you and re-runs at full speed under stress. Progress shows up as how fast you notice and how quickly it settles rather than as its absence.

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. The thing I had wrong longest was the target. I kept trying to get somebody to see what I could see, and the accuracy of what I could see felt like it should be the deciding factor. It never was. What moved things was saying one true sentence and then genuinely shutting up long enough for an answer I had not written.

Is it frustrating that reading a page like this changes very little on its own? Yes. A month of dropping the correction entirely, in a real house, with the actual person, changes a great deal.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From couples and behavior-change research that has been public for decades, and four strands carry most of the weight here.

Andrew Christensen and Christopher Heavey named and measured the demand and withdraw pattern in a 1990 paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, and it has been replicated widely since, including across cultures and in both directions of gender. Christensen went on to develop an approach to couples work built around acceptance of durable differences alongside change, and set it out for general readers in *Reconcilable Differences*, first published in 2000. That book is the single most useful thing to read next if the loop described in the middle of this page felt familiar.

William Miller and Stephen Rollnick built Motivational Interviewing around a finding that arrived from clinical work with problem drinkers: that arguing somebody toward a change reliably produces the counter-argument, spoken out loud, by them. Miller described the approach in a 1983 paper and the book with Rollnick followed in 1991, now in later editions. The righting reflex is their term for the urge to fix somebody with accurate information. Their method is designed for trained practitioners rather than for spouses, which is worth respecting, and the underlying principle about eliciting rather than telling travels intact.

James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente developed the stages model out of studying people who quit smoking without any professional help, publishing the framework in the early 1980s and a general-audience version, *Changing for Good*, in 1994. The model has real critics in the research literature who argue the stages describe change rather than causing it, and that criticism deserves stating plainly. Used as a locator rather than as a mechanism, it explains something this page needs, which is why the identical request lands completely differently depending on where somebody already stands.

John Gottman, working from the 1970s onward in an observational laboratory that everybody ended up calling the Love Lab, recorded couples having ordinary disagreements and coded them second by second. Flooding and stonewalling come out of that program, along with the four horsemen and the finding that contempt is the strongest single predictor of a relationship ending. His widely read general-audience book is *The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work*, written with Nan Silver and published in 1999. Some of the prediction-accuracy figures from that body of work have been criticized as retrospective rather than genuinely predictive, which is a fair criticism and does not touch the descriptive findings this page uses.

One more is worth naming because it addresses this exact situation. Robert Meyers developed Community Reinforcement and Family Training for the relatives of people who refuse help for a drinking or drug problem, and wrote it up for general readers with Brenda Wolfe as *Get Your Loved One Sober* in 2004. It works entirely through the family member, teaching them to change their own responses, and trials found it brought reluctant people into help substantially more often than the confrontational family intervention it was built to replace. The situation it addresses is narrower than this page, and the principle it demonstrates is exactly the one here: the person who is present can change the conditions, and the decision still belongs to the other one.

The rest is mine and carries no research weight at all: the five years and five thousand hours described above. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind and I am not a couples therapist. None of the researchers named here has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, go and check theirs.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Bring It Up Again?

Write two lists, tonight, privately, and show them to nobody.

On the first, write down every way your partner has put effort into this relationship in the last twelve months, from their position rather than yours. Hours worked. Money earned and handed over. A rule kept. A temper managed. A thing they stopped doing after you asked once, three years ago, and never restarted. Write it even where it does not feel like the thing you are missing, especially then. Most people find this list is longer than they expected and considerably harder to write than the complaint is.

On the second, write the specific thing you are actually short of, in one sentence, with no activity attached to it. Not he should go to therapy. The thing underneath it: I want to be asked about my day and I want the answer to matter. Then read the two lists side by side and notice whether his effort and your need are aimed anywhere near each other. In most houses they are not, and nobody has ever said so out loud, because the argument has always been about therapy instead. That costs you twenty minutes and nothing else, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever go anywhere or say a word about it.
