# Why do I promise more than I can actually deliver?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because the promise does two jobs at once and only one of them concerns the work. It can report real warmth accurately while reporting your week badly, spare somebody a disappointment you cannot watch, protect a picture of yourself the smaller number would dent, forecast for a version of you who never turns up, or end a conversation. All five produce the identical Friday.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
People recognize this in one specific sentence rather than in the general description, so start with these.

- "I said Friday. I knew it was the Wednesday after. Nobody asked for Friday."
- "I meant it completely at the time. By Tuesday I was looking for a way out of it."
- "I deliver about sixty percent of what I say, and I have never once been lying."
- "I heard the offer getting bigger while I was still making it and I did not stop it."

Here is the moment this page is about, and it is much smaller than the consequences that follow it. You are in a conversation. Somebody needs something. You have a rough sense of what it would take, and that sense is reasonably accurate, because you have done this kind of thing before. Then you open your mouth and a date comes out that is earlier than the one you were holding. Or a scope comes out that is larger. Or both. You said Friday. You were holding the Wednesday after. Nobody in the room said Friday. Friday came from you, in about half a second, and it came out sounding completely sincere, because it was.

That half second is the whole subject here. Not the missed deadline, not the apology, not the slow drift of a project that ended up at sixty percent. Those are all downstream and all of them have been written about extensively. What almost nobody looks at is the interval in which the size of a commitment goes up while it is being spoken, before any deliberate estimate has had time to arrive, in a person who has no intention whatsoever of misleading anybody.

There is a warmer version of the same interval and it is worth putting next to the first one, because they look unrelated and they are the same machine. You are at a dinner, or on a call that has gone well, and somebody mentions something they are struggling with. The warmth you feel is real, and it has no performance in it, no strategy behind it, and no habit of accommodation driving it. What it holds is a genuine wish to be useful to a specific person you like. And out of that entirely honest feeling comes an offer with a whole weekend in it. On the Tuesday that follows, the weekend arrives in a week that already has three other things in it, and the feeling that generated the offer is nowhere in the room.

Then there is the shortest version. Somebody raises something you do not want to discuss right now, and you say leave it with me, and the conversation closes. It closes immediately, which is the point. There was no plan behind the sentence and there was not meant to be one. The sentence was a door.

Before going further it helps to draw four boundaries, because several different behaviors get typed into a search bar in almost identical words and they lead to genuinely different places. If the pattern is agreeing to a request you did not want in the first place, [the word that leaves before you have decided anything is covered here](/patterns/saying-yes-when-you-mean-no/). If it is the unprompted offer of labor, the hand that goes out before anybody has asked for anything, [that has its own page and its own five roots](/patterns/volunteering-for-everything/). If your commitments are the right size and simply arrive late, every time, regardless of who asked, [the estimation machinery is the thing to read](/patterns/always-running-late/). And if the work reliably gets to the edge of finished and stops, [the last stretch has a page of its own](/patterns/abandoning-things-at-ninety-percent/). This page stays with one narrow thing, which is the moment of the promise and what happens to its size while you are making it.

## Why Does the Sentence Get Bigger While You Are Still Saying It?

Because it is being scored live, and a face is faster feedback than a calendar.

Slow the half second down, because everything else follows from what happens inside it. You begin with something modest and hedged, because the modest hedged version is what your actual estimate produced. I could probably take a look at that. Then, in the fraction of a second before the sentence ends, you watch it land. You see the small recalibration in the other person's expression, the polite acceptance of something less than they hoped for, the tiny drop. And you improve the offer before the sentence is finished. I could probably take a look at that, actually I can just do the whole thing, I will get it to you Friday.

Nothing dishonest happened there. What happened is that a second source of information arrived mid-sentence and it was far more vivid than the first. Your estimate came from memory, which is slow, dry and unavailable in the moment. Their face came from the room, at full resolution, in real time. When two signals compete for a decision that has to be made inside a second, the loud one wins, and the loud one is always the face.

Watch what the upgrade buys. It buys their expression changing back. That change is the entire transaction, it lands instantly, and it costs you nothing at the moment of purchase. The bill is issued later, in a different week, to a version of you who was not present for the negotiation and has no memory of agreeing to the terms. Two events that far apart do not connect in ordinary experience, which is precisely why intelligent people run this for twenty years and describe it, accurately, as being someone who likes to help.

There is one more feature of the half second worth naming, because people recognize it with a jolt. You are good at it. The upward revision is smooth. There is no stammer, no visible hesitation, no tell. Fluency of that quality comes from several thousand repetitions rather than from a decision, which means this started long ago, probably somewhere else, probably with different people in the room.

## What Is the Name for This, and How Much of Your Case Does It Cover?

There are four or five separate vocabularies for it, and each one describes the act with real precision while leaving your particular reason completely untouched.

The broadest is **impression management**, the study of how people shape the way others see them. Schlenker and Weigold set out the framework in 1992 and treated it as ordinary social equipment rather than as a form of deception, which is the right starting point. A promise is one of its sharpest instruments, because it makes a public claim about your capability before any work exists to support the claim. Schlenker, Britt, Pennington, Murphy and Doherty proposed the triangle model of responsibility in 1994, which explains why a broken promise feels so much heavier than its size: responsibility rises when a clear rule applies, when the event is connected to you, and when your identity is engaged. A promise constructs all three in a single sentence, and you built each one yourself.

The second vocabulary is the **intention-behavior gap**, the measured distance between what people sincerely intend and what they later do. Rhodes and de Bruijn reviewed the physical activity literature in 2013 and found the gap runs in one direction with striking consistency. Roughly half of the people who genuinely intended to act failed to follow through, while people who intended not to act almost always managed it. Intending is a poor predictor of doing and intending not to is an excellent predictor of not doing. That asymmetry sits exactly on the side of the promise.

The third is the mechanism underneath the second, and it is the single most useful finding on this page. Koehler and Poon reported in 2006 that when people predict their own future behavior, they weight the strength of their current intention heavily and the situations that will actually govern the behavior far too lightly. The forecast is built from how much you want to, right now, in this room. That is why warmth inflates a promise. Warmth raises the intention, the intention drives the forecast, and nothing in the moment corrects for the Tuesday.

The fourth vocabulary describes what happens after the number is public. Staw demonstrated in 1976 that people commit further to a course they have already chosen out loud, especially once evidence starts arriving that it was a poor choice, and Brockner reviewed two decades of that work in 1992. Applied to a personal promise this explains a specific and very familiar failure: the honest revision gets postponed week after week, because saying a smaller number on purpose feels worse than delivering late by accident, right up until the deadline settles the matter without you.

The fifth is the one that matters most for the argument here. Flyvbjerg, studying large projects that overran their forecasts by wide margins, drew a distinction between **optimism bias** and **strategic misrepresentation**. In the first, the person genuinely believed the number. In the second, the number was shaped to get a yes. The overrun looks identical from outside in both cases, and only the forecaster can tell which one produced it. That distinction transfers to a Friday promised at a kitchen table with almost no modification at all, and the rest of this page is built on it.

And there is the label most people reach for first. The **planning fallacy** names the tendency to underestimate how long your own tasks will take, and it is a real and well-replicated effect. It explains one of the five roots below cleanly and the other four badly. If your commitments are correctly sized and simply arrive late no matter who asked or how you felt about them, [the estimation machinery has its own page and the arithmetic there is the right tool](/patterns/always-running-late/).

Two honest cautions, because both of these circulate in stronger forms than the evidence supports. First, the popular version of the planning fallacy holds that people always underestimate task duration. Halkjelsvik and Jorgensen reviewed the literature in 2012 and found that underestimation is much less uniform than the popular version claims. It varies heavily by task type, and for short tasks people frequently overestimate instead. Treating every over-promise as an arithmetic failure is therefore a poor bet. Second, the field genuinely disagrees about why promises bind us at all. Charness and Dufwenberg argued in 2006 that people keep promises to avoid the guilt of failing an expectation they created, while Vanberg reported evidence in 2008 favoring a direct preference for keeping one's word. Both accounts predict the same behavior nearly all the time. Neither has settled it, and any page that tells you the answer is overstating what is known.

Here is what all of that vocabulary genuinely buys you. It establishes that this is measured, documented and extremely common, which takes the character question off the table early. It tells you the mechanism, which is that your forecast is built from present intention rather than from future conditions. It gives you a way to talk about a broken promise as a forecasting event rather than as a verdict on your reliability. And here is where every one of those terms stops. They name an act. Two people can say the identical Friday in identical circumstances and be doing entirely different things, one reaching honestly for something ambitious, one buying their way out of a face falling, one protecting a self-description that a smaller number would puncture. The vocabulary covers the what with real precision. The why sits outside all of it, and the why decides what would actually help.

## When Does an Over-Promise Need More Than a Page Like This?

Earlier than most people ask, and the line is drawn by what surrounds the promising rather than by how much of it there is.

This page assumes two things about your situation and both are worth stating plainly, because everything after them depends on them. It assumes the over-promising is costing you something real, and it assumes you are safe.

Read the next paragraph even if you are certain none of it applies. If the promises you make are to somebody whose reaction to a smaller number would change the temperature of the house for a week. If you have ever committed to something not because you wanted to but because you did not know what refusing would set off. If your income, your housing or your access to your own money depends on continuing to say yes to a specific person. In any of those cases the promising is doing safety work rather than social work, and the two look identical from outside while needing completely different responses. Services for exactly that situation exist in most countries, many of them free and confidential, and the people staffing them speak with far more callers who are unsure than callers who are certain.

Two more that hide inside this pattern. If the commitments are financial, and you are promising repayments, investments or contributions at a scale that would change decisions somebody else is currently making, that has stopped being a habit and become a structural matter that needs a professional with a calculator. If the promising happens mostly during stretches where you are sleeping very little, spending unusually, and feel able to take on considerably more than usual, and those stretches are followed by long flat periods, that pattern belongs with a licensed professional rather than with anything written here, and it is worth raising even if you are unsure.

And if what accompanies this includes a flatness that has not lifted for months, sleep that has been broken for a long stretch, or any thought of harming yourself, that belongs with a licensed professional now rather than later. Reaching for help early costs less than reaching for it after a year of decline.

The rest of this is written for the ordinary version, where everything is basically fine, everybody likes you, and you have been quietly failing to deliver about forty percent of what you say for a very long time.

## Was That Promise Optimistic, or Was It Buying Something?

One test does nearly all the work here, it takes about four seconds, and you can run it without telling anybody you ran it.

**Try to make the promise smaller. An optimistic promise gets smaller and nothing happens. A purchasing promise will not go smaller, because shrinking it hands back the thing the promise was actually for.**

That is the whole test. Take one live commitment, the most ordinary one you have, and in your head reduce it. Move the date out by a week. Cut the scope in half. Do not say anything to anybody. Just build the smaller version and notice what rises while you build it.

If what rises is logistics, you are in an optimistic promise. The revision feels mildly annoying, you start thinking about how to word it, you wonder whether they will mind, and the answer you arrive at is probably not much. That promise was an estimate, estimates revise, and the fix is arithmetic.

If what rises is dread, something else is going on. Not dread of the work, which you would have felt anyway. Dread of the sentence. A specific reluctance to be the person who says the smaller number to that particular face. When you cannot shrink a promise without losing something, the promise was buying that something, and the delivery was only ever the receipt.

The distinction matters more than it looks, because the two kinds need opposite treatment. An optimistic promise needs better arithmetic and gets it easily. A purchasing promise is completely immune to better arithmetic, because the number was never produced by arithmetic in the first place. Improving your estimating skills when the problem is purchasing is like sharpening a knife to fix a leak.

| | The promise was optimistic | The promise was buying something |
|---|---|---|
| Where the number came from | A rough estimate, made quickly | The other person's expression |
| Try shrinking it in your head | It shrinks. You start drafting the message | It will not shrink. You feel the face fall in advance |
| What you felt as you said it | Mild confidence, some enthusiasm | A small release, like a held breath going out |
| Did the size go up mid-sentence? | No. You said what you thought | Yes, and you heard it happen |
| Who set the date | You did, from the work | You did, from the room |
| What fixes it | Writing estimates down before speaking | Finding out what the promise was for |
| How it feels on Tuesday | Annoying. Too much to do | Dread, disproportionate to the task |

Read the last row twice, because it is the cleanest signal on the page and it costs nothing to observe. Tuesday dread that is proportionate to the workload is ordinary overcommitment. Tuesday dread that is wildly out of scale with a four-hour task is telling you that the four hours are not what you are dreading.

One more thing about the test, which keeps it honest. It does not ask you to break anything or tell anybody anything. It asks you to notice what happens when you build a smaller version privately. Many people run it and find plain logistics on every single commitment, and the correct conclusion in that case is that they have an estimating problem rather than the pattern described here.

## Why Is a Promise the Cheapest Thing You Own at the Moment You Make It?

Because it is the only currency in the room that costs nothing to spend and settles instantly.

Consider what else is available to you in that half second. You could say no, which costs a visible disappointment right now. You could name the true number, which costs a smaller disappointment right now plus whatever the smaller number seems to say about you. You could ask for time to check, which costs a small awkward pause and makes you look less decisive than you would like. Or you could promise something larger than you can do, which costs precisely nothing in the room, produces immediate visible relief in another person, and settles the conversation on the spot.

Every behavior that becomes automatic has that profile. The reward is immediate, reliable and requires no cooperation from anybody. The cost is delayed, uncertain in size and lands partly on somebody else. A system that learns from consequences will pick the promise every time, and it will pick it faster each year, which is why fluency in this grows rather than fades.

There is a piece worth stating directly, because it does most of the work in the roots below. A promise whose outcome sits less inside your control is the weaker promise to make, and it is weaker in exact proportion to how much of the delivery depends on things you do not run. Most inflated promises fail this quietly. The date you named assumed a clear week you do not control, a client who responds when you need them, a body that does not get sick, and a version of your attention that shows up on demand. The promise sounded like a statement about your effort. Most of what it actually committed to was outside your effort entirely.

That single criterion, checked before the sentence rather than after, catches a surprising proportion of these before they happen. Not by making you promise less. By making you promise the part you actually run.

## Does Under-Promising Have Costs of Its Own?

Yes, real ones, and any page that skips them is selling you a character upgrade rather than describing a mechanism.

The advice you will find everywhere is to promise less and deliver more. As a technique for a specific conversation, that is often correct. As a way of being, it has a bill attached to it that nobody itemizes.

Start with what systematically low estimates do to what you attempt. Every difficult thing anybody has ever built started as a number that looked unreasonable to the person saying it. Ambitious commitments are how hard work gets started, how new capability gets discovered, and how anybody finds out that they can in fact do the thing in five weeks rather than eleven. A person who has trained themselves to name only numbers they are certain of has also trained themselves out of the whole category of attempts that would have stretched them. That is a real loss and it does not show up on any list of broken promises.

Then there is what happens to how you are used. Reliability that is purchased entirely through modesty produces a specific and slightly sad outcome, which is that people stop bringing you the ambitious thing. They still like you. They route the interesting work elsewhere, because they have learned what you will say. Nobody announces this and most people notice it years late.

The research is not simply on the side of low expectations either. Norem and Cantor described defensive pessimism in 1986, a strategy in which people deliberately set low expectations and mentally rehearse what could go wrong. It works genuinely well for some people, improving their performance rather than merely protecting their feelings. It also actively harms others, particularly people who normally run optimistic, whose performance drops when they are pushed into the pessimistic mode. Lowering your stated expectations is a strategy with a cost curve rather than a virtue with a slope.

So the recommendation here is narrower and more useful than promise less. Keep the ambitious promises that are genuinely reaching for something, and make them properly, with the ambition stated as ambition. Stop making the ones that are buying you out of an uncomfortable second. The first kind sometimes fails and the failure teaches you your actual capacity. The second kind fails in a way that teaches you nothing, because the number was never a hypothesis about the work.

That is the honest frame for the whole page. All you can really do is test the hypothesis whether it is going to work, and it would be humorous to think that you can just make a plan that is going to work on the first strike. Life rarely works that way. An ambitious estimate that misses is a hypothesis returning data. An inflated estimate that misses returns nothing at all, because it was never a hypothesis about the task.

## Why Does the Same Inflated Promise Come From Five Different Places?

Because a spoken commitment carries no information about what produced it, and five very different needs all resolve into the same date.

This is the limitation that makes advice about over-promising so unreliable in general. Someone watching you say Friday has exactly one observation available: the word Friday. They cannot see whether it came from a rough estimate, from a wish not to watch a face fall, from a self-description you are maintaining, from a genuine misread of next week, or from wanting the conversation to end. Neither can you, most of the time, because the production happened faster than introspection.

| Where it comes from | What the extra size buys you in the moment | The evidence in your own week |
|---|---|---|
| The warmth was real | An honest expression of how much you want to help | It happens most with people you like and least with strangers |
| Their disappointment is unbearable | The face not falling, immediately | The dread on Tuesday is far bigger than the task |
| You are the person who can | A self-description staying intact | The smaller number feels like an admission about you |
| The forecast was genuinely wrong | Nothing. You believed it | It happens with strangers too, and the size is right, the date is not |
| The promise ended the conversation | An exit from a discussion you wanted out of | You cannot recall what you promised, only that it closed |

Only one of those five is a forecasting problem. That is the whole reason a general policy of being more realistic works so badly. It aims at the number, which is identical in all five cases, rather than at what the number was for, which differs in every one.

Notice too that the last column is available to you tonight without a conversation with anybody. Nothing there requires disclosure, apology, or a decision. It requires reading your own recent week honestly for about ten minutes.

## What If You Meant Every Word of It?

Then the promise was an accurate report of your feeling and a poor report of your week, and both of those things can be true at once.

This is the root people are most relieved to find and most surprised by, because from the inside it does not feel like a pattern at all. It feels like caring about somebody. Somebody you like is in difficulty. You want to help. The wanting is genuine, unmixed, and the best thing about you. And the offer that comes out of it is sized by the wanting.

Koehler and Poon's 2006 finding is the research version of exactly this. Self-predictions get built from the strength of the current intention, with the situational factors that will actually determine the outcome weighted far too lightly. Your intention at that dinner table was extremely strong. It was also, as a forecast, almost information-free about a Tuesday in three weeks with a deadline in it.

There is a specific tell for this root and it is clean. The size of your promises tracks your affection for the person rather than the difficulty of the task. You promise more to friends than to clients, more at the end of a good conversation than at the start of one, more to somebody visibly struggling than to somebody managing fine. If you laid out your last dozen commitments and sorted them by how much you liked the person, the sizes would sort with them.

What helps here is smaller than it sounds and it does not require caring less. It requires putting a gap between the feeling and the number. The feeling is accurate and worth expressing immediately. The number is a separate object and should be produced somewhere the calendar is visible. Say the warm thing in the warm room, in full, with no hedging at all. I would really like to help with this. Then say the number tomorrow. Those two sentences have almost always been fused in your experience, and they come apart more easily than you would expect.

## What If You Cannot Watch Their Face Change?

Then the promise is moving a cost from now to later, and later is where it becomes somebody else's problem as much as yours.

This root has a physical quality that people recognize instantly. There is a moment in a conversation where you can see a disappointment about to happen. It has not happened yet. You have perhaps a second in which you can prevent it, and preventing it is trivially easy, because all it takes is a bigger number. So you say the bigger number and you watch the disappointment not arrive, and something in you settles.

What actually happened is a transfer. The disappointment was not removed, it was scheduled. It will now arrive in three weeks, larger, attached to a failure rather than to a limit, and it will arrive in a week where somebody else has already planned around your number. A disappointment absorbed now is one conversation. A disappointment delivered late is one conversation plus a wasted plan plus a revision to how reliable you are considered to be.

The tell is that the dread is wildly out of proportion. A four-hour task should not produce a Sunday evening of low-grade avoidance. When it does, what you are dreading has very little to do with four hours of work. What you are dreading is the moment where they find out.

There is something worth knowing about what actually happens on the other side, because the imagined version is much worse than the real one. People calibrate reliability against what was said, not against some absolute standard of helpfulness. A modest promise delivered on time reads as competence. An ambitious one delivered at sixty percent reads as a pattern. Almost everybody who tries naming a smaller number for the first time reports the same thing afterward, which is that nothing happened. The face did not fall. They said fine, and moved on, and the entire cost you had been paying to avoid was imaginary.

If the difficulty is specifically that a refusal or a limit feels dangerous rather than merely uncomfortable, [the avoidance of the conversation itself is the more useful subject](/patterns/why-do-i-avoid-conflict/), and if the whole pattern only ever appears in response to what other people want, [people pleasing is the wider frame](/patterns/how-to-stop-people-pleasing/).

## What If the Honest Number Would Say Something About You?

Then the difficulty was never arithmetic, and no amount of better estimating will touch it.

Run this one from the inside. You know the task will take three weeks. Saying three weeks is mechanically easy, it costs one sentence. But the sentence does not arrive alone. It seems to carry a second statement you did not want to make, something about your speed, your capacity, how full your week already is, or whether this is straightforward for somebody like you. The bigger number says the work is easy for you. The smaller one seems to concede that it is not.

Schlenker's framing is exactly right here. A promise is an identity claim made in advance of any evidence, and the triangle model explains why breaking it lands so heavily. You created the rule, you attached yourself to the outcome, and you engaged your identity in the same sentence. That is a large amount of construction for one date.

The specific tell for this root is that the difficulty concentrates in rooms where you are being assessed rather than in rooms where you are liked. It shows up at work more than at home, with new people more than old ones, and in front of anybody whose opinion of your competence you are aware of holding. If the inflation disappears entirely with your oldest friends and appears reliably with a new client, the promise is doing identity work.

This is also the root where the standard advice does real damage. Telling somebody in this position to simply be more honest about their capacity is asking them to make the exact statement the whole pattern exists to avoid, with no preparation, on the strength of a general principle. That is not a small ask and it should not be made casually. What tends to move it is narrower: one smaller number, said once, to somebody safe, chosen for being low-stakes rather than for being important. The point here has very little to do with being more honest in general. The point is to obtain one piece of evidence that a smaller number does not produce the reaction you have been assuming, which is information you cannot get any other way. If worth in your case has to be re-earned by delivery every single time, [that arrangement has its own page and its own origins](/patterns/conditional-self-worth/).

## What If You Were Forecasting for a Version of Yourself Who Never Turns Up?

Then you have the one root that is genuinely a math problem, and it is the most tractable of the five.

Here the promise is not buying anything at all. Nobody's face was involved. You made an estimate, you believed it completely, and it was wrong. The version of you who does the work in your forecast is a person with a clear week, full attention, no interruptions and no competing obligations. That person exists occasionally. They do not exist on the specific Tuesday you committed to.

Zauberman and Lynch reported in 2005 that people expect to have considerably more spare time in the future than spare money, and commit time far more freely as a result. Next month reads as empty in a way that next month never turns out to be. That single asymmetry explains a great deal of ordinary over-committing, and it explains why a date six weeks out attracts a promise that the identical task next Tuesday would be refused.

Bazerman and Tenbrunsel described a related split between the self that decides and the self that acts, which are answering different questions under different conditions and rarely consult each other. The forecasting self is calm, has an overview, and is imagining. The delivering self is tired, has four other things live, and is inside the week.

The tell is that this root does not discriminate. It happens with strangers, with people you dislike, and with tasks you have no feeling about at all. The scope you promise is usually about right. The date is what is wrong, consistently, in the same direction.

The fix is unglamorous and it works. Write the estimate down before you say it, break the task into its actual parts rather than treating it as one lump, and add the parts you never count, which are the waiting, the review, the thing that comes back needing changes, and the first hour of getting back into it. Then look at the total and say that number out loud instead of the one in your head. If your commitments are correctly sized and simply arrive late every single time, [the full arithmetic and the segment-by-segment method live on the lateness page](/patterns/always-running-late/), and that is the better tool for this root than anything here.

## What If the Promise Was a Way Out of the Conversation?

Then the delivery was never the point, and the tell is that you cannot remember what you agreed to.

This is the least discussed of the five and the most common in working life. Somebody raises something at the end of a meeting, or catches you in a corridor, or brings up a subject you do not want to open right now. What you want is for the exchange to stop. The fastest available stopper is a commitment, because a commitment closes a loop in a way that a question or a hedge does not. So you say leave it with me, or I will sort that out, or send it over and I will take a look this week.

None of that was a forecast. It was a door, and it worked immediately, which is why it keeps getting used.

The distinguishing evidence is unusually clean. You cannot recall the content of what you promised. You remember that the conversation ended and you remember feeling relief. If you had to reconstruct the actual commitment you would need to go back through your messages. Nothing about this happens in the other four roots, where the content is vivid because the feeling attached to it was vivid.

There is an overlap with the unprompted offer that is worth marking rather than blurring. The sentence leave it with me appears on the volunteering page too, where it is read as an open commitment with no scope and no end date, which is accurate. The difference in emphasis is what the sentence is for. There it is offered into a silence that nobody filled. Here it is deployed to end an exchange that was already running. [The unprompted version and its own roots are covered properly there](/patterns/volunteering-for-everything/), and the two frequently appear in the same person.

This root moves fastest of all five, because there is no need underneath it and no shame attached to it. It needs one substitute sentence, prepared in advance, that closes a conversation without committing to anything. I want to give that a proper look rather than a quick answer, let me come back to you tomorrow. That closes the loop just as effectively and contains no promise at all. The only reason it does not get used is that it has never been rehearsed, and the moment it is needed is not a moment with any spare thinking in it.

## Why Does the Number Get Defended Instead of Corrected?

Because the honest revision gets more expensive every day it is postponed, while the reason to postpone stays exactly the same.

Watch the arithmetic of a slipping promise. On day two you know the date is wrong. Correcting it on day two costs one message and a small amount of embarrassment. On day nine, correcting it costs one message, the same embarrassment, plus an explanation of why you waited a week. On day sixteen it costs all of that plus the fact that they have now planned around a date you knew was wrong for two weeks. The price of honesty rises steadily and the price of silence stays at zero right up until the deadline, at which point it becomes very large all at once.

Staw's work on escalation of commitment describes the same shape in organizational decisions, and Brockner's 1992 review found it robust across settings. Once a course has been publicly chosen, evidence that it is going badly tends to produce further commitment rather than revision. Applied to a Friday you named at a kitchen table, this predicts precisely what people report: a fortnight of intending to send the message, and then a delivery at sixty percent on the day itself with an apology attached.

There is a second mechanism stacked on the first. The revision message feels like it requires an explanation, and explanations invite discussion. Discussion is exactly what you did not want, which was often the reason the promise got made in the first place. So the message keeps not getting written, because in your head it is four paragraphs long.

It does not need to be. The version that works is one line with a number in it. The date has moved to the twenty-second. The version that fails arrives late, carries several sentences of reasoning and no new date at all, which leaves the other person holding uncertainty rather than information. Give them the number and let them plan. And if the apology has become the main event, arriving in four sentences every time, [the apology itself has its own mechanics and its own page](/patterns/over-apologizing/).

## Where Does the Inflation Hide When You Would Not Call It a Promise?

In about seven places that nobody would list as a commitment, which is why most people undercount this pattern by a wide margin.

The most common is the softened qualifier. Should be able to becomes will. Probably becomes definitely, inside the same sentence, in response to nothing. The word that carried your uncertainty gets quietly removed and the sentence now contains a guarantee you did not intend to give.

Then there is the borrowed deadline. Somebody asks when, you sense they have a date in mind, and you name their date rather than yours. Nothing was demanded. You read the room and produced the number the room wanted, and now you own it.

The unrequested extra is a quiet one. You are asked for a summary and you offer to include the analysis. You are asked to look over one section and you offer to read the whole thing. The addition happens in the same breath as the acceptance and it barely registers as a decision.

The vague large yes belongs here too. We should definitely do that. I would love to help you with that at some point. These feel free because they have no date attached, and they accumulate into a set of soft obligations that produce guilt without ever producing a task.

The optimistic recovery is the most sophisticated version. You are already late on something. Rather than naming a realistic new date you name an aggressive one, to demonstrate that you are taking it seriously. This converts one missed promise into two, and it is done in the honest belief that it shows commitment.

Then the group promise, made in a room of several people, where the number is set by what sounds reasonable in front of everybody rather than by what is achievable by you. And the promise to yourself, which is the same machinery pointed inward, and which nobody counts at all despite it having the highest failure rate of the lot.

## What Does the Over-Promise Actually Cost, and Who Collects It?

Both of you, in different currencies, and neither bill arrives itemized.

Your side is the dread, and it is charged continuously rather than at the deadline. It is on Sunday evening. It is in the ten minutes before you open your email in the morning. It is the specific small flinch when a particular name appears on your phone. Multiply that across however many live commitments you are carrying and the total is substantial, and it is being paid for work that is often only a few hours.

Their side is stranger and it is the part people do not anticipate, because it is not about affection. What erodes is plannability. People carry on liking you a great deal while quietly ceasing to route anything important through you. That happens gradually, nobody announces it, and it is almost never framed as a loss of trust in the dramatic sense. It is simply that your dates stopped being usable as inputs, so they stopped being used as inputs.

There is a third cost and it is the quietest. Every inflated promise removes a small amount of information from the world about what you can actually do. After ten years of this, you do not know your own throughput, because you have never once measured it against a number you believed. That is a genuine loss of self-knowledge and it explains why the estimates keep being wrong in the same direction. You have no calibration data, because you never generated any.

One thing worth saying plainly, since it is the fear that stops people looking at this at all. None of that makes you unreliable as a person. Over-promising is close to universal, the research says so, and the people who do it most tend to be people who badly want to be useful. The cost is real and the character reading is wrong, and both of those things can be held at once.

## Why Has Deciding to Be More Realistic Never Worked?

Because it aims at the wrong layer, at the wrong speed, and it treats a five-cause pattern as though it had one cause.

Take the speed first. The inflation happens inside the sentence, faster than deliberation arrives. A general resolution to be realistic lives in deliberation. It will be present and effective in every conversation where you were never going to over-promise, and it will be absent in the warm ones, which are the only ones that matter.

Then there is the aim. Deciding to be realistic addresses the number. The number is identical across all five roots, so the resolution is fired at the one part of the pattern that carries no information. For the forecasting root it happens to be roughly the right target and it still fails on speed. For the other four it is aimed at the wrong object entirely.

There is a third reason and it is the one that catches thoughtful people. Insight into which root you have changes nothing on its own. The inside is only going to stay on the inside unless you make it known and public on the outside and back the idea up with action. And the required action is different in each case. For the warmth root it is separating the feeling from the number by a day. For the disappointment root it is one smaller number said once to somebody safe. For the identity root it is a single piece of evidence about what a smaller number actually costs. For the forecasting root it is writing estimates down before speaking them. For the exit root it is one rehearsed sentence that closes a conversation without committing to anything. There is no substitute for the right action, and the right action differs in every one of the five, which is the entire reason for spending ten minutes finding out which is yours. [The general problem of understanding something and continuing to do it anyway is covered separately](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/).

Expect something specific when you start, because it catches people out and gets misread as failure. When you stop making the inflated promise, the warm moment will feel flat. Something that was reliably producing a small good feeling has been removed, and nothing has replaced it yet. It is actually not that hard once you commit to it, but it does feel like there is something missing in the meantime. That gap is the ordinary shape of removing anything that was quietly doing a job, and it closes.

## Which Root Is Yours, From Evidence You Already Have Tonight?

From seven questions, all of which you can answer alone, tonight, without speaking to anybody.

1. **Shrink one.** Take a live commitment and build the smaller version in your head. Logistics rising points at forecasting. Dread rising points at one of the other four.
2. **Sort by affection.** Lay out your last ten promises and sort them by how much you like the person. If size tracks affection, the warmth root is live.
3. **Check the room.** Does it happen more where you are being assessed than where you are liked? A yes points hard at identity.
4. **Measure the dread against the task.** Four hours of work producing a weekend of avoidance means the four hours are not what you are dreading.
5. **Test your recall.** Can you state, right now, exactly what you promised and by when? If you cannot, and you only remember the relief, that promise was an exit.
6. **Check for strangers.** Does it happen with people you have no feeling about at all, on tasks you do not care about? If yes, at least part of this is genuinely a forecasting error.
7. **Listen for the upgrade.** In your last few conversations, did you hear the offer getting bigger while you were making it? Hearing it and not stopping it points at purchasing rather than at estimating.

Two or three of these will agree with each other, and where they agree is your root. Where they disagree, you probably have two, which is extremely common and not a complication. Most people find one dominant root plus a second that shows up in one specific area of life, usually work.

It is good to have one's blind spots uncovered, and this particular uncovering can be done privately and cheaply before anybody else is involved at all. Nothing on that list requires a conversation, an apology, or a decision. It requires being honest with yourself for about ten minutes.

## What Would You Have to Give Up to Say the True Number?

More than you would expect, and naming it in advance is what stops the change collapsing in week three.

This is the question most advice skips, and skipping it is why the resolutions fail. Something is being given up, it is worth having, and pretending otherwise makes the loss feel like evidence that you have chosen wrong.

You give up the instant relief. The half second where the face changes back is genuinely pleasant, it arrives reliably, and nothing else in the conversation supplies it.

You give up a version of yourself. If you have been the person who can, the true number retires part of that description, and there is a real adjustment in being someone who names limits out loud rather than someone for whom limits do not seem to apply.

You give up the exit. Some conversations will now run longer, because the sentence that used to end them is no longer available.

And you give up a certain kind of hope. An ambitious number carries a private wish that this time you will be the version of yourself who does it in five weeks. Naming eleven weeks means giving up on meeting that person on this particular occasion.

What arrives instead takes longer to notice and is worth considerably more. Your dates start being used as inputs by other people. Your Sunday evenings stop containing three items of low-grade dread. And after about six months of promising numbers you believed, you find out for the first time what your actual throughput is, which is information you have never had and cannot get any other way.

## What Does a Promise You Can Keep Sound Like on an Ordinary Tuesday?

Undramatic, slightly shorter than you are used to, and with a number in it rather than a feeling.

| How it runs now | Where it goes | What that sounds like on a Tuesday |
|---|---|---|
| A date named from their face | A date named from the work | I can do the twenty-second. Does that work for you? |
| Warmth and number fused in one sentence | Warmth now, number tomorrow | I would really like to help. Let me look at my week and come back to you today |
| Leave it with me | A closing sentence with no promise in it | I want to give that a proper look rather than a quick answer |
| Should be able to becomes will | The qualifier stays where you put it | It should be fine by Thursday, and I will tell you Wednesday if it will not be |
| Silent slippage until the deadline | A one-line revision on day two | The date has moved to the twenty-ninth |
| The whole task promised | The part you actually control promised | I can get you the first section this week. The rest depends on their review |
| Sixty percent, late, with an apology | The whole of a smaller thing, on time | Nobody comments. You notice that nobody commented |

If the middle column reads as out of reach tonight, hold off on any verdict about yourself. The left column was built by a real life under real conditions, every entry in it was solving something at the time, and most of what it was solving was small and reasonable.

One caution about the second row, because people overshoot it. Delaying the number does not mean withholding the warmth. The warmth should be said immediately and in full. What moves to tomorrow is the date, and only the date.

## How Long Does This Actually Take to Shift?

Faster than the guilt version suggests and slower than the resolution version does, and it runs at five different speeds.

Noticing arrives quickly. Ten days with a written list of every commitment you make and most people can name their root, name the two or three settings it clusters in, and describe the exact point in a sentence where the size goes up. That stage is uncomfortable in a specific way, because you will catch yourself doing it live and say the bigger number anyway. Seeing it counts. Everything after it depends on it.

The exit root moves fastest, often inside a single conversation, because it needs one rehearsed sentence and nothing else. The forecasting root moves within a month once you start writing estimates before speaking them, and it moves on method rather than on effort, so the work is tedious rather than hard. The warmth root takes a few weeks of deliberately splitting the feeling from the number, and it feels artificial for about five attempts. The disappointment root needs one piece of real evidence, which means one smaller number said to one safe person, and it can move in a day once that evidence exists. The identity root takes longest, because the smaller number has to stop meaning something about you, and that changes slowly and only through instances.

Expect the count to go up before it goes down. Around day four the list gets longer rather than shorter, which is almost always a jump in noticing rather than in doing. That is the ordinary shape of watching a behavior rather than a sign that anything has gone wrong.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From public bodies of work, none of which require going through me to check.

On impression management, Schlenker and Weigold published their review of interpersonal processes and self-identification in 1992 in the Annual Review of Psychology. Schlenker, Britt, Pennington, Murphy and Doherty published the triangle model of responsibility in 1994 in Psychological Review.

On the distance between intending and doing, Rhodes and de Bruijn published their meta-analysis of the intention-behavior gap in 2013 in the British Journal of Health Psychology. Koehler and Poon published the work showing that self-predictions overweight the strength of current intentions in 2006 in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology.

On forecasting your own future capacity, Zauberman and Lynch published the resource slack work on time and money in 2005 in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. Halkjelsvik and Jorgensen published their review of time and effort predictions in 2012 in Psychological Bulletin, and it is the reason the planning fallacy appears here with a caution attached rather than as a settled universal. Bazerman and Tenbrunsel have written extensively on the gap between the self that decides and the self that acts.

On commitment and its escalation, Staw published the study that named the phenomenon in 1976 in Organizational Behavior and Human Performance, and Brockner published the review of the escalation literature in 1992 in the Academy of Management Review. Flyvbjerg published the distinction between optimism bias and strategic misrepresentation in forecasting in 2009 in the Oxford Review of Economic Policy, drawing on a long program of work on large project overruns.

On why promises bind at all, Charness and Dufwenberg published the guilt aversion account in 2006 in Econometrica and Vanberg published the competing commitment-based evidence in 2008 in the same journal. That disagreement remains open, which is why it appears above as an open question.

On the costs of low expectations, Norem and Cantor published the defensive pessimism work in 1986 in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.

The rest is mine and carries no research weight at all. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, and I am not a therapist. None of the researchers named here has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own reading disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.

## What Is Worth Doing Before Your Next Conversation?

One prepared sentence, a written list, and a single smaller number, and the first four days ask you to change nothing at all.

1. **Write every commitment down for four days.** Every date, scope and vague large yes, including the ones you make to yourself. Do not change any of them. You are collecting a baseline, and a baseline from a normal week is worth more than a good week.
2. **Mark each one with the number you were holding.** Next to what you said, write what you privately thought before you said it. The gap between those two columns is the actual measurement, and most people have never once written it down.
3. **Run the shrink test on the three most recent.** Build the smaller version in your head and note whether logistics or dread comes up. This one question separates a forecasting problem from everything else, and it takes about a minute each.
4. **Sort by affection and by room.** Does size track how much you like the person, or does it track being assessed? Those two answers point at separate causes, and the tests are independent of each other.
5. **Prepare one delaying sentence and say it out loud alone.** Let me look at my week and come back to you today. It has to be rehearsed, because the moment it is needed contains no spare thinking.
6. **Say one smaller number to one safe person.** Chosen for being low-stakes rather than important. Then watch what happens, which in almost every case is nothing.
7. **Send one revision you have been postponing.** One line, with a new date in it, no explanation. Notice how much less it costs than the version you had been drafting in your head for two weeks.

That sequence costs almost nothing and produces something no article can hand you, which is a measurement of your own gap in your own handwriting. Then put whatever you decide into your real calendar and hold the calendar, because a decision that lives only in your head has no way of surviving a Wednesday.

If what landed hardest was the shrink test, and specifically the discovery that some of your promises will not go smaller, [our Sacred Valley seminar](https://florianrolke.com/the-soulsearching-experience/) works at that layer rather than at the level of the number.
