You opened the app to send one message, or to look at nothing in particular, and the name was already half typed before you noticed your hand doing it. There was no decision anywhere in the sequence. There was a gap of about four seconds, and the gap filled itself.

You knew what was there. Nothing had changed since the morning, and you had already checked in the morning. You looked at it for eleven seconds anyway, and then you put the phone face down and felt the thing you always feel, which is not quite sadness and not quite anything.

Then it happened again the next day, and it has been happening for months, and nobody in your life has any idea how much of your attention this takes.

## What Does the Checking Actually Look Like From the Inside?

Like an errand with no destination, run several times a day, by somebody who would be embarrassed to describe it out loud.

The sequence is remarkably consistent across the people who describe it. There is a gap: a kettle, a lift, the ninety seconds before you have to start something. There is a reach that precedes any thought about the person. There is a scan, which is faster than reading, because you are checking for a difference from last time rather than taking in content. Then there is a landing, where the scan finds nothing, or finds something, and either way the small charge you were carrying has now discharged.

And then there is the afterward, which is the part that never makes it into the way people talk about this.

Here are the sentences people actually run about it, in the phrasing they use when nobody is listening.

- "I checked at 7:04, and again at 7:20, and I could not tell you what I was looking for."
- "I know the layout of that page better than I know my own."
- "I found out from a photo. I had been checking every day so that I would."
- "I have a version of their life in my head that I built out of nine pictures."
- "I felt fine all afternoon and then I looked, and now the evening is gone."
- "I do not want them back and I still checked at half past midnight."
- "I made an account with nobody on it. It has one purpose."

Read them slowly. The one worth noticing is whichever produces a small physical jolt because it is yours word for word.

Three details tend to arrive together, and almost none of them get said out loud.

The first is that the looking gives no pleasure. People assume this behavior must feel good, the way a guilty snack feels good, and it very rarely does. Most of the charge lives in the two seconds before the page loads. After that it is work.

The second is the specificity of the knowledge. You can date photographs. You can tell which ones are recycled, which follower is new, whose comment appeared and then disappeared. You have assembled a timeline out of thin evidence and you defend it internally as though it were reported fact, when a large part of it is inference you invented at midnight.

The third is that it survives the feeling. A significant number of people in this pattern have entirely stopped wanting the person. They would not take a call. They would not go back. And the check continues on schedule, which is the detail that convinces people something is wrong with them, because it makes no sense from the inside.

There is one more thing worth naming, because it is the piece that produces recognition. You already know the exact number of times you looked yesterday. You did not have to count. You have been keeping a private tally for months and you have never once said the number to another human being.

## What Do Researchers Call This, and What Does the Name Cover?

There are joking lay terms for it and there is a serious body of research, and the gap between the two is part of why nobody admits how much of their day this takes.

Start with the everyday words, because those are what you have probably already searched. Profile stalking. Creeping. Lurking. Checking up on someone. Every one of those is said with a laugh, which is useful socially and unhelpful practically, because a word you can only say as a joke is a word that cannot carry a real answer. Nobody says "I creeped for forty minutes this morning and it set the tone of my whole day" at a dinner table. So the behavior stays a punchline in public and a weight in private.

The research vocabulary is more precise and considerably more useful.

**Interpersonal electronic surveillance** is the central term. Tokunaga set it out in 2011, defining it as the use of ordinary communication technologies to gain awareness of another person's online and offline activity, and studying it mostly inside romantic relationships. The framing matters. It treats the checking as an information-gathering behavior rather than as a moral failure, which is both more accurate and more workable.

**Partner monitoring** is the adjacent literature. Fox and Warber looked at how attachment style and uncertainty predict how much people watch a partner's page, and found what you would expect: the more uncertain the situation and the more anxious the attachment, the more monitoring. Uncertainty is the fuel, and monitoring is the machine it runs.

**Post-breakup monitoring** is where this page mostly sits. Marshall published the study in 2012 that is directly on point, examining what happens to people who keep watching a former partner's profile after an ending. The finding was that continued monitoring was associated with more distress about the breakup, more longing and more negative feeling, and less of the personal growth that usually follows an ending, and that this held after accounting for how much offline contact the person had. Marshall also found that staying connected online at all was associated with lower personal growth, which is a slightly uncomfortable result for anyone who prefers to stay on friendly terms.

Lyndon and colleagues looked at the same territory in university populations and reported that a large majority had monitored an ex online. Darvell and colleagues found similar rates in Australia. The picture across those studies is consistent enough to say plainly: this is close to normal behavior after an ending, and its frequency tells you very little on its own.

**Relational rumination** is the term for what happens between the checks. Nolen-Hoeksema's response styles theory described rumination as repetitive, passive attention to distress and its causes, and established that it prolongs low mood rather than resolving it. Trapnell and Campbell separated rumination from reflection, which sounds similar and works completely differently: reflection is curious and finishes, rumination circles and does not. Checking a profile is rumination with a picture attached to it.

Two further ideas make the mechanism visible.

**Uncertainty reduction theory**, from Berger and Calabrese in 1975, holds that people are strongly motivated to reduce uncertainty about others and that information seeking is the standard route. That is exactly what the checking is, and it explains why it does not stop: the uncertainty is about a reason, and a reason cannot be found in a photograph.

**Ambiguous loss**, developed by Boss, describes a loss with no confirmation and no account, where the person is gone and also somehow still present. Boss's original work was with families of missing persons and later with dementia, and the core observation transfers cleanly. A loss without a clear fact attached tends to suspend mourning rather than move it. A profile keeps a person physically absent and psychologically present in one tap, which is close to a machine for producing exactly that state.

| What the term names | Who set it out | What it captures well | What it leaves out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interpersonal electronic surveillance | Tokunaga, 2011 | The act, the channel, the fact that it is a category of behavior | Why any individual is doing it |
| Partner monitoring | Fox and Warber | The link between uncertainty and how much people watch | What the uncertainty is about for you |
| Post-breakup monitoring | Marshall, 2012 | The cost: more distress, more longing, less growth | Whether stopping fixes it for your root |
| Relational rumination | Nolen-Hoeksema; Trapnell and Campbell | The circling quality, and why it prolongs low mood | Where the circle started |
| Uncertainty reduction | Berger and Calabrese, 1975 | Why unexplained endings drive information seeking | Why some people can tolerate not knowing |
| Ambiguous loss | Boss | Why an ending with no account suspends grief | What your particular ending withheld |

Here is what all of that genuinely gives you. It gives an accurate name for something that has probably been filed under personal weakness since it started. It establishes that a very large number of people do this, which removes the private conviction that you are uniquely pathetic about one person. It documents a real cost, so you can stop arguing with yourself about whether it matters. And it hands you vocabulary, which is more than most people carrying this have ever had.

Here is the limit of it, said just as plainly. Every one of those terms describes an act and its consequences. None of them can tell you what you are looking for. Two people can have identical browser histories, identical frequencies, identical thirty-second visits at 7:04 every morning, and be doing two entirely different things. One is looking for an explanation nobody ever gave. One is reading a scoreboard. The vocabulary covers the what with real precision, and the why sits outside its scope. The why is the part that has to move before the behavior does.

## When Does This Belong With Somebody Qualified Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, ahead of everything practical, because on this particular topic there are three lines that matter more than any technique further down.

The first is width. Looking at a page is one thing, and this page is for that. If what began as looking has moved into contacting them through other accounts, messaging people close to them for information, appearing where you know they will be, or driving past somewhere, then the behavior has crossed out of what is described here. That crossing usually happens gradually and without a decision, which is exactly why it is worth naming in advance. It belongs with somebody qualified, and it belongs there before it goes further rather than after.

The second is legal, and it is short. If the person has asked you not to contact them, or if there is any formal instruction in place, stop and get advice from someone who deals with that specifically. Nothing on this page is a substitute for that, and a page about understanding your own motivation is the wrong tool for a situation that has a legal shape.

The third is the reverse case, and it needs saying clearly because some readers will arrive here from the other side. If somebody is monitoring you, reading your messages, tracking your location, appearing where you are, or using your accounts to keep tabs on you, this is the wrong page for that entirely and nothing here applies to your situation. That is a safety question before it is a psychological one, and domestic violence and harassment services exist in most countries specifically for it. They are free in many places, and they speak to far more people who are unsure whether it counts than people who are certain.

There is one more line, which is about what else may be going on. If the checking arrived alongside months of broken sleep, a flatness that has not lifted, or a withdrawal from things you used to do, that combination deserves an assessment rather than a self-directed practice. Any thought of harming yourself belongs with a licensed professional now rather than later.

The rest of this page is built for the ordinary, costly, stable version. The one where you go to work, hold your life together, and lose eleven minutes and half an afternoon to a page you already know by heart.

## Why Does Knowing Exactly What You Will Find Not Stop You From Looking?

Because the check is testing whether anything has changed since last time rather than reading what is there, and that test has to be run before it can be answered.

This is the piece that makes the whole thing coherent, and almost nobody arrives at it on their own. You are not going to the page for what is on it. You are going to find out whether the state of the world has changed since your last look. That is a question your knowledge of yesterday cannot settle, no matter how complete it is. So knowing what is there does nothing at all to remove the reason for going, which is why the argument you keep having with yourself has never once worked.

Underneath that sits a reinforcement structure that is close to laboratory grade.

Ferster and Skinner established decades ago that behavior reinforced on an unpredictable schedule is far more persistent than behavior reinforced every time. If a reward comes reliably, the behavior stops quickly when the reward stops. If a reward comes occasionally and unpredictably, the behavior survives long stretches of nothing. That is precisely the schedule a profile runs on. Two hundred checks return nothing. The two hundred and first shows a new photograph, a location, an unfamiliar name in a comment thread, and the size of that one result pays for every empty check on either side of it.

Add the cost of an attempt, which is close to zero. It takes one tap and about four seconds. There is no journey, no phone call, nobody who has to be asked, and no trace left with anyone who might notice. A behavior with a near-zero attempt cost and an occasional large payoff is one of the most durable structures there is, and it does not require anything to be wrong with you.

Then add the third thing, which is that the reach is faster than the decision. Motor memory is not routed through deliberation. The path from the home screen to the profile has been run several thousand times and now executes as a single movement. This is why so many people describe finding that they have already checked, in the past tense, rather than deciding to. There was no moment where you could have intervened, because the behavior completed before the part of you that would have objected was consulted.

Put those together and you get the thing that makes people despair. You know it will be empty. You know it will make you feel worse. You reach anyway, several times, and none of that is evidence of a weak character. It is what happens when a zero-cost, unpredictable, motor-encoded loop meets a question that cannot be closed.

## Why Is the Flatness Afterward So Specific, and Why Does It Never Work as a Warning?

Because the good part of the sequence is over before the page finishes loading, and the bad part arrives too late and too quietly to be attached to its cause.

Look closely at what actually happens in those eleven seconds, because most people have never broken it down.

There is a rise before the look. Something is about to be resolved, or found, or confirmed. That anticipation is where the whole charge of the behavior lives, and it peaks before you have seen anything.

Then the page loads, and the anticipation has nowhere to go. Either there is nothing, in which case the charge dissipates into a small vacancy, or there is something, in which case the something is nearly always worse than the not knowing. There is no third outcome. This is a system with two exits and neither of them is good.

Then, about thirty seconds later, comes the flatness. It is unusually specific and people describe it in almost identical language: heavy, slightly ashamed, oddly tired, as though a small amount of the day has been removed. It is not dramatic enough to be called pain. It sits somewhere below the level at which you would take action about it.

And it never stops the next check, for three reasons that are worth seeing written down.

| What actually happens | When it happens | Why it fails to teach you anything |
|---|---|---|
| The lift of anticipation | Before the look, under one second | Immediate rewards outweigh delayed costs by a wide margin |
| The scan finding nothing | During, two to four seconds | Reads as neutral rather than as a loss, so nothing registers |
| The scan finding something | During, occasionally | The payoff that keeps the whole schedule alive |
| The flatness | Thirty seconds to a few minutes after | Too slow and too mild to be linked to the act that caused it |
| The attribution | Minutes to hours after | You blame them, the photo, or your life, and never the check |
| The residue on the afternoon | Hours after | Feels like your general mood, so the cause is invisible |

The attribution row is the one doing the most damage. You do not think "checking made me feel worse." You think "seeing that made me feel worse," which points at the content rather than at the behavior, and then you conclude that the solution is to see a different picture next time. So you check again. The loop repairs itself using the very evidence that should have broken it.

There is a fourth reason, quieter than the others. Attention itself is doing work here. Just by focusing on that, you make it real again and again. Just by the very means of not spending any time there anymore, you will feel less of it. That is the mechanism nobody wants to hear, because it is uncomfortably simple. The person occupies as much of your inner life as you keep pointing at, and every check is a small act of pointing.

## Why Do Two People Check the Same Profile for Completely Different Reasons?

Because the visible behavior under-determines its cause, every single time, and this particular behavior is more uniform on the surface than almost anything else.

By the fruit it will be known. Unlike nature, though, the same fruit can spring from two different roots. You cannot tell the roots by the fruit. The fruit that looks the same on the outside can have two very different root causes, and the same behavior on the outside can happen for two very different reasons.

The checking is a code. It stands for something underneath it, and the visible act is the encoded form rather than the thing itself. You can describe your own version exhaustively, count the checks, log the times, map the accounts, and still be standing entirely on the surface of it. Only when we identify the cause, the root cause of the behavior, can we ideate and put together a plan for a resolution that is truly effective.

Here are the roots I see most often producing the identical thirty-second visit at 7:04 in the morning.

| The root | What the check is reaching for | The tell that points to it |
|---|---|---|
| An ending with no information | An explanation nobody gave you | You date photographs and build timelines. You care about when more than what |
| Comparison | A reading on whether you are losing | You check hardest after something goes badly for you, and a happy photo is the one that ruins the day |
| Hope kept technically alive | Permission not to begin grieving | You feel relief at the absence of a new partner. Blocking feels like a death |
| Vigilance | The current position of something that hurt you | You check before events and before Mondays, and finding nothing produces relief rather than sadness |
| Habit alone | Nothing. The loop outlived the feeling | You cannot say what you were looking for, and you do it about people you no longer care about |

**The ending with no information.** You were given a sentence that explained nothing. They needed space, or things had changed, or it was complicated, and then there was silence in the shape of an answer. The profile becomes the only remaining document that might contain the reason, so you read it forensically. Every photograph is evidence in a case with no verdict available. The cruelty of this root is arithmetic: the reason, if it was ever articulated even to themselves, was never posted anywhere, so the search runs indefinitely against a source that does not hold the answer. Boss's ambiguous loss describes the state precisely, and Berger and Calabrese explain the engine driving it. [An ending delivered as silence works on people in a specific way and has its own page](/patterns/ghosting/).

**Comparison.** This root looks identical from outside and is a completely different activity. You are not gathering information about them. You are taking a reading on yourself, using their visible life as the instrument. Festinger's 1954 work established that people evaluate themselves against others whenever no objective measure exists, and there is no objective measure of whether you are recovering at an acceptable rate. So a profile becomes a scoreboard, and the comparison is structurally rigged: their curated exterior against your unedited interior, their best eleven minutes of the month against every hour of yours. Verduyn and colleagues found that passive browsing, scrolling without interacting, predicted declines in well-being, with envy doing much of the work in between. [Measuring yourself against other people has its own full treatment](/insights/how-to-stop-comparing-yourself-to-others/).

**Hope kept technically alive.** This is the quietest root and often the most powerful. As long as you are still checking, the story has not ended, and a story that has not ended does not have to be mourned. Every visit is a small vote that it might yet turn out differently. People in this root will tell you honestly that they know it is over, and their behavior is running a different position entirely. The giveaway is the shape of the relief: finding no new partner produces a lift, which only makes sense if some part of you is still holding a place. Blocking feels catastrophic to this root, and it should, because blocking is the moment the grief would have to begin. [Grief that has not been allowed to start behaves in recognizable ways](/patterns/unprocessed-grief/).

**Vigilance.** Here the checking is a perimeter sweep. If somebody hurt you, humiliated you, or is still in your life through shared friends or a workplace, then knowing their current position feels safer than not knowing. The nervous system's arithmetic is sound: a located threat costs less than an unlocated one. This root checks before parties, before Mondays, before anywhere the person might appear, and the emotion after a clean check is relief rather than the flat sadness the other roots report. [A system that stays permanently ready for bad news has its own description](/patterns/hypervigilance/).

**Habit alone.** The loop outlived the feeling that started it. There was a time when the checking meant something, and that time ended, and the behavior stayed on. Now it runs on a cue rather than on an emotion: the app icon, four seconds of empty attention, the specific dead spot between two tasks. People in this root check on people they genuinely do not think about, cannot say what they were looking for, and are often more embarrassed than distressed. This is the fastest root to move and the one that responds to friction rather than to insight. [Changing something at the level of its conditions is worth reading separately](/patterns/how-to-break-a-habit/).

Take the second and the fifth, because from outside they are indistinguishable and they need opposite things. The comparison root will not be touched by deleting the app, because the comparison continues without pictures, using memory and imagination. What that root needs is something real of its own to stand on. The habit root will not be touched by any amount of self-examination, because there is nothing underneath to examine. What it needs is a longer path from the home screen. Give each of them the other one's advice and both will fail, and both people will conclude they are the problem.

This is why the same technique produces such different results in different hands. It comes down to the match of the tool with the work the tool has to do. You can have a screwdriver, and if it is the wrong one it will not work for you, regardless of how good a screwdriver it is. [The same advice landing for someone else and doing nothing for you has a mechanism behind it](/patterns/why-did-the-advice-that-worked-for-them-do-nothing-for-me/).

A label can be looked up in ninety seconds. A root has to be found, in you specifically, which is slower and considerably more useful.

## How Did an Ordinary Look Turn Into Something That Organizes Your Day?

Usually through a stretch where checking was accurate and necessary, followed by a stretch where it quietly stopped being either.

Origin stories get used as excuses, and that wastes a good piece of information. There is a real difference between giving something an explanation and turning it into a story so you have an excuse, and both of them sound identical out loud. The difference shows up in what you do next.

The versions I see most often run like this.

1. **The first fortnight, when checking was rational.** In the days after an ending, the page genuinely was the only source of information about a situation you were still inside. Checking was appropriate, and it worked, and it did something for the panic. Nobody notices the moment that stops being true, because there is no moment. There is a gradual fade from useful to expensive that takes about six weeks and has no marker on it.

2. **The period when there was new information daily.** If something was actually unfolding, a new relationship forming, a move, a decision, then for a while every check paid. That is the training period. By the time the flow of new information dries up, the behavior is established on a schedule that no longer matches reality.

3. **The one time you found something.** A single significant discovery installs this faster than months of ordinary looking. A photograph that answered a question, a date that lined up, a name you had heard once. After that the checking has a precedent, and precedent is what keeps an unpredictable schedule alive through long empty stretches.

4. **The setting with no clean exit.** Shared friends, a shared industry, a workplace, a school gate, a family. When the person cannot be removed from your world, their profile becomes the one channel you can control, so all the unresolved material routes through it. This version is unusually common and unusually sticky, because the reminders keep arriving from outside no matter what you do.

5. **The loss nobody treated as one.** A friendship that faded. A relationship that was short, or private, or never officially anything. A colleague you were closer to than the job explains. Doka called this disenfranchised grief, a loss that the people around you do not recognize as a loss, which means there is no permission to mourn it and no conversation available about it. So it goes underground and comes out as checking, at night, alone.

6. **A childhood spent reading somebody's state.** If you grew up monitoring a parent, the mood in a doorway, the sound of a car, whether tonight was going to be a bad one, then surveillance is your oldest and most practiced skill. It was not optional then and it worked. In adulthood it points itself at whoever is currently unpredictable, and a profile is the most convenient surface it has ever been offered. [Growing up braced for what arrives next leaves a specific residue](/patterns/growing-up-with-an-unpredictable-parent/).

There is nothing weak or foolish in any of those. Every one was a working solution applied by somebody with limited options, and it did the job it was built for. That is the entire reason it is still running.

## How Much Is This Actually Costing You, Look by Look?

Almost nothing in a great many cases, and a genuinely large amount in a few, and telling those apart is the most useful thing on this page.

A page that treats every glance at an old acquaintance as a symptom would be both wrong and useless, so here is the honest accounting. A very large share of profile looking costs nothing at all. You hear a name, you type it, you look at a wedding photo of somebody you knew at twenty-three, you feel mildly interested, and your day continues unchanged. That is curiosity, and curiosity is not a problem in need of a solution.

Then there is a middle band, where most of this lives. A couple of times a week, a small residue, an evening slightly tinted. Uncomfortable and survivable, and not worth the amount of self-criticism people spend on it.

And then there is the version this page is for, which has a shape you can recognize.

| The pattern | What it costs today | What it costs across six months |
|---|---|---|
| A look every few months at somebody from years ago | Nothing | Nothing |
| A look after their name comes up in conversation | Two minutes and a small residue | Nothing |
| Once or twice a week at a former partner | A brief drop, quickly gone | Very little, if it stays there |
| Daily, first thing, before your feet are on the floor | The temperature of the morning | The temperature of most mornings |
| Several times a day, including the people around them | An afternoon whose mood was set by a photograph | A year in which very little moved |
| A second account built for the purpose | The looking plus the concealing | Your own sense of yourself |
| Conversations rehearsed against what you saw | An hour you did not notice spending | A version of them in your head that is not the person |
| Checking before you can start work | Fifteen minutes and a false start | Work that has been slow for a year and you blamed yourself |

Four tests separate the cheap version from the expensive one, and they are more reliable than counting.

**Does it have a floor?** A cheap look stops on its own when curiosity is satisfied. An expensive one runs until something external interrupts it, and then resumes.

**Does it set the day?** If what you saw determines the tone of the next four hours, the behavior has taken control of something it should not have.

**Has it grown?** More accounts, more people, more times per day, more platforms. Growth is the clearest signal there is, because a stable habit is a very different animal from a spreading one.

**Are you hiding it?** Concealment is the strongest single indicator on this list. Not because hiding is shameful, but because you only hide something whose size you have already privately calculated. If you turn the screen away, clear the search suggestions, or maintain a separate account, some part of you has already done the arithmetic and reached a conclusion.

Pass all four and you have a habit that is costing you very little, and the guilt you have been carrying about it is doing more damage than the behavior. Fail two or more and there is something here worth working on, and only that thing.

## Are the Platforms Making This Harder Than It Has to Be?

Yes, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. The difficulty is engineered, and knowing that changes what you should hold yourself responsible for.

Consider what checking on somebody used to cost. To find out how an ex was doing in 1994 you had to telephone a mutual friend and ask, which meant a person heard you ask, which meant a social price. Or you had to go somewhere. Or you had to wait until you next ran into them. Every route had friction, and every route involved another human being who would notice how often you were asking.

Now compare what it costs today.

The address is permanent and one tap away. Everything they have posted going back years sits there in order, so three years of a person can be read in a single sitting. Search fields remember what you typed and offer it back before you have finished the first letter. Stories expire in twenty-four hours, which manufactures a deadline, and anything with a deadline gets checked. Suggested contacts resurface people you deliberately removed, sometimes weeks later. Mutual friends carry them into your feed sideways, in the background of somebody else's photograph. And the exits are priced socially: muting is invisible and weak, unfollowing may be visible, blocking is legible to both parties and reads as a statement.

There is a further turn of the screw specific to this behavior. On some platforms, looking is detectable. Viewer lists on stories mean a check can be seen, which produces an entire second layer of anxiety about being caught looking, and that anxiety is a large part of why second accounts exist. The platform has made the surveillance visible in one direction only, and then sold you the workaround.

None of that is accidental. Time on the application is the product being optimized, and your inability to stop looking at one particular person is, from the product's perspective, a success. [The same architecture drives the scrolling that has nothing to do with any individual](/patterns/doomscrolling/).

So the difficulty is real and it is not a character flaw. And this is the point at which the useful move is to stop there and change the conditions instead.

Your environment affects you and impacts you more than you think. It has already put you into this position that you are in. That is not to come from a victim type of frame, as though the environment were doing this to you. You have put yourself in the environment, and the environment is reinforcing it.

That distinction is practical rather than moral. An environment you built is an environment you can rebuild, and it responds considerably faster than resolve does. Logging out, taking the app off the home screen, clearing the search suggestions and using a browser with no saved password will do more in a fortnight than any amount of deciding to be better at this.

## Why Does Deleting the App Work for Eleven Days and Then Stop?

Because pressure applied at one point in a system gets displaced rather than removed, and you cannot see where it went.

That is where the balloon bulges, that is where the behavior comes to see. When you have a balloon full of water and you squeeze it in one place, it will bulge on the other side. But you cannot really see that. You do not quite know the cause of it unless you see the place where it is being squeezed.

The bulges in this particular pattern are consistent, and they take between one and three weeks to appear.

You delete the application and start checking from a browser, which is slower and therefore feels less like checking, so you do it more.

You stop checking their page and start checking the pages of people near them, which produces less information at greater cost and takes longer.

You stop entirely and the checking becomes internal. You run the same material in your head with no pictures, which is relational rumination in its pure form and is arguably worse, because at least the profile occasionally contradicted the story you were telling yourself. [Circling the same past material without any new input has its own page](/patterns/why-cant-i-stop-thinking-about-the-past/).

You stop checking this person and start checking a different one. The behavior was never really about the individual, and now it has found a fresh subject.

Or the energy comes out as something that looks entirely unrelated: irritability with a housemate, three hours of a different application, a conversation with a friend that you steer toward the person four separate times.

Every one of those looks like a separate problem, and one to three weeks is more than enough to break the causal link in anybody's mind.

There is a second failure that looks like success for longer. There is also one thing to change the behavior with force or with discipline. That is not necessarily what makes it stick. You have got to have a way to sustain that, and for that there also needs to be an element of environment that needs to change. Eleven days of clean abstinence is a real achievement and a fragile one, because the reflex is sitting exactly where you left it, waiting for a bad Tuesday.

## What Actually Happens in the First Two Weeks After You Stop?

It gets louder before it gets quieter, there is a vacuum around day four, and almost everybody misreads the vacuum as evidence that stopping was a mistake.

This is worth describing in detail, because the shape of it is predictable and knowing the shape is most of what gets people through it.

**Days one to three.** The urge arrives more often than the checking itself did. You will reach for the phone and find your thumb already moving toward a folder that no longer contains anything. This phase is uncomfortable and shallow, and it passes.

**Days four to ten.** This is the hard part and it is not what people expect. The checking was doing something with the feeling, keeping it at a manageable temperature, giving it a small ritual to discharge into several times a day. Remove the ritual and the feeling arrives undiluted, all at once, with nowhere to go. It feels like there is something missing in the meantime, and that is always the case, that you think there is something missing. You realize very quickly that you can live without all of it easily enough, and that there is no real challenge in it. But days four to ten do not feel that way from inside, and this is where most people go back. [Feeling worse after removing the thing that was hurting you is a documented shape rather than a sign you were wrong](/patterns/why-do-i-feel-worse-after-quitting-the-thing-that-was-hurting-me/).

**Days ten to twenty.** The first day you notice, in the evening, that you did not think about it. That noticing is the marker worth watching for. It arrives on its own, unannounced, and it is the first genuine evidence that the loop is not permanent.

**Weeks four to eight.** The pull becomes occasional and situational rather than ambient. Anniversaries, mutual friends, a song, a particular street. It stops being a background process and becomes an event that happens to you sometimes, which is an entirely different way to live.

Two honest notes about this stretch.

The first is that a relapse does not reset anything. There is no counter. If you look on day nine, the useful response is to write down four facts and continue, because the trend across a month is the only measure that means anything and a single look barely moves it.

The second is that you cut out everything that is kind of nice and feels good for the moment and is not good for you in the long term, you go through the withdrawal in the beginning, and then you find out it is actually not that hard once you actually commit to that. The first fortnight is the price, in full, paid up front.

## How Do You Stop Without Making It the Only Thing You Think About?

By working on the root and the conditions at the same time, in moves small enough that you do not have to become a different person by Friday.

1. **Name the root before anything else.** One sentence, written down: what am I hoping the page will tell me? Do not accept "nothing" as an answer, and do not accept a paragraph. The honest sentence is usually short and slightly humiliating, and it points at which of the five roots is running, which determines everything that follows.

2. **Add friction rather than willpower.** Log out. Take the application off the home screen and out of the dock. Clear the search suggestions so autocomplete stops offering the name after one letter. Use a browser with no saved password. Every one of those adds four seconds, and four seconds is enough for deliberation to catch up with motor memory, which is the entire game.

3. **Delay instead of banning.** When the pull arrives, say not now, at nine. Then let nine come, and either check deliberately or notice that you no longer want to. Suppression backfires reliably and postponement does not, because you are agreeing with the urge about everything except the timing, which removes the fight.

4. **Log it for two weeks without stopping.** Time, what you were doing sixty seconds before, what you hoped to find, what you found, and how you felt five minutes later. Do not try to reduce the frequency while you do this. The log is doing two jobs: it makes the flatness visible by totaling it, and the "what I hoped to find" column will turn out to say the same thing every time, and that sentence is your root.

5. **Replace the function, not just the behavior.** If the check is a scoreboard reading, the replacement is an honest written inventory of your own year, which is uncomfortable and finite. If it is a search for a reason, the replacement is writing the explanation you were never given, in your own words, deciding it is good enough, and living on it. If it is hope, the replacement is letting the grief start. [Letting go of something you are still holding open has its own treatment](/insights/how-to-let-go/).

6. **Choose the exit that matches the root.** Muting is enough for a habit and useless for the other four. Blocking works and is genuinely harder, and if it feels like a death then it is doing exactly what needs to be done. Be honest about which one your root requires rather than picking the one that costs you least this evening.

7. **Tell one person the real number.** The inside is only going to stay on the inside unless you make it known and public on the outside and back up the idea with action. One person who knows how many times a day, and who will ask you about it in a fortnight, changes the odds more than any private resolution.

8. **Do not moralize a relapse, count it.** Shame is the single most reliable trigger for the next check. The look that follows a bout of self-criticism about looking is not a coincidence, and treating each instance as data rather than as a verdict removes most of the fuel.

9. **Expect the vacuum and schedule something into it.** Days four to ten will be worse than days one to three. Put something in that week that requires your presence: people, a body, a task with a deadline. Empty attention is where the reach lives, and the most effective intervention available is to have less of it.

10. **Give the attention somewhere else to go.** Just by focusing on that, you make it real again and again. Just by the very means of not spending any time there anymore, you will feel less of it. Attention is the thing that keeps this person as large as they currently are in your inner life, and it is also the one resource you have direct control over.

Two warnings, because both failures are common.

The first is turning this into a purity project. People read a page like this, resolve to never look again, look once on day six, decide they have failed, and check eleven times that evening out of pure self-disgust. The target is a page that no longer runs your afternoon, not a perfect record.

The second is treating understanding as the finish line. Self-knowledge is useful and it also puts a lot of pressure onto you if you do not act upon it. You can identify your root precisely, name every mechanism on this page, and still check at 7:04 tomorrow morning. [Understanding a pattern and running it anyway is its own well-worn problem](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/).

## What Does It Look Like When This Has Stopped Costing You Anything?

Quieter than you would expect, and the right-hand column still contains some looking, on purpose.

Every pattern like this has two ends, and describing only the costly one leaves somebody with an accurate account of their problem and nowhere to walk. So here is the other end, in the same ordinary situations.

| The moment | How it goes now | How it goes when this has moved |
|---|---|---|
| You wake up and reach for your phone | The page is open before you are properly awake | You look at the weather and get up |
| Their name appears in a friend's photograph | Forty minutes and a changed afternoon | A small pull, and then the conversation continues |
| You hear they have somebody new | You verify it immediately, then again that night | You feel it land, and you do not go looking for the detail |
| An anniversary date arrives | A full evening of reconstruction | A heavy morning that passes by lunchtime |
| You are bored in a queue | The reach happens before you notice | You are bored in a queue |
| Something goes badly at work | You check, and the comparison makes it worse | The bad day stays the size it actually is |
| You see them at an event | Two weeks of preparation beforehand | Awkward for ninety seconds, then fine |
| You do look, once, in March | Proof that nothing has changed | A look, some curiosity, and no residue |
| Somebody asks how you are about it | A version you have edited | An honest answer that takes one sentence |

Read the right-hand column carefully, because it is deliberately unremarkable. Nobody in it has achieved indifference. Two rows still contain looking, and one still contains a bad morning. The change is that the page has stopped being the place where your attention goes by default, and the person has stopped being able to set the temperature of your day from a distance without knowing they are doing it.

Do not judge yourself if that column reads as out of reach today. The left-hand one was rehearsed several thousand times by somebody who had good reasons at the start.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Longer than you want, and the clock depends on which root is running rather than on how much discipline you apply.

The noticing arrives fast. Within a fortnight of deliberate attention, most people can catch the reach in progress: the specific four-second gap, the movement of the hand, the moment before the page loads. That stage is frustrating, because you are watching something you cannot yet interrupt, and it is a real step regardless of how it feels.

The pull itself runs on a different clock, and the clock is set by the root.

| The root | What actually ends it | Realistic timescale |
|---|---|---|
| Habit alone | Friction, plus the cue going away | Two to six weeks |
| Vigilance | The threat's real position becoming settled and known | As long as the situation takes to resolve |
| Comparison | Your own life containing something you are not measuring | Months, and it moves with the life rather than the behavior |
| An ending with no information | Writing the explanation yourself and deciding to live on it | Weeks after you actually write it, years if you never do |
| Hope kept technically alive | The grief, once it is allowed to start | As long as grief takes, which is longer than anyone wants |

That table is the most useful thing here for anyone who has been trying and failing. If your root is comparison and you have spent six months on abstinence techniques, the abstinence was never going to be the thing that moved it, and your failure to fix it says nothing about your discipline. If your root is hope, then the checking has been protecting you from a mourning you have not started, and stopping the checking without letting the mourning begin will feel unbearable and will not hold.

It also arrives in patches. You will stop checking the profile and keep checking the stories, or stop entirely for a month and then have one bad fortnight around an anniversary. That is the normal shape of it rather than evidence that none of it took.

Two things speed it up more than anything else. Naming the root accurately, because it determines which of the five clocks you are actually on. And changing one condition in your environment, because force produces the change and environment is what sustains it. [A change that holds everywhere except in one specific setting has a mechanism behind it](/patterns/why-does-my-change-collapse-around-my-usual-people/).

Is that frustrating? Yes, and I would rather say so than sell you a faster route that does not exist. In order to live an extraordinary life, you really have to have a good portion of courage together with that, and here the courage gets spent in four-second increments rather than in one dramatic decision.

## What Is Worth Doing Tonight, Before Any Bigger Decision?

Keep checking, for seven days, and write down four things every single time.

That is deliberately the opposite of what you expected, and it works better than a resolution, because a resolution gives you nothing to look at afterward and this gives you a document. Every time you check, note the time, what you were doing sixty seconds beforehand, what you were hoping to find, and how you felt five minutes later. One line each. No commentary about yourself, no judgments, nothing about what kind of person this makes you.

At the end of the week, read only the third column.

Two things come out of it. The first is the frequency, which is almost always higher than the number you have been privately carrying, and seeing it written down does more than any amount of being told. The second is more useful: the "what I was hoping to find" column will say roughly the same thing every time, in your own handwriting, and that sentence is your root. It is not a sentence you can arrive at by thinking. It only shows up when you catch yourself in the act, seven times, and write down what you wanted before you have had a chance to tidy it up.

That exercise costs you nothing, takes about twenty seconds a day, and it is yours whether or not you ever spend money on any of this.
