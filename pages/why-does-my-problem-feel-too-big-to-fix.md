  a: "It means restating a vague, globalized, or fatalistic description as a specific, bounded, actionable one, using what a single concrete instance reveals rather than what the general pattern suggests. # Why does my problem feel too big to even start on?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

A problem stops being workable when it gets stated as pervasive and permanent: everywhere in your life, and always true. That phrasing offers nothing to aim at, so no first move exists to be found, and the failure to start reads back as proof of how enormous the thing must be. Restating it as one dated instance, and naming what was actually lost in it, returns a move.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
Read these as sentences you have actually said, rather than as descriptions of a type of person.

- "I don't know, I just can't get myself to do it."
- "The three words I say all day are hard, exhausted, and challenging."
- "those moments where you don't know which way is up anymore."
- "I used to also not write my thoughts down because I was so impatient and hurried. that made the thoughts repeat"

Every one of those describes a problem that has never been given edges, and a problem without edges cannot be started on, however much you want to.

You wrote fix my life at the top of a fresh notebook page in January, and closed the notebook without writing a second line. That was the fourth January in a row, and the page still has one line on it.

Most people land on a page like this having already tried the two available explanations and found both of them useless. The first is that the problem really is that big, which explains the paralysis honestly enough and prescribes nothing except waiting for some future version of your life with more room in it. The second is that you lack discipline, which fails immediately against the rest of your week, since the same person handles other people's difficult problems competently and has been doing it for years. Something more specific is usually running, and it is sitting inside the sentence you use to describe the problem.

One plain line before any of it. If there is drinking or using in the picture that has started to frighten you, if a hopelessness has sat on you for months without lifting, or if any thought of harming yourself has arrived however briefly, that belongs with a licensed professional today, ahead of anything written here. And where a persistent difficulty of this kind has a physical shape, an exhaustion sleep never touches, a heaviness that arrived without a reason and stayed, raise it with a doctor or another qualified professional; this page is about how a problem gets stated rather than about any medical question, and the two should not be run together.

## What Does This Look Like on an Ordinary Week?

Almost entirely unremarkable, and made mostly of moments where nothing happens.

- Writing fix my life at the top of a fresh notebook page for the fourth January in a row, and closing the notebook without a second line.
- Telling a friend you do not even know where to start about something that, when they ask one plain follow-up question, turns out to be one specific recurring Tuesday-night argument.
- Opening a blank document to finally deal with the money situation, and closing the laptop forty minutes later having renamed the file three times.
- Describing the same problem to three different people over two years using the same three sentences, none of which contain a date, a person, or an action.
- Feeling a wave of exhaustion at the thought of everything that needs to change, and picking up your phone instead of picking one thing.
- Reading a paragraph that describes your exact situation, feeling genuinely seen for ten minutes, and doing nothing differently that week.
- Rehearsing the whole problem in the shower for the two hundredth time, in the same words, and arriving at the same place you arrived at yesterday.

Read that list looking for the moment somebody gave up. There is no such moment. What there is instead is a person with real intent, real capacity and a genuine willingness to do hard things, standing in front of something that has been described in a way that no effort can enter. From the outside, and from inside, that looks exactly like avoidance. It is worth knowing that the two produce the same photograph, because the photograph has been most of the evidence you were working from.

One more thing about that list is worth saying out loud. Every item in it is verbal. The problem has been spoken about, written down badly, rehearsed, researched and confided, and in all those repetitions it has never once been described in terms that contain a place to begin. That is not a small oversight in an otherwise sound process. That is the whole failure, running for years, disguised as a lack of will.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, near the top, because this boundary matters more than every practice further down put together.

If the weight you are carrying involves harm to yourself, an addiction, a hopelessness that has not lifted for months, or a condition you are currently managing with someone's help, bring in a clinician before you run any exercise from this page on yourself. A page cannot see you. It knows nothing about your history, and it will not be there at eleven at night when the thing actually happens. Asking for help in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve.

There is a second version of that boundary, and it belongs here rather than at the end. Some problems are genuinely large, and the correct response to a large problem is resources sized to match it. Debt that has passed the point where budgeting touches it needs somebody who does that professionally. A legal situation needs a lawyer. A body that has stopped working properly needs a doctor. A marriage in real trouble needs a couples counselor, which is a service this site does not provide and never claims to. Everything below is written for one specific thing: the gap between a problem you can feel and a problem you can act on. Where that gap is the whole trouble, this page is useful. Where the trouble is the problem itself, go and get the person whose job it is.

## What Does It Sound Like From the Inside?

Like an accurate report on the weight of something, delivered without a single detail anybody could work with.

These are the sentences that run, in the phrasing they run in rather than tidied into a description of a mood:

- "I keep waiting for the moment when I will have done enough to be allowed to rest."
- "There was a time when I was trapped in a room, and I was very small, and I did not know how to get out."
- "Where I live emotionally is where I end up, and then I blame whatever happened that day for putting me there."
- "I certainly have experienced that I was not very kind to myself in my head"
- "I'm always preparing for the moment when things fall apart."

Read them slowly, and watch for the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word, with no translation required.

Two features of that list deserve attention before anything else. The first is that every sentence is true and none of them is specific. There is no Tuesday in any of them, no room, no person, no ten minutes anybody could examine. They are accurate at the altitude of a life and empty at the altitude where anything gets done, which is precisely why they can be repeated for years without producing a single change.

The second is the tense. Waiting, preparing, always, where I end up. These sentences describe standing conditions rather than events, and a standing condition has no first move inside it by definition. You would not know how to start on the weather either. [Feeling flooded before you have even looked at the list has its own page](/patterns/why-do-i-feel-overwhelmed-all-the-time/), and it runs alongside this one often enough that separating them is worth doing early.

## Why Does a Problem Stated as Everywhere and Always Stop Being Workable?

Because those two words strip out every edge a first move could grip.

Take any difficulty and add two features to the way it gets described. First, make it pervasive: this touches everything, my work and my body and my relationships and how I feel on a Sunday. Second, make it permanent: it has always been like this and there is no version of me where it is different. Neither addition contains any new information about the situation. Both change what the situation admits. A problem carrying both will feel impossible while the underlying facts stay exactly where they were.

Those two axes have a name and a research history, which is worth having because it moves the whole thing out of the realm of feeling and into something checkable. Martin Seligman spent decades studying what he called explanatory style, the habitual way a person accounts for a setback, and set it out for a general audience in *Learned Optimism* in 1990. Three dimensions carry it: whether a cause gets treated as permanent or temporary, as pervasive or specific to one situation, and as personal or external. Explaining setbacks as permanent and pervasive tracks with passivity and poor follow-through, and it does so somewhat independently of how objectively bad the triggering event was. The early animal research behind the idea has been substantially reinterpreted since, including by Seligman and Steven Maier themselves in a 2016 paper revisiting what those experiments actually showed. The explanatory-style finding has worn better than the theory that first produced it.

Here is the same situation stated three ways, so you can see what each version makes available.

| The statement | What it claims | What it admits as a next move |
|---|---|---|
| I am a failure | A permanent fact about a person | Nothing. There is no first step toward not being a failure |
| Everything in my life is falling apart | A pervasive condition covering all of it | Nothing specific. Any action is too small to register against everything |
| I have always been like this | Permanence, stated as history | Nothing. History is closed |
| My whole life needs to change | Both axes at once, plus a deadline nobody set | A to-do list of everything, which is the same wall one level down |
| I avoid difficult conversations at work | A described behavior with a setting | Practice one, badly, on Thursday |
| I have put off calling the accountant since March | One dated, bounded thing | Call the accountant, or find out why that specific call is frightening |

Read down the third column. The first four rows offer nothing, and that emptiness has nothing to do with how hard the underlying situations are. Rows five and six might describe considerably harder problems than rows one through four. They are enterable anyway, because they have edges.

## What Is the Vagueness Actually Protecting?

An undefined problem cannot be targeted, which means it never has to change.

This is the part that turns a piece of grammar into a mechanism. The obvious reading of a vague problem statement is carelessness: you have simply never bothered to describe it precisely, and if somebody helped you word it better the whole thing would resolve. That reading fails a basic test. You are precise about everything else. You can describe a work problem in four sentences with numbers in it. The vagueness is specific to this one thing, it is stable across years, and it survives every attempt to sharpen it, including your own. Structures that survive that much pressure are usually doing a job.

The job is shelter. While the problem stays at the size of your whole life, nothing can be aimed at it, which means nothing can fail at it either. A precise problem statement creates the possibility of a genuine attempt, and a genuine attempt creates the possibility of a genuine miss. Most people who have carried something for years have also, somewhere along the way, quietly decided that finding out would be worse than not knowing. Vagueness is how that decision gets implemented without ever being made out loud.

Consider a man in his forties who has not spoken to his father in nine years and describes it as settled. A closed case rather than an open problem. Stated at the scale of this is just how it is now, the situation is untouchable, because nothing follows from a verdict that has already been passed. Underneath, the anger is doing real work: it supplies certainty about who was at fault, it supplies standing, and it supplies a form of connection to a story he has been telling for nearly a decade. Asking him to restate the estrangement as a relationship that needs fixing gets refused instantly, and the refusal is rational, since the request asks him to give up something that is currently paying him.

What moved it had nothing to do with his father and nothing to do with forgiveness. Naming what the anger was buying, before anything was asked of him, turned a settled verdict back into a live question, and the live question was small: which version of this story do I want to be carrying at sixty. That is bounded, dated and answerable. It is a completely different object from nine years of it is just how it is, and the difference is entirely in the sentence. [Whether to keep distance from a parent at all is its own separate question with its own page](/patterns/should-i-go-no-contact-with-a-parent/), and it stays separate from this one on purpose.

## Why Doesn't Breaking It Into Smaller Steps Work Here?

Because a verdict about yourself has no steps inside it to find.

The standard advice for a problem that feels too big is to break it down, make a plan, or start somewhere small. All three assume the problem already contains steps and the only missing piece is the will to go looking. That assumption holds beautifully for a project and fails completely for a statement about who somebody is. There is no small first step toward not being a failure. Divide my whole life is a mess into subtasks and you get a list of everything, which reproduces the identical overwhelm one level down, with the added discouragement of having now also failed at the broken-down version.

| The standard advice | What it quietly assumes | What it produces on a statement like this |
|---|---|---|
| Break it into smaller steps | The problem contains steps | A list of everything, and a second failure to add to the first |
| Just start somewhere | Any entry point will do | Twenty minutes of moving items around, then the laptop closes |
| Be more disciplined about it | The gap is effort | More effort aimed at the same unenterable wall |
| Make a plan | The target is known | A plan for a target that has never been specified |
| Take it one day at a time | Time will do the sorting | Years, and the same three sentences |
| Get clear on your why | Motivation is what is short | A powerful reason to attack something with no surface |

Every row in that table describes decent advice failing for the same reason, which should be reassuring rather than depressing. None of it failed because you executed it badly. It failed upstream of execution, at the stage where the target was set, which is a stage almost nobody is ever told exists.

There is one more thing hiding in that table. Just start small assumes you have never tried the vague version. You have, repeatedly, which is exactly how you got here. What the vagueness removes is the ability to identify a valid small step at all, so what gets attempted instead is a small step toward something adjacent, it produces nothing, and the null result gets filed as more evidence about you.

## Is That a Pattern You Run, or a Verdict About Who You Are?

A pattern is something you do, and it can move the moment it is named precisely. A verdict is something you are, and it has to be defended.

That difference decides more than almost anything else on this page. Watch what happens grammatically. I procrastinate on things that involve being judged names a behavior, a trigger and a context, and it invites investigation. I am a procrastinator names a category and closes the subject. The second one also has to be protected, because it is now part of the furniture of who you take yourself to be, and people defend their own descriptions of themselves even when the descriptions are costing them.

The uncomfortable corollary is that the most caring-sounding move can be the one that makes a problem permanent. Somebody finally puts a word on what you have. There is genuine relief in it, and the relief is real. What happens next is that the target moves from the pattern to the person, and a person is much harder to change than a behavior on a Tuesday. Describe what you do, when you do it, and what it costs. That description stays workable. A word for what you are tends to become an explanation that ends the conversation.

Consider a woman in her sixties who describes a twenty-year stretch of her life as time she simply lost. She has said it that way for a long time, and every time she says it the shape is the same: twenty years, gone, unrecoverable. Held like that, nothing follows from it, which is the tell. The description also pays something, and this is the part that gets missed. It is the largest thing that has ever happened to her, and putting it down would mean putting down a significance she has nothing to replace with.

What moved it took nothing away from her. The restatement added a column she had never counted: whatever ended in those years also took with it a set of fears and demands she had carried since she was young, and she is not carrying them now. The years were not returned and were not argued about. The problem stopped being twenty years lost and became a ledger with entries on both sides, which is a statement that actually contains something to do next. ## Why Do Capable People Refuse the Advice That Would Obviously Help Them?

Because the advice arrives as removal, and nobody surrenders a trait they value for a vague promise of improvement.

This is the most reliably missed reason that good advice gets quietly declined. Somebody who is intense, driven, funny, exacting or relentlessly responsible has usually built most of their working life on that quality. Then a well-meaning person tells them to stop being so intense, or to care less, or to let go of the standards, and the answer inside is an immediate and permanent no. That refusal gets read by everybody, including the person themselves, as resistance to change. It is closer to accurate cost accounting.

Subtraction asks for something much smaller, and it is the reason a restated problem gets accepted where the original advice got refused.

| What the advice sounded like | What was actually costly | The subtraction version |
|---|---|---|
| Stop being so intense | The self-contempt that arrives when the intensity misses | Keep the drive, drop the punishment that follows a bad day |
| Care less what people think | Rehearsing every conversation for two days beforehand | Keep the attentiveness, drop the rehearsal |
| Learn to say no | Saying yes automatically before checking anything | Keep the generosity, add a sentence that buys you an hour |
| Stop being such a perfectionist | Refusing to show anything until it is finished | Keep the standard, show the unfinished version to one person |
| Lighten up | The joke that has a blade in it under stress | Keep the humor, drop the bite |
| Stop working so much | Eleven hours because nothing else in the week counts for anything | Keep the work, build one other thing that counts |

Read the middle column. In every row the costly thing is one ingredient, and it is considerably smaller than the trait it was riding on. The right-hand column is the only version anybody would actually agree to, and it is available for almost every problem once the problem has been specified precisely enough to separate the trait from the ingredient. [Standards that have hardened into an all-or-nothing sort have their own page](/patterns/black-and-white-thinking/), and [the version where the punishment feels like the responsible thing to do has another](/patterns/why-does-beating-myself-up-feel-necessary/).

## Does the Size of the Fix Have to Match the Size of the Complaint?

No, and assuming it does is how people either never start or spend months working at the wrong depth.

This runs both directions, which is what makes it genuinely useful rather than merely encouraging. A years-old, emotionally loaded complaint can turn out to rest on one un-examined belief. A mundane-sounding daily habit can turn out to need slow structural work and a changed environment. The emotional weight of a complaint is a poor proxy for the depth of the repair it needs, and treating it as a good one produces two opposite errors that are equally expensive.

| How the complaint sounds | What people assume it needs | What it sometimes actually needs |
|---|---|---|
| Twenty years of blame toward a parent | Years of slow work before anything shifts | One belief located precisely, in an afternoon |
| I am bad at switching off | A personality overhaul, or acceptance | Two more things in the week that count for something |
| I keep leaving the dishes | A tidier system | Nothing psychological at all, and a different sink routine |
| I cannot get up in the morning | More willpower | A conversation about what the day is actually for |
| I snap at my kids on Thursdays | Deep work on childhood | A Thursday schedule that stops running you into empty |
| I check my phone constantly | An app blocker | Whatever the checking is currently keeping at arm's length |

Take the parent case seriously for a moment, because it is the one that stops people from ever beginning. Somebody has carried blame toward a parent for years, describes it as an old wound that will clearly require long, slow healing work, and has avoided starting precisely because the scale of the presumed work feels unmanageable. On inspection, almost everything else in their daily life is functioning. Habits are fine. Work is fine. Most relationships are fine. The block turns out to be a single belief, largely inherited from another family member's account of events, sitting on top of an otherwise workable life. Locating that one belief precisely, rather than assuming the presenting intensity meant a proportionally enormous intervention was required, turned a problem that felt like it needed years into one that needed a single afternoon of direct attention. [How a belief installs itself and what it takes to move one is set out in full elsewhere](/patterns/limiting-beliefs/).

Now the other direction, because a page that only ran the shrinking argument would be selling something. Consider a founder in her late thirties who works eleven-hour days and cannot rest on a Sunday without a low-grade dread arriving by evening. She describes the problem as I am bad at switching off and has read a great deal about burnout. Stated that way it is a permanent trait, which explains nothing and offers nothing. The actual mechanism is specific: the work is a single activity carrying nearly everything she values at once, and Sunday is the only stretch of the week where none of it is being met. Restating it as one activity is carrying certainty, standing and connection all at once, and nothing else in my week scores above a four turned a character verdict into something scoreable. It also pointed at a bigger project than she expected, since building two more sources for those things takes months. [The needs that every activity in your life is quietly supplying are laid out on their own page](/patterns/six-human-needs/).

Both cases were mis-sized before they were specified. One was smaller than it looked and one was larger. The specification is what told them apart, and no amount of sitting with the feeling would have.

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because five different situations produce the same stalled Tuesday and only one of them is the one this page is about.

| What it feels like | What it might actually be | The thing that tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| The problem is too big to start on | A scoping problem | One dated instance makes it visibly lighter within ten minutes |
| The problem is too big to start on | A genuinely large problem | Restating it precisely changes nothing about the weight at all |
| I know what to do and cannot do it | The repair is aimed at the wrong layer | The effort has been real, repeated, and produced nothing for years |
| I do not want to look at it | Active avoidance of what looking would reveal | You can state the problem fine and change the subject each time |
| Everything at once, all the time | Load rather than framing | The list really is that long, and it shortens when things come off it |
| I sort every outcome into total success or total ruin | A binary sorting habit | The same event flips category depending on the day you assess it |

Three of those have better pages than this one. [Effort aimed at a layer that was never the problem is its own separate question, with its own five places to look](/patterns/is-this-a-discipline-problem-or-something-else/). The second row is the one this page takes most seriously, and it gets its own section.

## What About the Problems That Really Are That Big?

Some are, and no amount of better wording touches them.

A terminal illness is not a framing problem. A business two months from insolvency, a custody dispute, a body that has stopped doing what it used to do, a person you love who is in genuine danger: these are exactly the size they feel, and being told to restate them more specifically is an insult dressed as help. Any page that claims relabeling shrinks a problem is selling optimism and should be closed.

What this page claims is narrower and testable. Restating a problem in bounded, dated, specific terms tells you which kind you have. That is all it does, and it is worth doing precisely because the answer sometimes comes back large. Write the one instance. Name what was actually lost or blocked in it. Then read what you have written and notice what happens in your chest. If something loosens, even slightly, the language was carrying weight the situation had not earned. If nothing moves at all, believe that, and stop looking for a better sentence.

The honest response to a genuinely large problem is resources sized to match it, and that is a real answer rather than a consolation prize. Money problems of a certain size need somebody who does insolvency for a living. Legal problems need lawyers. Medical problems need doctors, early. A relationship in real trouble needs a couples counselor, and this site does not offer one. Knowing you are in the large case is a considerable improvement on suspecting it while doing nothing, because large problems respond to correctly sized help and respond to almost nothing else.

## How Do You Restate a Problem So It Can Actually Be Worked?

In a fixed order, on paper, on one instance rather than on the pattern in general.

Nothing below is a validated protocol, and anybody offering you one for your own life is overselling something. What follows is built from how the stall holds, and it is designed so that one person can run all of it alone in under an hour.

1. **Write the problem down exactly as you normally say it.** In its worst, vaguest, most defeated phrasing. Do not improve it. That sentence is the object being examined, and cleaning it up first destroys the evidence.
2. **Circle every word that means everywhere or always.** Everything, nothing, whole, all, never, always, constantly, my life, I am. Count them. Most people are carrying three or four in a single sentence and have never once seen them written down together.
3. **Ask for one instance, and make it dated.** When did this last actually happen? Not the pattern. One occasion. A Tuesday in March, a specific phone call, a particular fifteen minutes in a kitchen. If nothing comes, go further back and take whichever one arrives first rather than the most representative.
4. **Describe those ten minutes with no adjectives in them.** What was said, what you did, what you did next. Adjectives are where the global statement sneaks back in, so strip them deliberately and see what remains.
5. **Name what was actually lost or blocked in that instance.** This is the load-bearing step and it usually produces something smaller and stranger than the complaint: a five-second freeze, one rule you were obeying, one thing you were sure would happen if you spoke. It is rarely what the general description advertised.
6. **Restate the problem by subtracting one ingredient rather than a whole trait.** Keep what you value and name the single element that made it costly. If the new sentence asks you to become a different person, it is still wrong and needs another pass.
7. **Read the restatement and check for a next action.** A workable statement contains a verb, a target, and something a reasonable person could attempt this week. If there is still nothing to do, you are one level too high; take the instance from step three and go smaller.
8. **Do one thing through it within seventy-two hours.** Small, specific, and preferably slightly early. The restatement has a shelf life, and the next section is about why.

Is any of that comfortable? No. Writing your own worst sentence down and looking at it in your own handwriting takes real, freaking discipline, and it is the specific discipline this situation requires rather than a general recommendation to try harder. It also takes about forty minutes, which is considerably less than the fourth January.

## What Does a Restated Problem Look Like on Paper?

Considerably duller than the original, and that dullness is the whole point.

| How it usually arrives | What one instance revealed | The restated version |
|---|---|---|
| My whole life needs to change | A Sunday evening dread that arrives at exactly six | I have nothing in my week that counts for anything except work |
| I am terrible with money | An unopened account, and a fear of the number | I have avoided one specific number since March |
| I have no discipline | Everything else in the week gets done on time | I have never scheduled the one thing I promised myself |
| I ruin every relationship | A pattern of going quiet at the point things deepen | I go silent when somebody gets close, and I have never said so out loud |
| I have wasted twenty years | A ledger with entries on both sides | I have never counted what those years took away with them |
| I am just an anxious person | A specific meeting, a specific rehearsal | I rehearse conversations that involve being evaluated |
| Everything is falling apart | Two things are difficult and eleven are fine | Two situations are genuinely hard, and here they are, named |

Read down the third column and notice how ordinary it looks. None of those sentences is inspiring. Every one of them can be acted on before Friday, which the left-hand column could not, at any point, in any year.

Notice also what the restatement did not do. It did not make anything smaller by decree. In two of those rows the restated problem is arguably harder than the original, since it now names something specific that will have to be faced instead of a general fog that could be carried indefinitely. Workable and easy are different properties, and only one of them was ever on offer here.

## Why Does an Accurate Restatement Close Again Within Days?

Because a restatement is a perishable thing, and almost nobody is warned about the shelf life.

The moment a problem gets described accurately, something loosens. It feels like the change. It is genuinely the necessary condition for the change, and on its own it lasts about as long as the mood does. Come back to the same sheet of paper in ten days without having done anything through it and you will read your own restatement as an interesting observation about yourself, which is what it becomes once the state that produced it has gone. [Why an accurate insight can wear off by the same evening has its own page](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/), and it is the natural next read after this one.

The practical response is small and slightly annoying. Attach one action to the restatement immediately, while the new sentence still feels true rather than remembered, and make the action specific about when and where. Peter Gollwitzer's work on implementation intentions, set out in *American Psychologist* in 1999, tested exactly this kind of if-then specification and found that plans naming a situation and a response reliably beat equally sincere intentions that named only a goal. The finding has been replicated widely, its effect sizes have been argued over, and the direction has held up. What that means here is unglamorous: I will call the accountant beats I will sort out the money, and I will call the accountant on Wednesday before lunch beats both.

There is a second reason to move quickly, and it has nothing to do with motivation. A restatement is a hypothesis. It might be wrong. Acting on it within a few days is how you find out whether the thing you named was actually the thing, and a wrong restatement discovered on Wednesday is far cheaper than one you carried around admiring for three months.

## Why Does Reading About the Problem Feel Like Working on It?

Because consuming material about the category produces most of the sensation of addressing it and none of the scoping.

This is the most respectable way to stall, and it is the one this exact page is at risk of participating in. Books about the general shape of your difficulty, videos that describe your situation with unnerving accuracy, a podcast that names something you had never had words for: all of it is genuine effort, all of it feels like progress, and none of it produces a dated instance. You can read for two years and still be unable to write down one Tuesday.

The tell is a single question, and it is worth asking yourself honestly right now. After everything you have read about this, could you write one paragraph describing one specific occasion, with a date on it and what was actually blocked in those ten minutes? If yes, the reading has been doing its job. If no, then nothing has been scoped yet, however much has been understood, and another book will not change that.

Understanding also has a way of becoming its own destination, especially for people who are good at it. Somebody articulate enough to describe their pattern in three well-chosen sentences can spend a decade producing better and better descriptions while the pattern runs exactly as it always did. [Looking at yourself as a practice, and what it does well and badly, is worth reading separately](/insights/what-is-introspection/), as is [the argument about why self-improvement so often makes the thing worse](/insights/why-self-improvement-fails/).

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

The restatement takes an hour. What follows takes anywhere from a week to a couple of years, and the restatement is what tells you which.

Two speeds are worth separating, because confusing them is what makes people quit. The scoping itself is fast. One sitting with a pen, done properly, produces a workable statement in most cases, and a surprising number of people report that most of the felt weight comes off during the writing rather than afterward. That happens when the presenting problem genuinely was a vague description sitting on top of one specific, nameable thing. It is common enough to expect and unreliable enough not to promise.

The second speed belongs to whatever the restatement revealed. Sometimes it is a fortnight of doing one slightly uncomfortable thing. Sometimes it is a rule you have obeyed since you were nine, and that takes months of repetitions under real conditions. Sometimes it is a situation that needs money, a professional, or somebody else's cooperation, and it runs on their timetable rather than yours. None of those outcomes says anything about you, and all of them are better than the fourth January.

Some of this does not resolve, and the page would be dishonest without saying so. You can restate a problem impeccably, act on it promptly, and find the thing underneath is larger and slower than you hoped. That is a real outcome rather than a failure of the method. What you will have then is an accurately sized problem and a correct idea of what it needs, which is a considerable upgrade on a feeling you have been carrying for four years with no edges on it.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From two lines of public research and one clinical model, all in print for decades, none of which needs to go through me.

Martin Seligman is the main strand. Working from experiments he began with Steven Maier in the late 1960s, he spent the following two decades studying explanatory style, and *Learned Optimism*, published in 1990, is where a general reader can find it. The three axes described above, permanence, pervasiveness and personalization, come from that work, along with the finding that habitually explaining setbacks as permanent and pervasive predicts passivity somewhat independently of the actual severity of the setback. Seligman and Maier revisited the original animal research in a 2016 paper in *Psychological Review* and revised what those early experiments were showing, which is worth knowing if you go looking; the explanatory-style material has held up more robustly than the theory that first generated it.

The claim that defining a problem is a separate step from solving it comes out of clinical psychology rather than out of self-help. Thomas D'Zurilla and Marvin Goldfried published the social problem-solving model in 1971, and Nezu and colleagues developed it clinically across the following decades. Its structural claim is the one this page is built on: problem definition and formulation is its own trainable stage, distinct from generating options and distinct from choosing between them, and people routinely fail at solvable problems because the problem was never stated in workable terms in the first place.

The general version of the same point sits in organizational psychology, under the names Locke and Latham. Across five decades they tested what makes a goal produce sustained effort, and summarized the whole program in a 2002 paper in *American Psychologist*. Specific, well-defined goals reliably outperform vague or do-your-best goals, largely because a specific goal gives a person something concrete to measure against. It is one of the more heavily replicated findings in the field, and it is the checkable version of what this page claims about personal problems: vagueness suppresses action on its own, independently of how hard the underlying thing is. Peter Gollwitzer's implementation-intentions work, in the same journal in 1999, extends that to the moment of action.

The rest of it is mine and carries no research weight at all. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, and none of the researchers named above has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, theirs is the one worth going and checking.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Try to Fix Anything?

Take one sheet of paper and give it four lines, in this order. On the first, write the problem exactly as you say it to yourself, in the worst and vaguest phrasing you actually use. On the second, write the date and place of the last time it genuinely happened, one occasion, no summarizing. On the third, describe what happened in those ten minutes with no adjectives in it. On the fourth, write what was actually lost or blocked, in the smallest terms that are still true.

Then read line one and line four next to each other. Two things usually land. The first is the distance between them: line one is about your whole life and line four is about a specific fifteen minutes, and both of them are honest descriptions of the same thing. The second is that line four contains something to do and line one never did, in four years of being repeated. That costs you forty minutes and a sheet of paper, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever go anywhere or tell anybody you did it.
