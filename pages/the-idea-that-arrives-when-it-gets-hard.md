- q: "Why does a better idea always turn up the moment the current one gets hard?"
  a: "Because the conditions that produce a new idea are the conditions the current project has just started supplying. Difficulty raises the price of continuing, and a mind that runs on unresolved problems goes looking for a fresh one. From inside, the timing reads as coincidence and the idea reads as inspiration. Written out on a line with the dates against it, the coincidence stops looking like one."
- q: "What is shiny object syndrome?"
  a: "A popular phrase for the habit of dropping whatever you are doing when something newer and more interesting appears. It travels through business writing, and increasingly through ordinary conversation, as a name for serial enthusiasm. It has no clinical status, no measurement instrument and no research literature of its own. It is an accurate label for a visible habit, and it explains nothing at all about why any particular person has it."
- q: "Is shiny object syndrome a real condition?"
  a: "No, and nobody serious claims it is. There is no diagnostic definition, no scale and no body of studies behind the phrase. It spread because it describes something people recognize the second they hear it. Recognizing yourself in a phrase is genuinely useful. Treating the phrase as the reason you behave that way is where it starts costing you, because a name closes the question that was worth asking."
- q: "What does shiny object syndrome mean in business?"
  a: "In business writing it usually means chasing a new tactic, tool or market before the previous one has been given long enough to produce anything. The complaint underneath it is fair, since most approaches need repetition before they show a result and switching resets the count to zero. What the business version misses is that the switching is often supplying something the person needs in order to work at all."
- q: "Why does the new idea always look better than the one I am working on?"
  a: "Because you are comparing two things at different stages. The new idea exists only as a conception, so it carries no compromises, no unanswered messages and no dull middle stretch yet. The current project exists in full, with every one of those attached to it. A fair comparison would set the new idea against the current one on its best week, and almost nobody runs that comparison."
- q: "Why can I not stay with one thing?"
  a: "Often because the one thing stopped producing the state that makes work possible for you. Some people run on having an unresolved problem churning in their head. While that is running, ordinary effort is easy and the difficult message gets sent. When it stops, the effort stops with it. Switching that looks like a shortage of discipline is frequently a supply problem wearing a discipline costume."
- q: "Is this a discipline problem?"
  a: "Rarely, and the arithmetic argues against it. Somebody who has begun and driven forward several substantial projects has produced more sustained effort than the discipline story allows for. What stops is specific, and it stops at a specific point. Treating a targeted stop as a general shortage of willpower sends you searching in the one place the answer will never be, which makes the next attempt harder rather than easier."
- q: "How do I know if the new idea is actually better?"
  a: "Write it out in one paragraph, including the first three concrete steps and the first genuinely dull task in it, then leave it alone for sixteen days. Read it cold. An idea with something in it survives that. An idea that was carrying a feeling reads flat, and the flatness is the information you wanted. Almost nobody does this, because the appeal of a new idea is that it is available right now."
- q: "Is having lots of ideas a bad thing?"
  a: "It is closer to an asset than a defect, and most writing on this gets that backwards. The faculty producing a new idea on a difficult afternoon is the same faculty that produced the first one, and without it there would be nothing to abandon in the first place. The useful question has never been how to have fewer ideas, only what the arrival of one is allowed to do to your week."
- q: "How is this different from abandoning things at ninety percent?"
  a: "Timing, and the two often run in the same person. That pattern starts at the end, where a nearly finished thing gets put down because finishing it would produce a verdict. This one starts in the middle, at the first genuinely hard stretch, and the new idea arrives before anything has been dropped. [The version that happens near the finish line has its own page.](/patterns/abandoning-things-at-ninety-percent/)"
- q: "Why do I buy the tool for the new idea before doing anything?"
  a: "Because a purchase is the fastest available action and it converts a feeling into evidence that something has begun. It also postpones the first real step by supplying a legitimate reason to wait. [The pattern where preparing replaces starting has its own page](/patterns/researching-instead-of-starting/), and it covers the buying and the reading in far more depth than a section here could."
- q: "Does writing my ideas down in a list actually help?"
  a: "For some people it settles the week completely and for others it makes the week worse, and which one you get depends on the root. Where the idea was rescuing you from one specific difficulty, a list is enough, because the idea only needed somewhere to go. Where the idea was supplying the state that gets you to your desk, a list removes the supply and puts nothing in its place."
- q: "What is shiny object syndrome in relationships?"
  a: "The phrase gets used for the pull toward somebody new when a current relationship reaches its ordinary difficult stretch, and the timing question transfers cleanly enough. The rest of it does not transfer. A relationship is a setting a personal pattern shows up in rather than a project with a next version, and any page treating a person as an idea to be evaluated has lost the thread completely."
- q: "Why do I get bored so easily?"
  a: "Boredom gets reported as a property of the task and behaves much more like a report on the fit between you and what is in front of you. Researchers who study it describe an unengaged state where attention cannot find purchase on the available activity. That state is genuinely uncomfortable and it pushes hard toward something else, which looks like a design feature rather than a fault in you."
- q: "Why does the excitement disappear a few weeks in?"
  a: "Because the part that was paying you is finishing. Early work is mostly discovery, and discovery answers questions, which is the payment. Later work is mostly execution of decisions already made, and execution answers nothing. The drop you notice somewhere around the third or fourth week is the changeover between those two, and it arrives on schedule regardless of how good the project actually is."
- q: "What if the idea itself is the thing I want, rather than the result?"
  a: "Then the product is the conception and everything after it is delivery, which is a service performed for other people. That root is real and it is no character flaw. It gets expensive only where something on the far side of the work was genuinely needed, by you or by somebody else. The practical answer is usually to hand the delivery to a person who likes delivering."
- q: "Why does deciding to focus never last more than a week?"
  a: "Because a decision changes what you intend and leaves everything that produces the arrival exactly as it was. The difficulty in the current project is still sitting there, the faculty that generates alternatives is still working, and nothing has been put in place to carry you through the dull stretch. A resolution against new ideas is a resolution against weather. Something in the week itself has to move."
- q: "What is the difference between a pivot and a switch?"
  a: "A pivot keeps the accumulated work and changes its direction. A switch discards the accumulated work and starts the count again from zero. The word pivot gets applied to both, which is convenient, because it makes the second one sound like strategy. The honest test is what you carried across. If nothing came with you except the enthusiasm, that was a start rather than a pivot."
- q: "How many ideas is too many?"
  a: "The count matters far less than the shape. Fifteen ideas written down with one being worked on is a healthy arrangement and probably a productive one. Three ideas, all live, all half-built, none of them touched this week, is the expensive version despite the smaller number. Lay them out with the date each one arrived and look at what was happening in the week it landed."
- q: "What actually happened just before the idea arrived?"
  a: "That is the single most useful question on this page and most people have never once asked it. Take the last three ideas that pulled you off something, find the date each one landed, then write down what the current project was asking of you in the two days before. A hard message, a decision, a stretch of dull work. The pattern is usually visible after three."
- q: "Why does the idea arrive complete, with a name and a plan attached?"
  a: "Because it assembles out of material you already have, and it arrives without the parts that only appear on contact with reality. A conception is made of the interesting decisions. The dull ones have not been met yet, so they are not in it. That is why a new idea can feel more worked out after one evening than the current project felt after a whole season of real work."
- q: "Does this mean I should never start anything new?"
  a: "No, and any page saying so is selling a rule rather than an answer. Some new ideas are correct, arrive at the right moment, and deserve to be started that week. What this page suggests is much smaller. Find out what the current thing was asking of you in the days before the new idea landed, and then decide with that information in hand rather than without it."
- q: "What is the cost of running four ideas at once?"
  a: "Attention divided across four live things produces exactly enough progress on each to keep it alive and never enough on any one to reach the stage where it starts paying. The cost sits somewhere other than the wasted hours, which were often enjoyable, and it is that nothing accumulates. Results in most fields come from repetition on one thing, and a fourth live idea makes repetition impossible."
- q: "How is this different from not being able to decide?"
  a: "Indecision holds two known options open and cannot pick between them. This pattern keeps producing new options, so a third one arrives before the first two have been settled. The two feed each other and they need different handling, which is why picking the right page saves you a great deal of reading. - q: "What if I have already wasted years on this?"
  a: "The hours are gone whichever way you decide now, and using them as an argument is one of the worst reasons available for anything. Two things are worth recovering instead. The ideas you dropped are usually still viable, since almost nobody else did them either. And the skill built on each attempt did not disappear when the attempt did. Most people here are further along than their own account allows."
- q: "Why do I feel relief the moment I decide to switch?"
  a: "Because deciding removes the difficulty that was sitting in front of you, and removal registers immediately while the cost of it arrives months later. The relief is real and it carries no evidence about whether the decision was right. Watch what it does over the following two weeks. A correct switch stays relieved. The other kind turns heavy again once the new thing reaches its own hard stretch."
- q: "Is this the same as being easily distracted?"
  a: "Distraction pulls you away for minutes and you come back. This pulls you away for months and you do not. The material is different too. A distraction is usually something undemanding, while what arrives here is a substantial piece of work that would take a year and might genuinely be worth doing. That is exactly why it is so hard to refuse and why advice about attention lands nowhere."
- q: "Where does this show up outside work?"
  a: "In renovations that change plan at the difficult stage, in training that switches sport in the boring month, in learning where a fourth language starts the week the third one requires speaking to a stranger, and in a house move that quietly becomes a different house move. The arenas differ and the timing does not. Look for the point in each one where the interesting part had just finished."
- q: "Does having a partner or a team fix it?"
  a: "It fixes one version completely and does nothing for the others, which makes it a useful test in itself. If somebody else expecting the work is enough to carry you through the hard stretch, the missing piece was structural and you can build it. If you push through with them watching and the next project then stalls in the identical place, something else was doing the work."
- q: "What does the version that holds look like?"
  a: "One thing being worked on, a written list of ideas that are explicitly not being worked on, and a habit of dating each new idea on the day it arrives. The ideas keep coming at the same rate as before. What changes is that arriving stops counting as an instruction. Most people describe the shift as quieter rather than as more disciplined, and still call themselves an ideas person."
- q: "What is the psychology behind shiny object syndrome?"
  a: "There is no psychology of the phrase itself, because it is not a research construct. There is real work on the pieces underneath it: how reliably people take the cheaper option when effort rises, how boredom functions as a push toward something else, how curiosity driven by interest differs from curiosity driven by a gap, and how badly creators forecast which of their own ideas will succeed."
- q: "How do I overcome shiny object syndrome?"
  a: "Start by refusing the framing, because overcoming implies the ideas are the problem and they are usually the asset. Keep generating them, date each one, and put a fixed gap between an idea arriving and anything being started on it. Then find out what the current project was asking of you in the days before the last three arrivals. Fix that, and arrivals become information rather than instructions."
- q: "Why do I feel scattered even when I am working hard?"
  a: "Because effort spread across several live things produces the sensation of a full week and very little that accumulates. Scattered is usually an accurate self-report about structure rather than about character. The uncomfortable part is that the weeks feel productive from inside, since all four things genuinely did move, which is how this can run for years without anybody involved ever calling it a problem."
- q: "Is this something I inherited or something I learned?"
  a: "No page can answer that for you, and anybody who does is guessing. What can be said is that the arrival of ideas is not the part that varies most between people in this pattern. What varies is what a household treated as a reason to stop, whether unfinished things were shameful or ordinary, and whether anyone around you ever modeled staying with something dull on purpose."
- q: "Why does the second idea arrive before the first one is finished, every time?"
  a: "Because the generator does not wait for a vacancy. It responds to conditions, and the condition it responds to is a stretch of work that has stopped producing answers. That stretch arrives in every project, usually earlier than people expect, so the second idea has an opening long before the first project is anywhere near done. Nothing is broken in that sequence. It is simply not an instruction."

  a: "If the switching has spread into things with real consequences, if it sits inside a flatness that has not lifted for weeks, if nothing at all has held you past the first month for a long stretch, or if the pace of new plans has started to alarm people who know you well. The same applies if there is any thought of harming yourself. That belongs with a licensed professional now rather than later."

# Why does a better idea always turn up the moment the current one gets hard?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because the new idea is answering a shortage the current project has just created. Difficulty raises the price of continuing, and a mind that runs on unresolved problems goes looking for a fresh one. The idea that shows up comes from the same faculty that produced the first one, and it lands in the week the first one stopped paying.

The project is four months old and the interesting part of it finished a while ago. What sits in front of you this week is a price you have to set and then defend, a cold email to somebody who read the last one and did not answer, and a stretch of assembly with no decisions in it worth making. You know how to do all three. None of them will teach you anything.

Then, on a Thursday, in a hardware store, buying something for an unrelated job, the idea arrives.

It arrives whole. It has a shape, it has a first customer, it has a name for it, and the name is good. By the time you reach the car you can see the first three months of it. By the evening there is a domain name registered and a page of structure on the whiteboard, and the feeling in your chest is the one you had at the very beginning of the thing you are supposedly still doing.

Now the part that every account of this leaves out. Nothing has been abandoned. The current project is still open on your desk. If somebody asked you tonight what you are working on you would name it, honestly, with no sense of lying. The abandonment, if it comes, comes weeks later and it comes quietly, by neglect rather than by decision, and by then the story will be that the second idea was simply better.

This page is about the arrival rather than the abandonment. The switching is what everybody writes about, because switching is the part other people can see. The arrival is the event. It has its own timing, it has a function, and the function is not what it looks like from outside.

One thing before the mechanism, because it decides how the rest of this reads. **The ideas are not the problem.** In most of the people who recognize themselves here they are the single most valuable thing they own. The faculty that produced a complete business in a hardware store on an ordinary afternoon is the same faculty that produced the current project, and the one before that, and it will produce the next one whether or not anybody approves. A page that opens by asking you to have fewer ideas has already lost, and most of them open exactly there.

## What Does the Arrival Actually Look Like From the Inside?

Nothing like a decision, which is the first thing that has to be said about it.

You do not weigh the new idea against the current one. There is no moment of comparison, no evening spent thinking about whether to switch. What happens is much simpler. Something appears in your head, fully formed and pleasant, and the pleasantness attaches to no conclusion about anything else. It is just there, the way a piece of music you have not heard in years is just there.

The physical part is consistent enough across people to be worth describing. There is a small acceleration. Standing up becomes easier. The urge to tell somebody arrives within about an hour and is surprisingly strong, and it usually gets acted on, which matters later, because telling somebody converts a private thought into a semi-public commitment before anything at all has been checked.

The second consistent feature is the completeness. New ideas in this pattern rarely arrive as fragments. They arrive with a structure already in place, sometimes with pricing, often with the first three people to approach, usually with a name for the thing itself. People read this as evidence of quality. It is better understood as evidence about the material the idea was made of, which is a subject you already know a great deal about, minus every part of it that only shows up on contact with reality.

The third feature is the one nobody mentions, because it makes the whole thing sound less romantic. The idea arrives more often on some days than on others. Ask anybody in this pattern when their best ideas come and you will hear about walks, driving, the twenty minutes before sleep. Ask instead what the current project was demanding of them that week, and the answers cluster in a way the first question never reveals.

## Why Is the Timing So Reliable?

Because two separate things peak in the same week, and neither of them is under your control.

The first is the price of the current work. Every project has an early stretch made mostly of discovery, where each session answers a question that was genuinely open. Discovery is cheap, in the sense that it pays for itself while it happens. Somewhere after that, usually much earlier than people expect, the questions run out and what remains is execution of decisions already made. Execution costs and returns nothing except progress, which is a thinner reward and a slower one.

There is a measured tendency here worth knowing about, because it explains the pull rather than the push. In a set of experiments published in 2010, people chose freely and repeatedly between two options that differed only in how much mental effort they required. Given no other difference they took the easier one, and most of them could not say afterward that they had noticed any difference at all. Mental effort behaves like a cost in the arithmetic, and it gets avoided in the same quiet way any other cost does. I am leaving the authors unnamed on purpose. [The page on the easiest thing you never do](/patterns/putting-off-the-easy-thing/) reached that work first and names them there, and putting the same four researchers on two pages would make one line of evidence look like two.

The honest limits on that finding matter. Those were laboratory tasks measured over minutes, not four-month projects. Individual differences are large, and a substantial minority of people show the opposite preference and reliably choose the harder option. Stretching a result about card decks to a decision about your working year is my extension of it rather than anything the researchers measured. What it establishes is narrower and still useful: an increase in effort shifts the balance toward whatever is cheaper, without ever presenting itself as a choice about effort.

The second thing peaking in that same week is the availability of alternatives. There is a described model in psychology, usually called goal shielding, in which commitment to one goal actively suppresses your access to competing ones. While the commitment holds, the alternatives are simply less present in your mind. When it weakens, they become available again. I am naming the model rather than a researcher, because it belongs to a line of work rather than to one finding, and because the person most associated with it already appears elsewhere on this site for entirely different work.

Put those two together and the timing stops looking mysterious. The week the current project starts costing more than it returns is also the week your commitment to it thins slightly, which is also the week the alternatives your mind had been quietly suppressing become available again. The new idea does not interrupt anything. It walks through a door that has just opened.

That is why it feels like inspiration. The arrival is genuinely unbidden and genuinely pleasant, and nothing in the experience of it points back at the difficult stretch that made room for it. From inside, you had a good idea on a Thursday. From outside, with three dates written down, you had a good idea in the third hard week of three consecutive projects.

## What Is Shiny Object Syndrome, and What Is the Phrase Actually Worth?

It is a popular name for the visible half of this, it is accurate about that half, and it is worth considerably less than the confidence with which people use it.

The phrase describes a person who abandons the current thing whenever something newer and more interesting shows up. It travels through business and entrepreneurship writing, where it usually means switching tactic or tool or market before the last one had time to produce a result, and it has spread from there into everyday speech as a shorthand for serial enthusiasm. Both uses are recognizable. People hear it once and know immediately whether it applies to them.

Now the part that needs saying plainly, because almost no page ranking for this phrase says it. **This is a popular expression, not a finding.** There is no clinical definition of it. There is no measurement instrument, no questionnaire, no validated scale and no body of research studying it as a construct. Nobody has established how common it is, whether it is stable across a life, or whether the people it gets applied to have anything in common beyond the behavior the phrase describes. It arrived through business books and it spread because it is vivid.

It has also picked up a second life as a folk label attached to a specific medical explanation, and you will meet that version constantly if you search the phrase. That version does something different from the business one. It takes a described habit and hands it a cause, and the cause has not been demonstrated for the habit. A phrase with no research behind it cannot acquire a mechanism by being repeated next to one.

Here is what the phrase genuinely earns. It names a real and recognizable pattern of behavior. It gives people a way to raise the subject about themselves without making it a confession, which matters more than it sounds, because most people in this pattern have never said any of it out loud. Several people describe themselves with it before they can describe anything else about it, and that first sentence is frequently what makes the rest of the conversation possible. It arrives in almost exactly these words: *"It's like shiny object syndrome. And so I felt a bit scattered in that. And I'm not okay with it."* And, from somebody else entirely, *"I'm kind of just letting the shiny object syndrome just go out and keeping my focus short."* Both of those are people reaching for a name so they can start talking, which the phrase does well and which is most of what it is for.

Here is exactly where it stops. The phrase names the **what**. It has nothing to say about the **why**, and it quietly implies that no why is needed, because a syndrome sounds like an explanation on its own. Two people can drop the identical project in the identical week while doing two completely different things. One has run out of a state they need in order to work at all. One has just met a specific piece of difficulty they would rather not meet. The phrase covers both with one word and sends both toward the same useless advice, which is to want the new thing less.

| What the phrase gets right | Where it stops |
|---|---|
| Names a real, recognizable pattern of switching | Says nothing about what the switching does for the person |
| Gives people language to raise it about themselves | Implies a defect in the person rather than a condition in the week |
| Points at a genuine cost, since results need repetition | Treats the ideas as the problem, when the ideas are usually the asset |
| Travels easily, so it is easy to look up | Has no definition, no instrument and no research literature behind it |
| Fits the business complaint cleanly | Carries a medical implication it has never earned |

One more thing about the phrase, and it is the reason to handle it carefully rather than avoid it. A description said often enough starts working as an instruction. "I am a shiny object person" is a sentence about identity, and identity sentences are self-fulfilling in a way that descriptions of behavior are not. Said once, as a way of naming something, it is useful. Said as who you are, it starts doing work you never asked it to do. Somebody put it as *"I'm probably your worst person you work with because I'm, I'm very squirrel-like."* That is a verdict on a person, offered as an apology, and it is a great deal harsher than anything the behavior underneath it would support.

## When Does This Belong With Somebody Qualified Rather Than a Page Like This?

Here, before any of the practical material, because it outranks everything below it.

This page assumes a particular situation: you produce good ideas, you have carried at least one of them a long way, and the difficulty sits at a specific point rather than everywhere. Where that assumption is wrong, most of what follows will not fit, and following it will cost you time you do not need to spend.

Four situations belong somewhere else. If nothing at all has held you past the first month for a long stretch, the subject is wider than a hard week inside one project and it deserves proper attention rather than a method. If the switching sits inside a flatness that has settled over your months and has not lifted, the projects are downstream of something else. If the pattern has reached things with real consequences, so that medical appointments, legal deadlines and letters from institutions are also being left, that has moved past what a page can help with. And if the pace of new plans has started to worry people who know you well, particularly alongside changes in how you are sleeping, that is worth taking to a professional soon rather than eventually.

If there is any thought of harming yourself, that belongs with a licensed professional now rather than later. Services exist in most countries, a good number of them free, and they speak with far more people who are unsure than people who are certain.

Everything below is written for the ordinary and expensive version: a capable person with a real generator, a real record of starting well, and a reliable stopping point that nobody has ever looked at closely.

## Is the Arriving Idea a Distraction, or the Thing That Makes the Work Possible?

For a large number of people here it is the second, and getting that backwards is the most expensive mistake available on this subject.

Read the first sentence in the recognition list above again, because it contains the whole mechanism and it was said without any theory attached to it. The person is describing outreach, which is about as unrewarding as work gets. Sending a message to somebody who may not answer returns nothing on the day. There is no discovery in it, no craft, no visible progress. And the report is that they can do it while an idea is churning in their head, and cannot do it when there is no idea churning, at which point bed wins.

That is a supply statement rather than a character statement. The churn is what makes the unrewarding action possible. It runs underneath the day like a second process, and while it runs the ordinary work of the first process gets done, because something is paying you while you do it. When the churn stops, the ordinary work has to pay for itself, and most ordinary work does not.

Once you see it that way, the arrival of a new idea stops looking like defection and starts looking like resupply. The generator produces a fresh unresolved problem at precisely the moment the old one stopped being unresolved. None of that is a fault in the machinery. It is the machinery working exactly as it has always worked, and it is the reason you have ever finished anything at all.

The consequence is the part worth sitting with, because it explains a failure most people in this pattern have already lived through. The standard advice is to stop chasing new ideas and focus on one. Follow it properly and you remove the supply without replacing it, and the month that follows is usually worse than the month before it. Less gets done, not more. The person then concludes that they lack discipline, which is precisely the wrong reading of a real experiment they just ran on themselves.

The honest version of the fix is harder and duller. Keep the generator. Keep the churn. Change what the churn is attached to, so it runs on an open question inside the current project rather than on a new project entirely. That is a real move and it works for the people whose root is this one, and it is useless for the other four, which is why the next few sections separate them before anything practical happens.

## Why Does the New Idea Look Better Every Single Time?

Because you are comparing a conception with an implementation, and that comparison has only ever had one outcome.

A conception is made entirely of the interesting decisions. Where it sits, who it is for, what it is called, what makes it different, what the first version does. Those are the enjoyable parts of any project and they are the parts your mind can produce in a hardware store, because they require nothing except what you already know. Every unpleasant element is missing, and it is missing for a structural reason: unpleasant elements only appear on contact with reality, and this thing has had no contact with reality yet.

The current project, by contrast, exists in full. It has the compromise you accepted in month two, the message that has been unanswered for eleven days, the part that turned out to be harder than anybody said, and the price you still have not set. Set those against a clean conception and the conception wins every time, in every person, on every subject. The comparison is rigged, and it is rigged in a way that has nothing to do with the merits of either.

There is a second layer to this, and it explains why the new idea feels different rather than merely better. Litman published work in the mid two thousands separating two kinds of curiosity that people had been treating as one. The first is driven by interest, where new knowledge is pleasurable in itself and there is no obligation attached. The second is driven by deprivation, where there is a specific gap you need closed and the closing brings relief rather than pleasure. Both are curiosity, and they feel almost nothing alike.

That distinction maps onto the two projects with uncomfortable neatness. A new idea offers the first kind. Everything about it is open, nothing is owed, and every hour spent on it produces something you did not know. A project four months in has usually turned into the second kind. There are specific gaps, they have to be closed, closing them produces relief at best, and nobody involved is enjoying it.

The honest limits of that work are worth stating. Litman's distinction rests on self-report questionnaires, the factor structure has been argued about, and the separation is cleaner on paper than it is in the measurements underneath. Applying a distinction built for curiosity about facts to a decision about a year of your working life is my extension of it rather than something anybody tested. What it does explain, better than any account of willpower, is why the two states feel like different species of wanting.

The practical use of all this is one instruction. If you want a real comparison, compare like with like. Write out the new idea at month seven, with its own unanswered messages and its own dull assembly in it, and set that against the current project as it actually stands. Almost nobody does this, and the people who do it report that about half their arrivals survive it, which is a much higher number than the skeptical version of this page would predict.

## Why Do Two People Get the Same Idea in the Same Week for Completely Different Reasons?

Because the arrival is visible and what produced it is not, and the visible part underdetermines the rest by a wide margin.

Two people can register a domain name on the same Thursday, four months into projects of similar size, with the same amount of enthusiasm. One of them has run out of the state they need in order to work at all. The other has just been handed a piece of work they would rather not do and the idea walked in through the gap. Same behavior, same week, same sentence about it afterward, two completely different situations, and two completely different things that would help.

Here are the five roots I see producing an identical arrival, in people whose behavior is indistinguishable from outside.

| The root | What the arrival is doing | How you would know it is this one |
|---|---|---|
| The churn ran out | Restoring the unresolved problem that makes ordinary effort possible | Your output drops across everything, including things you like, whenever no idea is running |
| It is a specific exit | Getting you out of one nameable piece of work, this week | You can identify the exact task, and the idea arrived within two days of meeting it |
| The idea is simply right | Reporting an actual opportunity you have noticed and the current thing has not delivered | The reasoning survives sixteen days cold, and you can say what changed in the market or in you |
| Several live options | Keeping any single thing from ever being the thing that failed | You are strangely reluctant to close any of them, and closing one feels heavier than starting three |
| The conception was the product | Delivering the thing you actually wanted, which was the having of it | You lose interest at the same point every time, feel no real regret, and give ideas away freely |

Take the first and the fifth, since from outside they are identical. Both people generate constantly, both drop things, both describe themselves the same way. The first would be transformed by attaching the churn to an open question inside the current work, because the supply is what they were missing. The fifth would find that exercise pointless, because they got what they came for at conception and the answer for them is to build a working life where more of the time is spent conceiving and less of it is spent delivering. Hand each of them the other one's solution and you have taken a year of their life.

None of this can be read off the behavior, which is why the phrase at the top of this page cannot do the job people ask of it. A label can be looked up in a second. A root has to be found, in one person, slowly, and that is the part that actually changes anything.

## What If the Churn Is What You Have Been Running On?

Then the arrival is a supply response, and it is the most common of the five by some distance.

The signature of this root is that your capacity moves as one block. When an idea is running you are good at everything, including the parts of your life that have nothing to do with it. Messages get answered. The house is better. You go to the gym without a negotiation about it. When no idea is running, all of that drops together, and it drops on things you genuinely enjoy, which is what separates this from ordinary loss of interest in one project.

People in this root usually describe the empty weeks with some embarrassment, because from outside they look like laziness and from inside they feel like a flat tire. Nothing is wrong. Nothing hurts. There is simply no reason to stand up, and the reasons that used to work have stopped being reasons.

There is a body of work on the state underneath this that is more useful than the popular version. Eastwood, Frischen, Fenske and Smilek published a paper in 2012 arguing that boredom is best defined as the experience of wanting to be engaged and being unable to engage, which locates it in attention rather than in the activity. On that account, being bored is a report about the fit between you and what is in front of you, and the discomfort of it is the point. Elpidorou argued in 2014 that boredom functions as a push, moving people away from situations that are not engaging them and toward situations that might.

Both of those need their limits stated. The 2012 paper is a definitional and theoretical piece rather than an experiment, and the boredom research field has spent the years since arguing about which of several definitions to use. Elpidorou's is a philosophical argument about function, which fits the evidence rather than testing a prediction, and functional accounts of unpleasant states have a long history of sounding more settled than they are. What the two together establish is modest and worth having: the state that precedes your new idea is a recognized one, it has been described carefully, and describing it as a personal failing was never accurate.

What follows practically is a genuine fork. If this is your root, an ideas list will not help you, because a list is a way of storing an idea rather than a way of running one. What helps is finding an open question inside the current project that is real, that you do not know the answer to, and that is large enough to churn on. Most projects have one. It is usually the question everybody in the project has been avoiding because answering it might change the plan.

## What If the Idea Arrived to Get You Out of One Specific Thing?

Then it is targeted rather than general, and it is the easiest of the five to test.

This root produces the cleanest timing of all of them. The idea does not arrive during a slow stretch. It arrives within a day or two of a specific, nameable task appearing on your week: the price conversation, the message to the person who went quiet, the piece of work that will show you whether the last three months were a mistake. The idea shows up carrying an argument for why the current thing was the wrong direction anyway, and the argument is usually good, because you are intelligent and intelligent people produce good arguments for what they already want.

The test is simple and it is uncomfortable in a specific way that gives it away. Take the last three arrivals. Find the date of each one, which is easier than people expect because there is usually a message, a registration or a purchase attached to it. Then write down, from your week at that time, what the current project was asking of you in the two days before. If the same kind of task appears twice out of three, you have found something, and what you have found is much narrower and much more fixable than a personality.

The reason this root matters more than its share of the population is that it produces the highest quality decoys. A general loss of interest is easy to be skeptical about. An idea that arrives with a market analysis attached to it is not. The strength of the case is a poor guide here, because the case was built after the fact by somebody very good at building cases.

What helps this root is also narrow. Do the specific task first, badly if necessary, and then decide about the new idea. Not as a discipline exercise, and not because finishing everything is a virtue. Because the decision made after the task is a different decision from the one made before it, and only one of the two is worth anything. A large number of people find the new idea still looks good afterward, and start it with a clear head. A similar number find it evaporates entirely once the task is behind them, which is information they could not have bought any other way.

## What If the New Idea Is Simply Right?

Then start it, and this page has nothing to warn you about, which is a sentence most writing on this subject refuses to say.

The whole genre treats every new idea as evasion, and that framing is wrong often enough to do real damage. People change direction because they have learned something, because the market moved, because the first version was a good way to discover what the second version should be, or because the thing they set out to do turned out to be smaller than the thing next to it. That happens constantly, and a person who has read too many pages about focus will push on with a dead project for a year out of a principle they picked up from somebody selling discipline.

There is one thing worth knowing before you trust your own verdict on this, and it is not a reason to distrust it. Berg published a study in 2016 following circus performers and the executives who selected their acts, comparing forecasts of which new acts would succeed against how those acts actually performed. Creators were poor at picking their own best ideas. Managers were worse. The people who did best were creators put into the role of evaluating other people's work, which suggests the difficulty sits in the position rather than in the person.

The limits on that are real. It is a field study in one unusual industry, supported by laboratory work in the same paper, and the accuracy differences are relative rather than absolute. Nobody in any of those groups was good in any general sense. What it supports is narrow: your confidence in your own new idea carries less information than it feels like it carries, and the correction is available for free. Put the idea in front of somebody whose judgment you respect, in writing, as somebody else's proposal, and ask what they would advise. The change in your own reading when you write it that way is worth the fifteen minutes on its own.

The other correction is time. An idea that is genuinely right is still right in sixteen days. Nothing correct is damaged by a delay that short, and almost nothing that was carrying a feeling survives one. That single rule separates this root from the other four with more accuracy than any amount of introspection about motives.

## What If Having Several Live Options Is the Point?

Then the arrival is doing protective work, and the tell is what happens when you try to close one.

Three live projects mean that no single project is the thing you are doing. If one of them goes badly, it was one of three. If somebody asks how it went, there is always another one to talk about. Nothing you own can ever be the definitive test of what you can do, because the definitive test requires one thing to be the thing, and the arrangement is built specifically to prevent that.

People in this root notice something odd about themselves, usually without connecting it to anything. Starting a fourth thing is easy and closing the second one is genuinely hard, harder than the work in it would justify. Closing means the remaining two carry more weight, and weight is the thing being distributed. The relief people expect from clearing a project rarely arrives, which is why so few of them do it twice.

This root sits close to two others that have their own pages, and picking the right one saves a lot of reading. If the difficulty is a live choice between two known options that will not settle, that is [the page on not being able to decide](/patterns/not-being-able-to-decide/), and the mechanism there runs on the choosing rather than on the generating. If the thing being protected shows up mainly at the end, where a nearly finished piece of work becomes a permanent verdict, [the page on abandoning things at ninety percent](/patterns/abandoning-things-at-ninety-percent/) owns that, and it is the better fit for anybody whose stopping point is late rather than early.

The version here is earlier and quieter than either. Nothing gets abandoned dramatically. Options accumulate, each one arriving with a good reason, and the accumulation is the whole point rather than a side effect. What moves it is rarely a resolution about focus, and much more often making one thing officially the thing, out loud, to a named person, for a fixed period, and then finding out that the sky stays where it is.

## What If the Conception Was the Product?

Then you already got what you came for, and everything after it was delivery, which is a service performed for other people.

This is the most misread root of the five, because from outside it looks like every other version and from inside it feels like none of them. Some people are conceivers. The pleasure lives in the moment a shapeless thing becomes a shaped one. Ownership of the finished object is close to worthless to them. They will give an idea away in a conversation and feel nothing except pleasure that somebody is using it.

For a person like that, the arrival is the entire event. The months afterward are packaging, and there is no reason packaging should be enjoyable. Calling that a defect helps nobody, and a great many good things get conceived by people who never built them.

Two boundaries are worth drawing on this root, because it gets confused with its neighbors constantly. It is different from the version where the absorption itself is what you were after, and where the subject is interchangeable because the state is the goal. [That one is covered properly on the page about pouring everything into one thing](/patterns/pouring-it-all-into-one-thing/), which owns the total, effortful, socially admired stretch and what it is standing on. It is also different from scrapping a piece of work and rebuilding the same thing from nothing, which is [covered on the page about starting over from scratch](/patterns/starting-over-from-scratch/). The root here produces a new subject, not a cleaner version of the old one.

The honest question for this root is narrow, and it asks nothing about whether you should change. It asks whether anything on the far side of delivery is actually needed. Money is one. Reaching people is another. A record of what you can do, so that the next opportunity does not require proving it again from nothing, is a third. Where none of those is genuinely required, a life spent conceiving is a life, and it owes nobody an account of itself. Where one of them is required, the answer almost never involves learning to enjoy delivery, and almost always involves arranging for somebody else to do it, and paying them properly.

## Which of the Five Is Yours, From Evidence You Already Have Tonight?

Six questions, none of which requires you to introspect about your motives, which is the least reliable measure in the box.

1. **What happens to everything else during a stretch when no idea is running?** If your whole capacity moves as one block, including things you enjoy, look at the churn root first.
2. **Can you name the exact task that was sitting in your week when the last idea arrived?** If a specific task arrives fast and it is the same kind of task as last time, that is the exit root and it is the most fixable one here.
3. **What does closing a project feel like, compared with starting one?** If closing is disproportionately heavy, several live options are doing protective work.
4. **How do you feel about somebody else building one of your ideas?** Genuine pleasure points at conception being the product. A specific sting points somewhere else.
5. **Where in the arc do you stop, across the last five things?** Early and repeatable points at this page. Late, within sight of the end, points at [abandoning things near the finish line](/patterns/abandoning-things-at-ninety-percent/) instead.
6. **Did the last idea survive sixteen days?** If two out of three of your recent arrivals still read well cold, your generator is better than your reputation for it and the problem is structural rather than judgmental.

Most people get two answers pointing one way and one pointing somewhere else, and the honest reading is usually that two roots are running together. That is ordinary. A person can be short of churn, and be handed a specific task they do not want, in the same week, and produce one idea that is doing both jobs. What the questions buy you is a shortlist rather than a verdict.

## What Were You Doing in the Two Days Before the Idea Arrived?

This is the diagnostic, it takes about twenty minutes, and almost nobody has run it.

Everything else on this page is context. The reason this exercise carries the weight is that it uses evidence you already have, sitting in places you did not think of as records, and it produces a pattern rather than an opinion.

1. **Write down the last five ideas that pulled you off something.** Not every idea you have had. Only the ones that took real attention away from live work.
2. **Date each one.** This is easier than it sounds. There is almost always a registration, a purchase, a message to a friend, a search, a document created. Find the artifact and take the date from it.
3. **Beside each date, write what the current project was asking of you that week.** Concretely. Not "it was hard". The specific thing: a price, a message, a piece of assembly, a conversation with somebody who had gone quiet, a decision you had been carrying for a month.
4. **Beside that, write what you did instead in the following seven days.** Again concretely, and honestly, including the buying and the reading.
5. **Then read only the third column.** That is the whole exercise. Two questions. Does the same kind of task appear more than once, and is it the same kind of task each time.

Three shapes come out of this. A repeating task type, which points at the exit root and tells you exactly what to build around. A blank third column across all five, where nothing in particular was happening and the ideas arrived in genuinely quiet weeks, which points at the churn root. Or a scatter with no pattern at all, which happens, and which usually means your generator is running normally and the difficulty is in what you allow an arrival to do rather than in why it arrives.

One thing to expect. This exercise tends to feel bad for about a day, because seeing five dates lined up against five difficult tasks is heavier than carrying the same information in pieces. That heaviness is the pattern becoming visible, and visible is more workable than diffuse.

## Why Does Writing Ideas Down Settle Some People and Make Others Worse?

Because a list stores an idea, and storing is the right answer for two of the five roots and the wrong one for the other three.

The advice is everywhere and it is usually given as though it were universal. Keep a list. Write the idea down the moment it arrives, promise yourself you will look at it later, and go back to work. One of the recognition lines at the top of this page is somebody describing exactly that habit, in their own words, with a small book for the purpose.

For the exit root it works well, and it works for a reason worth naming. If the idea arrived to get you out of one specific piece of work, its job was to occupy the space that task was about to fill. Writing it down gives it somewhere to sit and takes about a minute, after which the specific task is still there, which is uncomfortable and correct. People with this root often report that the list feels like it is working, and it is.

For the churn root the same instruction makes the week worse and nobody warns them. Writing an idea down deliberately stops it running. That is the entire purpose of the technique. If the running was what made your ordinary work possible, you have just switched off the supply in order to protect a project that now has nothing driving it. Two weeks later there is a longer list and less output, and the conclusion drawn is that the person failed at a simple method.

For the several-options root a list quietly becomes a sixth live thing, since nothing on it ever gets closed. For the conception root a list is genuinely satisfying, because it is a record of the product, and it should be kept without any expectation that it will change behavior.

| If your root is | A list will | What to do instead or as well |
|---|---|---|
| The churn ran out | Remove the supply and lower your output | Find the real open question inside the current project and run on that |
| It is a specific exit | Work, and work quickly | Write it down, then do the specific task the same day |
| The idea is simply right | Do nothing either way | Put a seventeen-day gap on it and let the idea prove itself |
| Several live options | Become another open loop | Close one thing officially, out loud, before adding anything |
| The conception was the product | Feel good and change nothing | Keep it as a record, and hand delivery to somebody who wants it |

The general point underneath the table is the one this whole page is built on. A method that works is a method matched to what is actually happening, and there is no method for the pattern in general, because the pattern in general has five different things underneath it. Being handed the wrong one and failing at it is how most people in this position arrive at a low opinion of themselves.

## Where Does This Hide When You Would Not Call It an Idea?

In six places, and most people recognize themselves in two or three before noticing the rest.

| Where it shows up | What it looks like from outside | The timing that gives it away |
|---|---|---|
| Work and business | A second venture beginning while the first is mid-build, complete with a name and a logo | The week the first one needed a price defended or a hard call made |
| The house | A renovation plan that changes shape at the point of the dull work, so the loft becomes the new project | The weekend the boiler, the roof or the paperwork came due |
| Training | A new sport or a new program starting in the boring month of the last one | Two weeks after progress stopped being visible week to week |
| Learning | A fourth language, a fifth course, a new instrument, all begun well | The week the previous one required speaking to a stranger or being marked |
| Money | A new savings structure, a new platform, a new approach, researched thoroughly | Shortly after a plan required twelve boring months of the same action |
| People | A new group, a new circle, a new professional community | Around the point the existing one required a difficult conversation |

The learning row is the most reasonably defended and the most worth examining, because everything on it is genuinely good for you. Nobody could reasonably call starting a fourth language a failure. It becomes something else when the start reliably lands in the week the third one asked you to be a beginner out loud in front of a person.

The money row is the one people never connect to the others. A new approach to saving or investing, arriving with real research behind it, looks nothing like a shiny object. The tell is the same. Look at what the old approach was asking of you, which in most cases was twelve months of doing nothing interesting.

The people row is the one to handle carefully, and this page will not go far into it. A relationship is a setting a personal pattern shows up in rather than a project with a next version. If the timing pattern shows up around people as well as around work, that is worth knowing about yourself. It is not a reason to start evaluating anybody in your life as an idea.

## Is "This One Is Actually Better" an Explanation or an Excuse?

Both are available, they use identical words, and what separates them is what you do in the following minute.

Giving something an explanation is useful and frequently true. Turning that explanation into a story you tell yourself and other people, so that you have a reason and never have to face the real issue underneath, is a different use of the same sentence. The distinction is worth more than anything else in this section, and it applies to a great many things beyond this page.

"This one is actually better" can be an entirely accurate report. You looked, you compared like with like, the new thing genuinely has more in it, and you can say what changed. That explanation ends in a decision. You close the old thing deliberately, say so to somebody, and start the new one with the count reset on purpose rather than by drift.

The same sentence used as a story has a signature. It arrives fast, before any checking. It gets slightly more polished each time you say it. It closes the subject rather than opening it, and you notice a small reluctance when somebody asks a follow-up question about the old project. And the old project never gets closed, it just stops, which is the detail that gives the whole thing away, because a genuinely better idea makes closing the worse one easy.

The test is simple and slightly uncomfortable. Ask what would have to be true for the explanation to be wrong, and then notice whether you want to look. An explanation survives that question. A story cannot, which is why it comes with a small flinch attached to it.

None of this is a reason to be hard on yourself. The stories exist because they work, and they work because they are usually partly true. Noticing which one you are running is the entire task here, and nobody is keeping score.

## What Does This Cost, and When Does the Bill Arrive?

Four costs, and the largest of them sits somewhere people never look.

The first is that nothing accumulates. Almost every field pays on repetition, where the same action performed for long enough starts producing results the early version of it could not. Four live things divide your attention finely enough to keep all four alive and never enough to get any of them past the point where the returns start. The hours were often enjoyable and the learning was real. What is missing is the compounding, and compounding is where most of the value in any craft lives.

The second is a reputation that quietly diverges from the truth. People stop asking about the thing from the spring. They learn, kindly and without saying anything, that your announcements are interesting and your deliveries are uncertain, and they adjust what they bring to you accordingly. Nobody is angry. The offers just arrive somewhere else.

The third is one people report as the surprising one. The ideas you dropped were frequently good, and a decent number of them were not built by anybody else either. Watching a version of your own conception appear five years later, made by somebody with a fraction of your ability and all of their attention, is a specific and unpleasant experience, and the people it happens to describe it in almost identical words.

The fourth cost is the private one and it accumulates the fastest. Each unstarted or half-built thing quietly strengthens a sentence about what you are like, and the sentence gets applied in advance to the next arrival. That is why the ninth idea starts with less conviction than the third, and why people this far into the pattern become gradually more reluctant to say out loud what they are working on. The generator is still producing at full rate. What has changed is that the person no longer believes their own enthusiasm, which is a hard way to live and it is entirely fixable.

## Where Does This Stop and Something Else Begin?

Several patterns sit close to this one, and picking the right page saves a great deal of reading.

| If this is the closer description | Go here instead |
|---|---|
| The stop happens near the end, on a thing that is nearly finished | [Abandoning things at ninety percent](/patterns/abandoning-things-at-ninety-percent/) |
| Preparation, reading and buying stand in for the first step | [Researching instead of starting](/patterns/researching-instead-of-starting/) |
| Two known options are live and neither will settle | [Not being able to decide](/patterns/not-being-able-to-decide/) |
| One thing takes everything while something else in your life is wrong | [Pouring it all into one thing](/patterns/pouring-it-all-into-one-thing/) |
| The same thing gets scrapped and rebuilt from nothing, repeatedly | [Starting over from scratch](/patterns/starting-over-from-scratch/) |
| The undone item is small and easy rather than large and interesting | [The easiest thing you never do](/patterns/putting-off-the-easy-thing/) |

The boundary worth stating explicitly is with the first one, because the two get confused constantly and a large number of people have both. That page starts at the drop, where a thing at ninety percent gets put down because finishing it would convert potential into a permanent verdict. Everything there is about the end of the arc. This page starts much earlier, at the first hard stretch, and the new idea shows up while the current project is still open and still being worked on. If your stops cluster near the finish line, that page is yours. If your stops cluster in month four, this one is.

The second boundary is with preparation. Buying the tool, reading the material and building the plan are a whole pattern in themselves, with their own roots, and they follow the arrival here rather than causing it. If most of your time goes into getting ready rather than into generating, the other page is the better fit and it will be more use.

## Why Does Deciding to Focus Never Survive the Month?

Because a decision changes what you intend and leaves untouched every condition that produces the arrival.

This is worth being precise about, because most people reading this have made the decision several times and drawn a conclusion about themselves from how it went. The resolution is genuine. It usually holds for two or three weeks, sometimes longer. Then a hard stretch arrives in the current project, an idea shows up, and the resolution turns out to have no purchase on any of it.

Nothing failed there except the theory. The difficult work is still difficult. The generator is still generating, and it should be. The state you were running on either got replaced or it did not, and a resolution does not replace it. Deciding to want something less has a poor record against anything, and it has a particularly poor record against a faculty that has been rewarding you since you were young.

There is also a second effect worth watching for, because it appears somewhere unrelated and nobody connects it. Force a month of pure focus onto a person whose supply was the churn and the pressure comes out sideways. It shows up as a stretch of doing nothing at all, or as an enormous vague new plan that never reaches a first step, or as an irritability at home that nobody links to a project.

None of that is an argument against pushing through a hard stretch. Push through it. One delivered thing teaches you more about your own pattern than a year of reading about it. What it is not is evidence that anything has moved, and expecting it to be evidence is how people end up more discouraged after a success than they were before it.

The version that holds is duller. Change a condition rather than a preference. Attach the churn to a real open question inside the current work. Put a fixed gap between arrival and action. Make one thing officially the thing, to a named person, for a fixed period. None of those requires wanting the new idea any less, which is why they survive contact with a hardware store on a Thursday.

## What Does the Version That Holds Look Like on an Ordinary Week?

Smaller and less dramatic than people expect, and recognizable in about four details.

It looks like ideas arriving at exactly the same rate as before. That is the first thing people get wrong about the resolved version, and the mistake puts them off attempting it. Nobody in this pattern becomes a person with fewer ideas, and nobody should want to.

It looks like every arrival getting written down with a date on it, and the date being the useful part. The date is what turns a pile of enthusiasms into a record you can read a pattern from, and reading that pattern once a season is most of the maintenance this needs.

It looks like a fixed gap between an idea arriving and anything being spent on it. Sixteen days is the number this page keeps using, and the exact figure matters less than the fact that it is fixed in advance and applies to everything, including the ones that feel different. The ones that feel different are the reason to have the rule.

It looks like one thing being officially the thing, known to at least one other person, for a stated period. Not a vow of focus. A stated period, with an end, after which the choice is genuinely open again.

And it looks like a much quieter relationship with your own enthusiasm. The idea still arrives in the middle of an unrelated errand and it still feels good, and it stops functioning as a verdict on whatever you were doing the day before. Most people describe that shift as relief rather than as discipline, and the phrase they use about themselves usually stays the same.

## How Good Is the Evidence on This Page?

Mixed, and the parts of it that are weak are worth naming precisely, because the strong parts do not need the weak ones.

The label at the top has no research behind it at all. Shiny object syndrome has no clinical definition, no scale, no prevalence figure and no literature studying it as a construct. Everything on this page about the phrase is a description of how the phrase is used, not a report of anything measured.

On effort as a cost, the demand selection experiments were published in 2010 in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, and the authors are named on [the page about the easiest thing you never do](/patterns/putting-off-the-easy-thing/) rather than here, for the reason given above. Those were short laboratory choices, individual differences were large, and a meaningful minority prefer the harder option. Applying it to a four-month project is my extension of a finding about card decks.

On boredom, Eastwood, Frischen, Fenske and Smilek published their definitional account in 2012 in Perspectives on Psychological Science, and it is a theoretical paper rather than an experiment. Elpidorou published the functional argument in 2014 in Frontiers in Psychology, and it is philosophy about what boredom is for rather than a tested prediction. The field has spent the decade since arguing about which definition to use, and I have taken the version that fits what people describe, which is a defensible choice and still a choice.

On the two kinds of curiosity, Litman published the interest and deprivation distinction in the mid two thousands. It rests on self-report questionnaires and the factor structure has been disputed. On forecasting your own ideas, Berg published the circus study in 2016 in Administrative Science Quarterly, and its accuracy differences are relative rather than absolute, in one unusual industry, supported by laboratory work in the same paper by the same author, which makes it one line of evidence rather than two.

Goal shielding is named here as a described model rather than as one person's finding, deliberately. It has real experimental work behind it and it is also the kind of model that gets stated with more confidence than the measurements support.

Everything else here is mine and carries no research weight at all. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, and I am not a therapist. None of the researchers named has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my reading of it disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.

## How Long Does This Take to Change, Honestly?

Longer than a resolution and shorter than you fear, and it moves in completed cycles rather than in weeks.

Seeing it takes one evening. With five dates written against five difficult tasks the shape is usually obvious, and the specific root becomes plausible inside a week of paying attention. That stage is genuinely frustrating, because you can watch the whole mechanism operating and still find yourself registering a domain name on the way home.

Changing it moves at the speed of finished cycles. Most people need two or three deliveries before a hard stretch stops reading as a signal to leave, and small counts fully. Something delivered in nineteen days does the same work as something delivered in nineteen months, which is the single most useful piece of news in this section, because it means the training is available immediately and cheaply.

It also comes back, and it comes back first on the projects that matter most, which is the ordinary shape of this rather than evidence that nothing took. A pattern that supplies something returns whenever the supply runs low, and the projects you care about are the ones that go through the longest unrewarding stretches.

Worth saying plainly: understanding this page changes very little on its own. Self-knowledge is useful and it also puts real pressure on you if you do not act on it. Reading a good explanation of why the ideas arrive when they do, and then adding this page to the list of things you understood and did nothing about, is the pattern operating on the page itself.

## What Is Worth Doing This Week, Before You Start Anything New?

Two things, and together they cost about an hour.

Run the five-date exercise tonight. Last five ideas, dates against them, what the current project was asking of you in the two days before each one, and then read only that column. Twenty minutes, no decisions, no starting anything and no closing anything. The point is to find out whether the same kind of task keeps appearing, because that answer decides which of the five roots you are working with and every practical move on this page depends on it.

Then put a fixed gap on the next arrival. Sixteen days between the idea landing and anything being spent on it, including the domain name, including telling people. Write the idea out properly on the day it arrives, with its first three steps and its first genuinely dull task in it, and then leave it alone. Reading it cold at the end of the gap is the cheapest test available and almost nobody has ever run it on themselves.
