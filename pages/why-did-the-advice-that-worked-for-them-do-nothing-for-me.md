# Why does the advice that changed someone else do nothing for me?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because a piece of advice is engineered for one cause, and two people can run the identical behavior for opposite reasons. The fix that changed your friend was built for their driver, their depth, and the way they take in an instruction. Aimed at yours, it produces full effort and no movement, which then gets misread as a verdict about you.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
These are the sentences that run privately, usually late, usually a few weeks after another honest attempt has quietly gone nowhere.

- "I know exactly what this pattern is and where it comes from, and I did it again last night."
- "I have done a lot of work on myself. I have not changed much."
- "I am not going to try something else and find out it does not work either."
- "I got what I said I wanted and it did not feel like anything."

What those add up to is a person who has stopped running experiments on themselves and started running a verdict instead, which is the most expensive thing on this page.

[How a seminar week locates the cause before it hands anybody a fix](https://florianrolke.com/the-soulsearching-experience/)
</div>

The book is still on the table where you left it three weeks ago, with most of the second chapter underlined. Your friend read the same book in March, changed something visible by May, and has mentioned it twice since without meaning anything by it. You did the work properly, on the days you said you would. That is the part that stays with you, because doing it properly was supposed to be the whole variable.

Most people reach a question like this having already tried the two available explanations and found both of them useless. The first is that the advice was empty, which falls apart the moment you remember watching it work on somebody you know well. The second is that you did not want it enough, which falls apart against three weeks of getting up early for something you had no appetite for. Something more specific is running underneath, it has a shape, and you can check it against your own history of failed attempts this afternoon.

One plain line before any of the rest, because this page cannot be the right thing for everybody who lands on it. If you are in crisis, if hopelessness has not lifted for months, if there is an addiction in the picture, or if you are having any thought of harming yourself however briefly it passes, that belongs with a licensed professional today, ahead of anything written here.

## What Does This Look Like on an Ordinary Week?

Undramatic, repetitive, and almost impossible to describe to anybody who asks how things are going.

- Reading the book that visibly changed a friend's life and closing it feeling nothing shift.
- Following someone's exact morning routine for three straight weeks and ending the month in the same place.
- Writing the same insight in a journal for the fifth time, in almost the same words as the first time.
- Watching a coworker try the trick that fixed your own procrastination and seeing it do absolutely nothing for them.
- Sitting in a group call while someone else's breakthrough visibly lands, and the exact same sentence leaves you cold.
- Doing everything the podcast said, in the stated order, and still ending the week exactly where you started.

Read that list looking for the moment somebody was lazy. There is no such moment, and its absence is the reason this goes unexamined for years. A person who never tries has an obvious next step available to them. A person who has tried six times, properly, has no obvious next step at all, because the only variable they know how to adjust is effort, and effort has already been spent.

The fourth item on that list is the one worth sitting with longest. Almost everybody has been on both ends of this. You have watched a fix fail on somebody else while it was still visibly working on you, and you did not conclude anything terrible about them. You assumed something about their situation was different. That assumption is correct, it is available in the other direction, and almost nobody applies it to themselves.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, near the top, because this boundary matters more than every practice further down put together.

If a low mood has not lifted for months, if you are managing a condition with a clinician already, if there is an addiction in the picture for you or somebody close to you, or if any thought of harming yourself has been showing up, none of that gets solved by a sharper read of your own patterns, and it deserves a professional now rather than after another experiment. The same holds if the reason nothing you try ever sticks is that somebody else controls your time, your money, or your movements, since no read you run on your own behavior can reach a situation like that.

And if a difficulty of this kind has been persistent, heavy, or present for as long as you can remember, it is worth raising with a doctor or another qualified professional, because this page is about the pattern and carries no opinion on any medical question.

Everything below is written for the ordinary version: the routine that lasted nineteen days, the framework that made total sense and changed nothing, the fifth journal entry saying what the first one said. Asking for help in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve.

## Where Does Advice Aim, and Where Does the Problem Actually Live?

Advice aims at the layer everybody can see, which is the last one in the chain and rarely the one holding the problem in place.

Underneath any stuck behavior there are four layers, and they nest. At the bottom sits a driver, one of a handful of things every person runs on: certainty, variety, mattering, connection, growth, contribution. Above that sits a value, the state you learned at some point would deliver on that driver. Above the value sits a rule, the specific and often oddly arbitrary condition that has to be met before the value counts as satisfied. And on top, visible to everybody, sits the vehicle, the actual behavior you use to try to satisfy the rule.

| The layer | What it is | What it sounds like from inside | What advice usually says to it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The driver | One of a handful of things everybody runs on | Rarely anything at all. It runs underneath language | Almost nothing, since it cannot be observed from outside |
| The value | The state you learned would deliver on the driver | "I need to be on top of things" | "Have you thought about what you actually want?" |
| The rule | The condition that has to be met before the value counts | "It only counts if nobody had to wait on me and I did it without help" | Almost nothing, because the rule has usually never been said out loud |
| The vehicle | The visible behavior you use to satisfy the rule | "I will start once the plan is clear" | "Wake up earlier." "Set a boundary." "Just start." |

All the attention collects in the bottom row, and it collects there for an honest reason. The vehicle is the only layer anybody can watch, including you. Your friend can see that you postpone things. Your partner can see that you work late. You can see it too, which is why your own description of the problem is almost always a description of a vehicle. So the advice arrives pitched at a vehicle, and it is aimed at the one layer where nothing is actually stuck.

That explains something readers usually experience as a personal failure. An instruction to the vehicle works beautifully when the vehicle really is the problem, which happens often enough to keep the whole genre in business. It does nothing whatsoever when a rule two layers down is quietly disqualifying every version of success you could reach. [The drivers themselves, one by one, with what each of them sounds like when it goes unfed, are laid out on their own page](/patterns/six-human-needs/).

## What Does It Sound Like From the Inside?

Like a person conducting an ongoing internal case against themselves, using evidence they gathered honestly.

These are the sentences that come up when somebody has been at this a long time and has begun to draw conclusions:

- "I do not think I can change this. I have looked at it from every angle already."
- "I know exactly what I would tell someone else in my position, and I am still doing the other thing."
- "It is my own fault. I am lazy. I am not a good person."
- "I am fine, mostly. There is just this thing underneath that never quite goes away."

Read them slowly, and notice the second one in particular. Somebody who can see a friend's version of this clearly, name the cause, and prescribe the right move, while going home and doing the opposite themselves, has proved something important about their own situation without noticing. Whatever is holding their pattern in place has nothing to do with a shortage of understanding, since the understanding is demonstrably present and working fine on somebody else's life.

The third sentence does a different kind of damage. Lazy is a verdict about a person, and verdicts close investigations. Once the case is settled, the only remaining move is to try harder at the same layer, which produces another failure, which strengthens the verdict. That loop can run for a decade on nothing but sincerity, and everybody inside it is working hard.

## What Do Two Opposite Causes Look Like Inside One Complaint?

Like two people describing the identical week in identical words while needing opposite things from it.

Take chronic postponement of a big, important project. Two people both have it. The first has read everything on the subject; a coworker's step-by-step planning template, which strips ambiguity out of the first move, finally does it for them. The second person has already tried and abandoned two nearly identical systems. For the first person the delay was a certainty problem: an undefined plan feels riskier to begin than to postpone, so removing the ambiguity removes the block. For the second, the delay was buying something else entirely. Starting late keeps a ready-made excuse in reserve, and a project that begins fully defined and on time removes that excuse and raises the stakes of being seen to fail. The template makes the second person's avoidance stronger, and it does so precisely because it works as designed.

What moved it for the second person had nothing to do with planning. It was separating the outcome of the project from what a poor outcome would mean about them, which removed the thing the delay was protecting in the first place.

Now take a much more common complaint: always working, cannot rest. Two people, same sentence, same visible calendar. Generic advice arrives, take a real day off, block the time. The first person takes the day and feels worse: restless, faintly threatened by the empty hours. The second takes the day, enjoys it, and finds the exhaustion back within a week. For the first, one object, the business, has been carrying certainty, mattering and connection all at once, so an empty day is a live threat to three drivers simultaneously rather than a rest. For the second, two inherited commitments are colliding, provide relentlessly and be fully present at home, and a single day off satisfies neither, so it relocates the guilt without touching the collision generating the tiredness.

| The complaint, word for word | One version | The opposite version | What separates them | What each one actually needs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "I keep putting the big thing off" | Starting feels unsafe while the plan is undefined | A late start keeps an excuse in reserve | Whether a fully specified plan makes it lighter or heavier | A first move small enough to remove ambiguity, or the outcome separated from what it would say about you |
| "I am always working and cannot rest" | One object carrying three drivers at once | Two commitments that cannot both be honored | Whether an empty day feels threatening or merely guilty | A second vehicle built for each driver, or both rules said out loud so the collision becomes visible |
| "I cannot say no to anybody" | Being needed is where connection actually arrives | An old rule about what refusing makes you | Whether saying no feels lonely or feels shameful | Another reliable source of connection, or the rule examined and rewritten |
| "I lose interest around week three" | The driver was variety, and the novelty has run out | The finish line is close enough to deliver a verdict | Whether the drop-off is boredom or a tightening in the chest | Variety engineered into the middle, or the verdict defused before the deadline |
| "I sabotage things right as they go well" | Success threatens a rule about who you are allowed to become | Success removes a familiar source of drama | Whether the wreck feels like relief or feels like coming home | The rule surfaced, or an honest replacement for the intensity |

Every row on that table is one complaint with two engines. The advice you were given picked one column, silently, and if you happened to be in the other one it was always going to do nothing. That is worth reading as good news, since a fix that produces no movement has told you which column you are in.

## What Does Stress Reveal That a Calm Self-Report Cannot?

Which two drivers are actually running you, which is a different answer from the one you would give on a quiet Sunday.

Everybody has all of them. What varies is which two dominate, and the dominant pair forecasts a surprising amount: what will reliably stress you, the characteristic shape of your failures, and the specific objection you will raise to any change somebody proposes. The trouble is that the pair does not show up in a self-report. Asked calmly, people answer with their values, meaning what they have concluded is admirable to want, and there is nothing dishonest about that answer. It just describes a different thing.

Pressure removes the room to perform, so pressure is where the read gets taken. Think about the last genuinely bad week you had. What did you reach for first when it started going wrong? What did you protect when time ran out and something had to give? Which loss, in the middle of all of it, would have been unbearable rather than merely bad? Those three answers usually name the pair between them, and they name it more accurately than a questionnaire ever could.

There is a second, faster tell. Consider what you get most defensive about. A driver going unfed produces disproportionate heat around the exact subject nobody is supposed to notice, which is why the sentence that made you bristle in a conversation last month is a better piece of data than anything you concluded about yourself afterward.

Two things this read does not do. It does not hand you a type, and a pair that describes you this year may describe you differently after a decade or a bereavement or a child. And it does not tell you what to do, only where to aim. Everything past that point is a question of depth.

## At What Depth Does Your Version Actually Sit?

Somewhere in one of five places, and a repair aimed at the wrong one produces full effort with zero movement.

This is the part that separates the same named problem in two different people. Procrastination, no discipline, low self-esteem, cannot commit: each of those labels can sit at any of five depths, and each depth takes an entirely different repair.

| Where it can live | What it looks like from inside | How fast it moves | What a repair aimed here looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| The driver structure | The behavior snaps straight back the moment real stress arrives, however sincere the attempt | Slowest. Months | Building something that genuinely delivers what the old behavior was delivering, before removing it |
| A single rule | One oddly specific condition disqualifying everything you achieve | Fast, sometimes within a week of naming it | Saying the rule out loud in one sentence, then writing a version you could actually meet |
| Your state in the moment | The same reaction fires on the same trigger and passes within the hour | Fast enough to interrupt in seconds | Changing what your body is doing, where your attention is pointed, and the words you are using, while it happens |
| The feeling you default to | Everything gets read through the same emotion regardless of what happened that day | Slow, and it improves unevenly | Noticing the return trip and interrupting it repeatedly, over months |
| Habit and environment | It only happens in one room, at one time, with one set of cues present | Immediate, and slightly embarrassing | Changing the cue, the room, or the sequence, and nothing psychological at all |

Two errors follow from this table, and they run in opposite directions.

The first is aiming too deep. Most popular advice defaults to the most effortful layer available, identity, childhood, a full rewiring, because depth sells and because it sounds serious. Applied to a problem that is genuinely one unexamined rule or one unmanaged cue, that burns months of motivation on an intervention the situation never called for. Somebody spends a year on the origins of their procrastination when the actual cause was a phone on the desk and a first step nobody had defined.

The second is aiming too shallow. A behavioral tip gets applied to something living in the driver structure. It produces four or five days of compliance, then reverts the moment the underlying driver reasserts itself, and the reversion gets filed as relapse rather than as a mismatch. Both errors produce the identical feeling, which is sincere effort and no result. [Whether your version is a discipline problem at all, and what else it could be, is worked through in more detail on its own page](/patterns/is-this-a-discipline-problem-or-something-else/).

The useful move here is boringly simple. Take the last three fixes that failed you and write down, for each one, which of those five rows it was aimed at. Most people find all three landed in the same row, and that row is usually the one nothing of theirs lives in.

## What Happens When a Right Answer Arrives in the Wrong Wrapper?

It gets refused on contact, and the refusal then gets filed as further proof that nothing works.

A correct point pushed through the wrong filter reads as pressure. People take in instructions differently, and the differences are stable enough to predict. Some move toward what they want and some move away from what they fear. Some need to know a thing is possible and some need to know it is necessary. Some check an answer against their own judgment and some check it against what other people have found. Some are looking for what matches their existing view and some are scanning immediately for what is missing from it.

| The identical, honest sentence | Who it lands for | Who it repels | What is actually happening |
|---|---|---|---|
| "This is simple, and a lot of people are already doing it with real results" | Someone who needs the risk to come down | Someone who needs to be distinct | The second person hears that doing it would make them nothing special |
| "This is hard, and most people who try it drop out" | Someone who needs a challenge worth their time | Someone already low on confidence | The second person hears a prediction about themselves |
| "Here is the research, and here is what it found" | Someone who checks against external evidence | Someone who checks against their own read | The second person hears an instruction to outsource their judgment |
| "Just try it and see what happens" | Someone who moves toward possibility | Someone who moves away from risk | The second person hears an unbounded exposure with no exit |
| "If you carry on like this, here is where it ends" | Someone who moves when the cost gets clear | Someone already avoiding | The second person adds it to the pile they are avoiding |

Nothing in the left-hand column is dishonest, and no sentence there is wrong. Every one of them fails for somebody, entirely on delivery, which means a failed attempt tells you very little about whether the underlying idea was any good.

There is a public version of this idea that has been around for decades. In the early 1980s, working with people trying to change heavy drinking, William Miller began describing what the field then called resistance as something produced in the room between two people rather than as a trait belonging to the person who was stuck. He and Stephen Rollnick built motivational interviewing around that observation and later dropped the word resistance from their own vocabulary in favor of terms that locate the friction in the interaction. You do not have to accept the whole approach to take the useful piece: when a good instruction produces a shove-back, the shove-back is information about how it arrived.

This is also why you can agree with something completely and still watch yourself do nothing about it. Agreement happens in the argument. Reception happens somewhere else, and the two are not required to match.

## Does Matching Advice to the Person Actually Work, or Is That Marketing?

Both, and the honest split matters: matching to readiness and to what somebody actually values has real support, while matching people to fixed styles has a poor record and gets sold hardest.

Start with the part that failed. The popular idea that people have learning styles, and that teaching should be matched to them, was examined in a 2008 review by Harold Pashler and colleagues in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*. They found that almost none of the studies had run the test the claim requires, and that the few properly designed ones mostly contradicted it. The broader research on matching instruction to individual aptitudes has a similar history. Lee Cronbach set out the ambition in his 1957 address to the American Psychological Association and returned to it nearly two decades later to say plainly that the reliable interactions had been much harder to find than anyone hoped. Any page promising you a clean personal profile that predicts which technique will work should be read against that record, this one included.

Now the part that held up. In 1967 Gordon Paul framed the question that clinical research has been chewing on ever since: what treatment, delivered by whom, is most effective for this individual, with that specific problem, under which set of circumstances. The framing survived because the answer turned out to matter. James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente, studying smokers who had quit without help, found that where somebody sat on a path from not-yet-considering through to actively-changing predicted whether a technically sound intervention produced movement or a shove-back. Offer an action-stage tool to somebody who has not yet decided anything and it reliably fails, however good the tool.

Further back, Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess, following the same children from infancy in the New York Longitudinal Study begun in the 1950s, arrived at goodness of fit: the identical parenting approach produces good outcomes with one child's temperament and poor ones with another's, without either approach being better in itself. That finding is the cleanest independent precedent for what this page claims. Identical input, opposite outcome, explained by fit rather than by the quality of the input or the character of whoever received it.

So the flat verdict, stated as plainly as it deserves. Fit is real and it is not a personality report. What it depends on is where you currently are, what the behavior is currently buying you, and how much of it you have already decided. Those things are checkable against your own history, which is what makes them worth checking.

## Why Does the Fifth Book Feel Like Progress?

Because learning can quietly become its own vehicle, and it happens to be an unusually efficient one.

A new framework delivers growth, which feels like movement. It delivers certainty, since a clear model of your problem is enormously calming. And for anybody who reads seriously, it delivers a bit of mattering as well, because understanding yourself well is a respectable thing to be good at. Three drivers, one behavior, none of it requiring you to do anything different on Tuesday.

That is why the fifth journal entry can say almost exactly what the first one said. The entry is doing its job. It is producing the felt sense of progress on schedule, and the felt sense is genuine rather than fake. What it has never done is get tested against a real week, which is a separate operation with a much lower hit rate and considerably less pleasant weather.

None of this means stop reading. It means that understanding locates the problem, and locating something is the step before doing anything about it. A week that produces an accurate account of your pattern and no change in what you do has produced half of something. [What actually stands between an accurate insight and a changed life has its own page](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/), and it is worth reading directly after this one, since the two failures sit next to each other and get confused constantly.

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because six different situations produce the same feeling of trying hard and getting nowhere, and only some of them are the one this page describes.

| What it feels like | What it might actually be | The thing that tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing I try works | A correct fix aimed at the wrong depth | It produced a real shift for a few days, then reverted cleanly |
| Nothing I try works | Advice that was simply thin | It produced nothing from day one, no shift at all, not even briefly |
| I keep rejecting good advice | A correct point arriving through the wrong filter | You agree with it completely and still feel a shove-back you cannot explain |
| I have no discipline | Two commitments colliding | Constant tiredness with no single activity you can point at as the cause |
| I never follow through | Unmanaged environment and cues | It only ever happens in one room, at one time of day, with one thing within reach |
| I am fundamentally different from other people | Three or four mismatches in a row | Every failed attempt was aimed at the same layer, and you had never checked which |

The first two rows are the pair worth learning to tell apart, because they lead to opposite next moves. A mismatched fix is worth keeping in the drawer, since it will suit something later. Thin advice is worth putting down entirely. The distinguishing question takes ten seconds: did anything at all move, even for two days?

The last row deserves its own note. Being genuinely unusual is possible, and it is a much rarer explanation than a run of misfires, which is the most ordinary thing in the world. Almost everybody who reaches that conclusion reaches it after aiming at the same layer repeatedly, without ever having considered that a layer was something you could choose.

## Where Does Understanding Stop and Excusing Begin?

At the point where the explanation starts doing the work the action was supposed to do.

There is a real risk in everything above, and it deserves naming rather than hiding. A page that tells you the failures were about aim rather than about character can be picked up as permission: it was never my fault, my situation is complicated, the fixes were built for other people. Every one of those sentences can be completely true and still function as an exit.

The distinction that holds is between the cause and the responsibility. Where your pattern came from, what it is currently buying you, and why the last four fixes missed are all matters of cause, and none of them is a moral question. What you do on Thursday is a separate matter and it is yours. Understanding why you postpone things does not postpone them for you.

You cannot hate yourself into a different life, and the attempt has an efficiency problem quite apart from its cruelty. A verdict closes the investigation, and a closed investigation stops producing information. Somebody who concludes they are lazy has exactly one move left, which is more effort at the same layer, and they have already tried that. Somebody who concludes they aimed wrong has five candidate layers, a history of failed attempts to sort against them, and something to do this week.

Accepting where you are turns out to be the practical position rather than the soft one. It leaves you able to look at the last three years honestly, which is where all the evidence you need is currently sitting.

## What Actually Moves It?

Running the read before choosing, or re-trying, any fix, and doing it on your own history rather than on a description of the problem.

Nothing below is a validated protocol, and anybody offering you one for this is overselling something. What follows is built out of how the mismatch happens, and it is designed so that you can run it alone, on paper, in about twenty minutes, using material you already have.

1. **List the last three fixes that failed you, by name.** The routine, the framework, the app, the book, the promise you made in January. Be specific and include how long each one lasted, in days, before it stopped.
2. **For each one, write down whether anything moved at all.** Even two good days count. This single column separates a thin fix from a mismatched one, and it is the fastest useful sort available to you.
3. **Name the vehicle you were actually working on.** Almost always this is the sentence you would use to describe the problem to a friend. Write it down and label it as the visible layer, so it stops standing in for the cause.
4. **Take the driver read from a bad week rather than a calm hour.** What you reached for first, what you protected when time ran out, what would have been unbearable to lose. Two answers, roughly, and expect them to be slightly unflattering.
5. **Ask what the behavior is currently paying you.** Something is arriving on time, every time, or the pattern would have collapsed years ago. Protection, relief, certainty, an excuse held in reserve, a reason to matter. Write the payment down in one plain sentence.
6. **Place it in one of the five depths.** Driver structure, a rule, your state in the moment, the feeling you default to, or habit and environment. Guessing is allowed. A wrong guess that gets tested beats a right guess that never does.
7. **Check the delivery on the fix you are about to try.** If the framing reads as pressure, an insult, or an unbounded risk, rewrite it in your own words before you attempt it. The content stays. The wrapper is yours to change.
8. **Pick one repair matched to the depth, and give it a bounded run with a date on it.** Three weeks, or six, decided in advance, with a note in your calendar for the day you assess it honestly.

Is any of that comfortable? No. Reading back three failed attempts in your own handwriting, with the dates attached, takes real, freaking discipline, and it is a very different discipline from the one everybody keeps recommending, which is more effort at the layer that already failed you.

Two honest caveats. This is not a way to make thin advice work, and some of what you were given genuinely was thin. And the read itself takes real time: locating the depth can happen in one sitting, while confirming that a matched repair actually produces movement takes weeks rather than days.

## What Does the Other End of This Look Like?

Considerably less dramatic than a breakthrough, and made mostly of better questions asked earlier.

| Where you are now | The other end of it | What it looks like on an ordinary Tuesday |
|---|---|---|
| A failed attempt reads as evidence about you | A failed attempt reads as evidence about the aim | The routine collapses on day nineteen and you write down which layer it was aimed at |
| The problem gets described by its name | The problem gets described by what it is buying | You say the delay is protecting you from a verdict, out loud, and it sounds accurate |
| Advice gets accepted or rejected whole | Advice gets sorted by depth first | You read the article, recognize it as a state-level fix, and use it for a state-level problem |
| A shove-back means the idea was wrong | A shove-back means the wrapper was wrong | You rewrite somebody's instruction in your own words and find you can suddenly do it |
| More effort at the same layer | The same effort, aimed one layer down | You stop adding calendar blocks and say the colliding rule out loud instead |
| Every result gets read as a verdict | Every result gets read as a measurement | Week three ends badly and you are curious about why rather than resigned |

Nothing in that middle column is impressive, which is the point. The change is in what you do with a result, and results arrive weekly whether or not anybody is reading them properly.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

An afternoon to run the read, weeks to confirm it, and it moves unevenly.

The two speeds are worth separating, because confusing them is what makes people quit in week two. The sort itself is fast. Listing three failed attempts, marking which produced a brief shift, and placing each one against the five depths takes about twenty minutes, and most people come out of it with a strong candidate they had genuinely never considered.

Confirming it is slower. A repair aimed at a rule can move within a week of the rule being said out loud. A repair aimed at the driver structure takes months, since something has to be built that delivers what the old behavior was delivering before the old behavior will release its grip. And the first candidate is wrong reasonably often, which is a normal part of this rather than a failure of it. A wrong guess that gets tested returns better information than the alternative, which is another year of aiming at whatever the last article suggested.

There is also a version of progress that does not feel like progress. The first genuinely useful sign is usually that a collapse stops meaning what it used to mean. The routine still fails on day nineteen and you find yourself reaching for a notebook rather than a verdict. That is a smaller event than anybody wants, and it is the one everything else is built on.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From public motivation and behavior-change research, most of it in print for decades, and every strand can be checked without going through me.

Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, working at the University of Rochester from the 1970s onward, built self-determination theory around the claim that autonomy, competence and relatedness function as basic psychological needs, and that whether they are satisfied or frustrated predicts motivation and behavior change. That body of work is the citable backbone for the general claim on this page, which is that whether an intervention lands depends on which underlying need it does or does not address. Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess, running the New York Longitudinal Study from the 1950s, gave the idea its clearest independent precedent with goodness of fit, showing that identical approaches produce opposite outcomes across different temperaments. James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente's stages-of-change work, developed from studies of self-quitting smokers, made the same point about readiness: a technically sound intervention offered at the wrong stage produces resistance rather than movement.

Three more strands hold up the harder edges. Gordon Paul's 1967 formulation of the specificity question is where the whole matching problem gets stated properly. Lee Cronbach's 1957 address on the two disciplines of psychology set out the ambition to match treatments to individuals, and his own later reckoning with how poorly that search had gone is the reason this page states the limits of fit as plainly as its promise. William Miller and Stephen Rollnick's motivational interviewing, first described in the early 1980s, is where resistance stopped being treated as a property of the stuck person. Harold Pashler and colleagues' 2008 review of learning styles is the cautionary case, and it is included here precisely because it cuts against the easy version of this page's argument.

The rest of it is mine and carries no research weight at all. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, and none of the researchers named above has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, theirs is the one worth checking.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Try Another Method?
