# Why do we keep having the same fight over and over?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

A fight repeats because the last round settled the rule and never reached the need underneath it. The topic, money or chores or the in-laws, is the vehicle. Both of you are also reading the same event through different, equally real filters, and each of you then responds in the way that most confirms the other's worst reading. That loop closes on its own.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
You can usually tell which kind of fight this is by how well you already know your own next line.

- "We had a fight about that about a year ago. It was a case of you will do something about this or else and we came to a compromise."
- "I was very low on the list of priority, even though I would never admit it."
- "I've ignored him - when he's saying the same thing over and over again and I don't feel like it serves anyone."
- "All that time I thought it was him."

Years of the same round leaves a residue that outlasts any single argument, and that residue is usually what a couple is actually carrying by the time one of them goes looking for help.

</div>

The conversation started about the dishes. Four sentences later it is about whether you ever listen to her, and both of you are delivering lines you have delivered before, in the same order, at roughly the same volume, on an ordinary Tuesday evening that had nothing in particular wrong with it.

Almost everybody arrives at this question carrying one of two explanations, and both of them miss in the same direction. The first is incompatibility: this many years of the same argument must prove the two of you were badly matched from the start. The second is a skills gap: you need better communication, I-statements, turn-taking, a rule about not going to bed angry. Each one points at something real, and neither accounts for the single most striking feature of a recurring fight, which is how consistent it is. Badly matched people argue about a wide spread of unrelated things. A skills gap produces messy conversations everywhere. What you have is one argument, wearing different clothes, arriving on something close to a timetable.

That regularity is information. It tells you the fight has a structure, and a structure can be described, which means it can be worked on without anybody having to be the villain of it.

A word on who this page is written for. It is written for two people in an ordinary, difficult, non-dangerous relationship who both still want it to work and cannot understand why the same forty minutes keeps happening. If what you mostly recognize is the sediment rather than the round itself, [anger that had nowhere safe to go has its own page](/patterns/resentment/). If you are the half who goes quiet and stays quiet, [the avoiding side is set out separately](/patterns/why-do-i-avoid-conflict/). If the size of your own reaction is the thing that frightens you, [that has its own mechanism](/patterns/why-do-i-overreact/). ## What Does a Recurring Fight Actually Look Like on an Ordinary Week?

Smaller than the phrase suggests, and made mostly of moments nobody would think to put in a complaint.

- The dishes conversation that somehow ends with "you never listen to me."
- Going quiet on the drive home from his family's place, again, and neither of you mentioning it.
- The hobby you negotiated down to twice a month a year ago, which technically nobody has broken.
- A small comment about the budget that, four sentences later, is somehow about whether he respects you at all.
- The specific Sunday-evening tension that shows up before anything has actually happened yet.
- The joke that has landed fine nine times, landing very differently on the tenth.

Read that list looking for the dramatic one. There is no dramatic one. The whole pattern is built out of five-minute exchanges that would sound trivial to a stranger and land with a familiar, sinking weight on the person living inside them.

What sits under those surfaces has a shape worth laying out plainly, because the surfaces rotate and the thing under them tends not to.

| The topic on the surface | What it is usually carrying | How you can tell |
|---|---|---|
| Money | Whether one of you is treated as a competent adult | The word "allowed" turns up, from either side |
| Chores | Whether the invisible load is seen at all | It ends in "you never notice", not in a schedule |
| A hobby or a sport | Where you rank against the thing that makes him feel alive | The fight is about hours and the hurt is about priority |
| His family, or yours | Whose household gets to set the terms | Silence in the car, not raised voices at the table |
| Sex and timing | Whether wanting somebody is still safe to show | Both people report rejection and neither believes the other |
| Phones and attention | Whether you are actually in the room together | It flares in company and gets settled at home |
| Parenting decisions | Which childhood is being avoided this time | One of you gets far angrier than the decision warrants |

None of that means the surface topic is fake. The dishes are genuinely undone. The budget is genuinely tight. What the table describes is why solving the surface has never once ended the argument.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, near the top, because this line matters more than everything below it put together.

If you are afraid of your partner, this page is the wrong tool. That includes fear you have talked yourself out of. If you change what you say, wear, spend, or do in order to avoid a reaction, if there have been threats, if you have been pushed, held, blocked from leaving a room, or frightened by damage to objects, if your money, your phone, your movements or who you are allowed to see is controlled by somebody else, none of that is a recurring fight and none of it improves through better naming of needs. ## What Kind of Problem Is a Fight That Keeps Coming Back?

A perpetual one, in the technical sense, and that is a much better diagnosis than it sounds.

Decades of observational research on couples split conflict into two categories. Some problems are solvable: a decision, a logistics failure, a genuine misunderstanding that clears once the facts are on the table. Most are perpetual, meaning they grow out of durable differences in personality, temperament, or need, and they get managed across the life of a relationship rather than closed once. The often-quoted figure from that work puts roughly sixty-nine percent of a couple's conflict in the perpetual category. Not two thirds of couples. Two thirds of the conflict inside a normal, functioning, affectionate relationship.

That number rearranges the question you came in with. If most long-term conflict recurs by design, then a fight coming back is weak evidence about your relationship and strong evidence about relationships in general. The useful question stops being why does this keep happening and becomes what is this particular one made of, and what does it cost us each time it runs.

The same research draws a second line that matters more. A perpetual problem can sit in dialogue, where two people know the difference is there, can talk about it with some humor, and accept that it will not be finished. Or it can sit in gridlock, where the same conversation has calcified into entrenched positions, each side defending against being erased by the other. Gridlock is the state that hurts. Moving a perpetual problem from gridlock into dialogue is a realistic goal, and it is the actual target of nearly everything below.

## What Does This Sound Like From the Inside?

Like a private commentary that neither of you has ever said out loud in the room where it would matter.

These are the sentences that run underneath a fight of this kind, in the phrasing people actually use rather than smoothed into a description of a mood. Some are the complaint, some are the moment the complaint started to move.

- "I don't trust him anymore."
- "He doesn't trust me with money, but I think it's ill-founded."
- "I want to be with my wife. I love my wife. But we don't have that many things in common. We've got a lot of different things that we value and that we're interested in."
- "I tend to overthink every little thing in relationships, looking for signs they're pulling away."
- "I don't want to stop being angry, because if I don't get angry then I keep getting taken advantage of, and then no one will pay attention to me and I will make no progress at all."
- "I was just treating him like he was this person in a war and I had to do everything myself, but no, he's a strong bloke."
- "I know that focusing on me, me, me, me, me is not the answer."
- "One wasn't right and one wasn't wrong, it was just different."
- "It's not that the marriage isn't gonna work, and it won't if she changes. It will work, but it's not anything she can do. It's something I have to do."

Read them slowly, and notice which ones land with a small physical jolt because they arrived word for word.

Two things about that list are worth pausing on. The first five describe a position: a verdict about the other person, or about what the fighting has cost, held privately and defended quietly. The last four are what it sounds like when the position moves, and none of them involve the other person changing anything at all. That is the actual sequence, and it is a good deal less satisfying than the one most people are hoping for.

The fifth sentence deserves its own note, because it explains a great many stuck fights. Anger gets kept on purpose. It is doing a job: it is the only thing that has ever produced attention, or protection, or motion. Being told to let it go reads as being told to go back to being ignored. Nothing shifts until the job the anger has been doing gets acknowledged out loud, which is a very different move from being talked out of it. [Anger that points outward has its own page](/insights/how-to-deal-with-anger/), and it is worth reading beside this one.

## Why Doesn't the Compromise You Agreed On Ever Settle It?

Because the compromise fixed the rule and never touched the need the rule was covering for.

Here is the shape, using a composite that recurs constantly. A couple fights about a hobby: how many hours, which weekends, how often it takes him out of the house. It escalates over a year, then culminates in a hard conversation where a limit gets set. Twice a month. Both of them accept it. He honors it. She stops raising it. Both would describe that argument as settled.

Twelve months later they are fighting about a work trip, then about his phone, then about a Saturday. Nobody broke the agreement. The agreement was never the point. What she wanted was to feel as significant and as alive to him as the hobby made him feel, and there is no number of monthly hobby sessions that delivers that. The rule was aimed at the vehicle. The need underneath it went unnamed, so it kept looking for a new vehicle, and it found one every few months for a decade.

This is why the standard advice fails so reliably at this specific problem. I-statements, turn-taking, picking your battles, never going to bed angry: all of them operate on the topic. A better-negotiated version of the wrong subject is still the wrong subject. And picking your battles assumes the topic is the generator, so avoiding it simply delays the flare-up onto something else, usually something less legible, which makes the next round harder to talk about rather than easier.

| What got agreed | What it delivers | What it never touches |
|---|---|---|
| A frequency for the hobby | Predictable hours | Whether she ranks above it |
| A cleaner chore split | A fairer schedule | Whether the load she carries is seen |
| A budget rule | Fewer surprises | Whether he is trusted with a decision |
| A phones-down rule at dinner | Forty quiet minutes | Whether either of you is actually present |
| An agreement to stop mentioning his mother | Fewer flashpoints | Whose household sets the terms |
| A promise not to raise your voice | A calmer volume | Everything that was being said at volume |

None of those agreements is a bad idea. Each one is worth having. What none of them can do is close a fight, because they were built to regulate a behavior and the fight is being generated one level down.

## Why Does the Same Saturday Mean Safety to One of You and a Closing Door to the Other?

Because you are running different dominant needs, and both of your reactions are accurate reports.

Take the most common version. One partner wants Saturday locked in a week ahead. The other wants to decide on Saturday morning. Each reads the other's preference as a character flaw: rigid, or flaky. They have had this argument for eleven years, and it has slowly migrated from the calendar onto who they each are as people.

What is actually happening is that a confirmed plan is doing two opposite things at once. To the partner who scans for certainty, a fixed plan is the thing that lets the week be enjoyable at all, because the ground is settled and attention is freed. To the partner who scans for variety and aliveness, the same fixed plan closes a door on the week and reads as one more line on a life already full of obligations. Neither reading is a distortion. The calendar entry genuinely produces those two felt states, in those two people, every time.

One of the most useful sentences anyone in this position ever says is short: "One wasn't right and one wasn't wrong, it was just different." It removes the fault without removing the disagreement, which is exactly the correct amount of work for one sentence to do. The disagreement is real. The villain was never there.

| The same Saturday | The partner who runs on certainty | The partner who runs on variety |
|---|---|---|
| A plan confirmed on Monday | Relief, and the week becomes usable | A door closing, and a small drop in energy |
| A plan left open until Saturday morning | Low-grade dread all week | The week stays alive and worth waking up for |
| The other person's preference | Carelessness about the household | Control over an already scheduled life |
| What the argument feels like | Being asked to live without ground | Being asked to live without air |
| The line each one reaches for | "You never commit to anything." | "You have to control everything." |
| What each is actually asking for | Predictability | Room |

Once a couple can see that table with their own names on it, the fight usually drops a size, because the accusation stops being available. What survives is a genuine design problem, and design problems are workable. The version that has moved this fastest in practice is a fixed frame with unallocated space inside it: Saturday is protected, and what happens inside Saturday gets chosen that morning. One partner gets the ground, the other gets the room, and an argument that ran for eleven years becomes a logistics conversation inside a month.

The same framework explains a great deal of what else recurs. Needs like certainty, variety, significance, connection, growth, and contribution run in everybody, at different strengths, and the strong ones do most of the arguing. [The full set is laid out on its own page](/patterns/six-human-needs/), and [the certainty end in particular has a page of its own](/patterns/need-for-certainty/) if you recognized yourself in the left-hand column above.

## What Makes the Loop Close Without Either of You Choosing It?

Each of you responds to the other in the exact way that proves the other's worst assumption right.

Watch it run. One partner reaches for connection during a difficulty, wants to talk it through now, gets louder and more insistent as the response fails to arrive. The other reads volume and insistence as danger and does the only thing that has ever calmed it, which is to go still, go quiet, and wait for it to pass. The stillness reads as contempt to the first partner, who pushes harder. The pushing reads as more threat to the second, who withdraws further. Neither one chose the cycle. Each of them is making a sensible response to the move in front of them, and the sum of those sensible responses is a machine that runs by itself.

This has a name in the clinical literature, where it is described as a pursue-withdraw or demand-withdraw cycle, and it is the closest published match to what most couples mean by the same fight. Its defining feature is that it is content-independent. It runs the same way about money, about the in-laws, and about a bathroom shelf, because the topic was never what was driving it.

There is a sizing version of the same asymmetry that causes at least as much damage. One partner processes a shared problem by making it bigger out loud, because that is how it becomes real enough to act on. The other minimizes the identical problem, because that is how it becomes small enough to carry. Each concludes something unkind and mistaken about the other: she catastrophizes everything, he does not care about anything. Both are running the same coping habit in opposite directions on the same event.

And there is a third version, the one that gets defended hardest, where each partner has a move that restores their footing in an argument. One threatens to leave, which is intended to reclaim standing and reliably produces less of it. One lands a joke that is a fraction too sharp, which is intended to keep things bearable and teaches the children in the next room a way of speaking that neither parent would ever choose to teach. Both moves are decades old. Neither was invented for this marriage. And the gap between what each move was for and what it actually produces is enormous, which is the most useful thing to look at, because looking at it needs no accusation from anybody.

| The move | What it is intended to do | What it actually produces |
|---|---|---|
| Going silent mid-argument | Prevent saying something unforgivable | Reads as contempt, and raises the volume opposite |
| Raising the volume | Get a response at last | Reads as danger, and produces more silence |
| Threatening to leave | Restore standing | Less respect each time, and a threat that stops working |
| A joke with an edge on it | Keep the evening survivable | A style of contempt the whole household learns |
| Listing the evidence | Be understood correctly | Reads as prosecution, and triggers a defense |
| Conceding fast to end it | Restore peace | An unsaid grievance that returns with interest |

Sit with the middle column for a moment. Almost nothing in it is malicious. That is the actual finding, and it is the reason a fight like this can survive two people who genuinely love each other and are both trying hard. If the part that never resolves for you is the sense that nothing you say ever arrives intact, [feeling unheard runs on a two-part loop of its own](/patterns/why-do-i-feel-unheard/) and is worth reading beside this.

## How Do You Tell an Ordinary Recurring Fight From One That Has Curdled?

By looking for contempt specifically, rather than by counting how often you argue or how loudly.

This is the one part of the page where a flat verdict is available, so here it is. Frequency of conflict is a weak predictor of whether a relationship survives. Contempt is a strong one. In the long observational studies, four behaviors stand out as the most reliable markers of trouble, usually listed as criticism, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling, and of those four, contempt is singled out as the most corrosive by a distance.

The four are worth being able to name in your own kitchen.

1. **Criticism.** An attack on the person rather than a complaint about the event. "You forgot the booking" is a complaint. "You never think about anybody but yourself" is a criticism, and it invites a defense rather than an answer.
2. **Contempt.** Mockery, eye-rolling, sarcasm, name-calling, imitation of the other person's voice, and anything delivered from a position of being the better human in the room. This is the marker that matters most.
3. **Defensiveness.** Meeting a complaint with a counter-complaint or an innocence plea. It is completely understandable and it reliably escalates, because it tells the other person that nothing they raised has landed.
4. **Stonewalling.** Shutting down and going blank mid-conversation. Often it is a flooded nervous system rather than a punishment, which does not change how it lands.

| What you are seeing | Ordinary recurring conflict | Something that has curdled |
|---|---|---|
| The tone | Frustrated, loud, sometimes unfair | Cool, superior, amused at the other person's expense |
| What gets attacked | The event, the schedule, the decision | The person, their character, their family, their body |
| After it ends | Some form of repair, awkward or otherwise | Nothing, or a joke about it later at their expense |
| In front of other people | Mostly held back | Delivered publicly, as a performance |
| The private description | "We fight about this constantly" | "There is something wrong with him as a person" |
| What either can still see | The other person's good qualities, on a good day | Very little, and the list has stopped updating |

If the right-hand column is where you live, the honest advice is different from the advice below. That situation needs a couples professional rather than a page, and it needs one soon, because contempt tends to build a stable structure around itself. [The resentment it grows out of is covered separately](/patterns/resentment/), and reading that page beside this one is usually more useful than either alone.

## Does the Pattern Belong to the Relationship, or Did It Travel In With One of You?

Run the base-rate check, because the answer changes what you are actually working on.

Take a composite that shows up constantly. A husband withdraws, physically and emotionally, at the exact point closeness deepens. His wife has spent years reading that withdrawal as evidence about their marriage: something is wrong between us, he has stopped wanting me, I have done something. It is a completely reasonable reading, because inside a relationship the only sample you have is that relationship.

The check is a single question, asked about both of you: has this move shown up before, with other people, at roughly the same point? In this composite the answer was yes, in every serious relationship he had ever had, at the same stage each time. That single fact relocates the problem. It stops being something wrong between them and becomes a pattern he runs, which is what finally allowed him to examine it rather than defend against it or apologize for it for another decade.

The relocation does something for both people at once. The partner who has been reading the withdrawal as a verdict on herself gets to stop. The partner running the pattern gets a problem he can actually work on, which is a considerable improvement on being the defendant in a case he keeps losing.

This is also where the older research earns its place. Bowlby and Ainsworth's work established that early close relationships install durable expectations about what happens when you get near somebody, which is the checkable reason a conflict pattern can be portable rather than local. [What the settled end of that looks like has its own page](/patterns/secure-attachment/), and [the version where the same kind of partner keeps reappearing is worth settling separately](/insights/why-do-i-attract-the-same-people/).

Two cautions on using this. It cuts both ways, so run it on yourself before you run it on them, and be honest about the answer. And portable does not mean permanent. A pattern that traveled in with somebody is workable by that person, on their own, which is often faster than waiting for a joint breakthrough that keeps not arriving.

## Why Does the Same Fight Run to the End in Under a Minute?

Because it is a stored sequence with its own triggers, and by now it is the most practiced thing either of you does together.

By the tenth round, a recurring fight is a memorized route rather than an improvisation. Both of you know it: an opening line, a reply, the point where it stops being about the topic, the two or three sentences that always appear near the end, the way it stops. Sequences like that get stored together with their cues, which is why the whole thing can run to completion in seconds once a familiar cue turns up. A particular tone. A particular phrase. Sunday evening. The car on the way back from one specific house. The specific tiredness of the hour after the children go to bed.

This is also why bringing up an old grievance reopens everything so reliably. The old incident is part of the retrieval context rather than a piece of evidence, so mentioning it switches the sequence on regardless of what either of you intended.

And it explains the most demoralizing part of the whole business, which is what happens after a good reconciliation. You make up. It is genuine: real relief, real closeness, sometimes the best hour of the month. Then the identical fight arrives eight days later and both of you conclude that the making-up meant nothing. It meant something. What it changed was your state, which is a real thing and a temporary one. What it did not change was the need underneath the complaint or the cues that start the sequence. State resets in an evening. Structure does not, and the two get confused constantly because the good feeling arrives immediately and is extremely convincing.

There is a practical consequence. Insight in a calm debrief on Sunday afternoon does very little on its own. The change has to happen where the sequence actually runs, at the cue, in the first thirty seconds, many times. [Why understanding a pattern so rarely changes it has a page of its own](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/), and it is the single most useful thing to read if you have already had the good conversation four times.

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because four different situations produce the phrase "we keep having the same fight" and they need different responses.

| What it looks like | What it might actually be | The thing that tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| One argument on repeat, both people still trying | A perpetual difference in gridlock | Both can still name something they admire in the other |
| One argument on repeat, one person disengaged | A relationship where somebody has already left quietly | Whether they care how the fight ends |
| Many unrelated arguments, constant friction | Load: money, illness, small children, a bad year | It started at a datable point and tracks the pressure |
| The same fight, plus mockery and public digs | Contempt, which is a different category | Whether either of you is amused at the other's expense |
| The same fight in every relationship one of you has had | A portable pattern | The base-rate check across earlier partners |
| No fights at all for two years, and a growing flatness | Deadlock rather than conflict | Nobody has raised anything, and nobody misses raising it |
| The same fight, with fear attached | Coercion | Whether one of you edits their behavior to prevent a reaction |

Rows four and seven are the ones to be honest about, and they both point off this page. The rest are the ordinary territory. Most couples find themselves split across two rows rather than sitting neatly in one, which is normal and slightly annoying, since it means there is more than one thing to work on.

## What Actually Moves a Fight That Has Run for Years?

Three things, none of them fast, and all of them done at the cue rather than in the debrief afterward.

Nothing below is a validated protocol and anybody offering you one for a specific marriage is overselling something. What follows is built from how the loop runs.

1. **Name the cue before you name the topic.** Write down the last three rounds and look for what they share: the hour, the room, the level of tiredness, the opening line. A cue you can both name out loud in advance becomes something you can interrupt. A cue nobody has noticed runs unopposed.
2. **Say what the topic has been standing in for, outside the fight.** Not during. Pick a neutral hour and say the plain version: what I actually want when we argue about the hobby is to matter to you as much as it does. That sentence is harder to say than any amount of arguing about hours, which is precisely why the arguing has been happening instead.
3. **Sit in their chair and score it.** Take the six needs, certainty, variety, significance, connection, growth, contribution, and rate honestly, in their voice, how much of each your partner actually gets from being with you. Most people discover one that scores far lower than they expected, and it is almost never the one the fight has been about. This is uncomfortable in a way that is difficult to describe and it moves more in twenty minutes than a year of negotiating did.
4. **Run the base-rate check on yourself first.** Has your half of this shown up with earlier partners, at roughly the same point? Answer before you ask them the same question.
5. **Change your own half without announcing it as a deal.** A pattern needs both halves running. Stopping your part is available to you alone, today, with nobody's permission. Announcing it as a trade turns it back into a negotiation, and the whole value of this move is that it does not require the other person to move first.
6. **Subtract the ingredient, keep the trait.** If the costly move is a joke with an edge on it, the humor stays and the edge goes. If it is directness, the directness stays and the threat riding on top of it goes. Trying to delete a whole trait fails, and it should, because the trait is usually one of the things they were loved for.
7. **Design instead of negotiating.** A negotiated rule splits a difference and leaves both people slightly short. A design gives each need its own actual container: a protected frame with genuinely unallocated space inside it, a fixed budget with a no-questions portion, a standing evening with no agenda attached. Design solves what compromise only divides.
8. **Stop auditing what you are not getting, for one week.** Running a private tally of unmet needs organizes both your giving and your withholding around the same scorekeeping question, and the account never balances. One week of dropping the audit is a genuinely strange experience and it tells you something you cannot find out any other way.
9. **Repair the specific thing, and let the round end.** After a fight, work the actual problem rather than opening a second front to establish who was worse. Repair that stays on topic accumulates. Repair that expands does not.

Is any of that comfortable? No. Point three in particular tends to land badly for a day or two, and the discomfort is the mechanism rather than a side effect, since what is being built is the ability to look at your own half without immediately defending it. It is small, repetitive, and freaking unglamorous, which is exactly why it holds when a single long overdue conversation does not.

## What If Your Partner Will Not Do Any of This?

Then you do your half, and you find out what happens, because a loop needs both halves to keep running.

This is the most common objection and the most quietly hopeful part of the whole subject. A recurring fight is a two-person machine. If one person reliably stops supplying their half at the cue, the sequence has nowhere to go. It does not end the disagreement, and it does change the fight, usually within weeks, often in ways that surprise the person who stopped.

There is a line people reach here that is worth quoting in full, because it marks the turn: "It's not that the marriage isn't gonna work, and it won't if she changes. It will work, but it's not anything she can do. It's something I have to do." Read that as somebody locating the only part of this he can actually move.

Two honest cautions. First, this is different from over-functioning. If you already initiate every repair, carry every conversation, and manage every mood in the house, adding more of your own effort will make the ledger worse rather than better, and the resentment that follows is entirely predictable. [The one-sided version of that arrangement is covered here](/patterns/why-am-i-always-the-one-who-cares-more/), and reading it first is worth the twenty minutes. Second, changing your half is not the same as absorbing everything. Saying less at the cue is a change. Saying nothing about anything, ever, is [conflict avoidance](/patterns/why-do-i-avoid-conflict/), which reliably produces a fight of its own about eight months later.

The other thing worth saying plainly: a partner who refuses to look at any of this is often responding to being coached rather than delivering a verdict on the relationship. Nobody enjoys being handed a framework by the person they are arguing with. Telling them something true about your own half, and then stopping, works far more often than asking them to read anything.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Weeks to months for the cycle, longer for the residue, and it is rarely a straight line.

The two speeds are worth separating. Catching it gets fast. Most people can spot the sequence starting within a few weeks of paying deliberate attention, and catching it counts even on the nights you go ahead and run it anyway. Watching yourself deliver the line you promised yourself you would skip, while knowing exactly what you are doing, feels like failure and is genuinely a step forward.

Responding differently takes longer, because the sequence fires before conscious thought does. The realistic progression is noticing an hour afterward, then twenty minutes afterward, then during, then early enough that a choice exists. Each of those transitions takes a stack of real instances, on real Sunday evenings, with the actual person, rather than rehearsed ones worked out alone.

The residue is the slower part, and nobody warns anybody about it. Years of the same round leave sediment, and the sediment does not clear the week the fight changes. Expect a stretch where the argument is genuinely better and neither of you feels much warmer yet. That stretch is where most couples conclude it did not work, usually about six weeks in. Expect relapse under load too: illness, a job loss, a bereavement, a move, small children. The old sequence stays installed. It just gets less of your week.

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. The thing I had wrong longest was the target. I kept trying to win the round, or to have one conversation good enough that the round would stop. What actually changed things was much duller: noticing the cue about ten seconds earlier than I used to, and saying one less clever thing.

Is it frustrating that reading a page like this changes very little on its own? Yes. Twenty interrupted rounds, in real time, on evenings that actually mattered, changes a great deal.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From couples research that has been public for decades, and three strands carry most of the weight here.

John Gottman, working with Robert Levenson from the 1970s onward and later in an apartment laboratory at the University of Washington that everybody ended up calling the Love Lab, recorded couples having ordinary disagreements and coded what happened second by second. Two findings from that program sit under most of this page. The first is the split between solvable and perpetual problems, set out for general readers in *The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work*, written with Nan Silver and published in 1999, along with the often-quoted figure that roughly sixty-nine percent of a couple's conflict is perpetual, and the distinction between a perpetual problem in dialogue and one in gridlock. The second is the Four Horsemen, criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling, with contempt named as the strongest single predictor of a relationship ending. The same body of work is the source of the harsh startup, the finding that how the first few minutes of a conflict discussion go predicts how the discussion ends, and the observation that stable couples maintain roughly five positive exchanges to every negative one during conflict rather than only during good weeks.

Sue Johnson developed Emotionally Focused Therapy with Les Greenberg in the 1980s, building directly on attachment research, and named the pursue-withdraw cycle that this page has been calling the loop. Her general-audience book *Hold Me Tight*, published in 2008, describes three recurring versions of it that she calls demon dialogues: Find the Bad Guy, the Protest Polka, and Freeze and Flee. If the middle of this page felt uncomfortably familiar, that is the vocabulary to search next. The same pattern appears in the research literature under a second name, demand and withdraw, studied at length by Andrew Christensen and colleagues, whose work also makes the case that acceptance of a durable difference achieves more than another attempt to eliminate it.

John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth supply the layer underneath. Bowlby set out, across three volumes published between 1969 and 1980, that closeness runs as a system with its own signals. Ainsworth made it observable through the Strange Situation procedure in Baltimore in the 1960s and 70s. That is the independently checkable reason a conflict pattern can be portable, traveling with one person across relationships rather than belonging to any of them, which is one of the distinctions this page turns on.

The rest is mine and carries no research weight at all: the five years and five thousand hours described above. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind and I am not a couples therapist. None of the researchers named here has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, go and check theirs.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before the Next One Starts?

Write out the last round as a transcript, from memory, tonight, before anybody says anything else about it. Five lines is enough. The opening sentence, whoever said it. The reply. The exact point where it stopped being about the topic. The two or three lines that always turn up near the end. And how it stopped, including who conceded and how long the quiet lasted afterward.

Then do three things with it. Circle the cue: what time was it, which room, how tired were you, what had happened in the two hours before. Underline the sentence where the subject changed, because that is where the real fight starts and it is almost never the sentence people remember. And at the bottom, finish this line in your own handwriting: what I actually want when we fight about this is ______. Most people stall on that last one for a surprisingly long time, and the stall is the finding. A need you cannot name in private has no chance at all of being met in an argument. That costs you fifteen minutes and nothing else, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever go anywhere or tell your partner you did it.
