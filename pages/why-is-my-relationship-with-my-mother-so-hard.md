# Why is my relationship with my mother so hard?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because the friction usually runs on a structure rather than a character flaw. Two of them do most of the work: a caretaking role that reversed before you were old enough to notice it, and a verdict about your own worth formed in one ordinary, forgettable moment with her. Both keep running in adulthood, and both are workable without her agreement.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
Some of these have been running so long they stopped sounding like opinions.

- "My mother was weak, totally weak."
- "I felt like with my mom, I had to be the parent."
- "I was a little girl. I shouldn't have to raise myself."
- "You know, my mother was sort of there but not there, so there were no demands from my mother."
- "My mom doesn't accept growth."

A role that reversed thirty years ago is still setting what every phone call costs you, and it can be put down without her participating in it.

[How the seminar works with a role that reversed before you could name it](https://florianrolke.com/the-soulsearching-experience/)
</div>

You call your mother with good news and hang up feeling worse than before you dialed. Nothing happened on that call you could describe to anybody afterward, which is most of what makes this so hard to talk about.

That is the shape of it. Not a scene, not an incident, just a reliable cost attached to an ordinary interaction, arriving so consistently that you have stopped expecting anything else and started managing the calls instead.

One plain line before anything else, because everything below is written for the ordinary version. If there was violence, sexual harm, or ongoing coercion, if you are frightened of her or of anybody in your family, or if you are carrying a hopelessness that has not lifted for months or any thought of harming yourself, that belongs today with a licensed professional and with people who can help you get somewhere safe. A page cannot hold that, and nothing here is a substitute for it.

Most people arrive at this question having already tried the two available explanations and found neither one fits. Either she was clearly harmful, which would at least make the anger legible, or she was fine, loving, well-intentioned, never cruel, which leaves you as an ungrateful adult with an inexplicable problem. Almost nobody lands cleanly in either. If you already know she was actively controlling, invalidating, or unable to let you be a separate person, [the controlling-parent version](/patterns/controlling-parents/), [the invalidation version](/patterns/emotional-invalidation/) and [the version where separateness itself was the problem](/patterns/enmeshment/) each cover their own ground properly and will serve you better than this page will. What follows is for the harder middle case, where the relationship is genuinely difficult and neither person is straightforwardly the villain.

## What Makes a Mother Relationship So Much Harder to Name Than a Father One?

The demands were rarely written down.

A father relationship that went wrong usually leaves a legible list. Achievement, performance, never disappointing him, never outshining him, a specific version of you that was acceptable and a specific version that was not. That list can be recited, argued with, resented, and eventually examined, which is why the whole territory has a well-worn vocabulary and [its own page here](/patterns/unresolved-father-issues/).

A great many mother relationships leave nothing comparable. People describe the arrangement in phrases that sound like an absence of a problem: she was sort of there but not there, there were no demands from my mother, she just really appreciated it when I showed up. Asked directly what her relationship required, people go quiet, because the honest answer is almost nothing. Then, if the question is held open long enough, something else surfaces: I guess I would have wanted to be wanted more.

| | The relationship built on demands | The relationship built on absence |
|---|---|---|
| What was required of you | A list you could recite at seven | Showing up |
| What went wrong | You failed to meet the terms | The terms were never set |
| What it left behind | A verdict about your capability | A question about whether you were worth pursuing |
| How easy it is to describe | Painfully easy | Nearly impossible without help |
| How other people respond | They recognize it immediately | They tell you she sounds lovely |
| Where the anger goes | Straight at the person who set the terms | Nowhere, so it turns into contempt or self-blame |

The right-hand column is the one that keeps people stuck for decades, because there is no scene to point to. A deprivation with no incident attached is much easier to carry indefinitely and much harder to justify out loud, including to yourself. Never being sought after leaves a real, specific injury, and it happens without anybody doing anything you could put in a sentence.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Sooner than most people assume, and this boundary matters more than every practice further down put together.

Bring in a licensed professional if the relationship involved physical violence, sexual harm, or a pattern of coercion that is still operating. The same holds if contact with her reliably brings back memories that flood you, if you lose hours or days after seeing her, or if there is an addiction, a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, or any thought of harming yourself, however briefly it passes. None of that gets settled by understanding a structure better, and a page has no way to help with it.

There is also a middle case worth naming, since it catches people who would benefit most. Plenty of adults conclude that what happened to them was too mild to justify professional time. The bar is lower than that. Persistent guilt, a rage that surprises you, a relationship you dread and cannot stop initiating, or a pattern you can see clearly and cannot interrupt are all ordinary reasons to sit with a counselor for a few months. Asking in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve.

## Who Was Actually Parenting Whom?

In a large share of these relationships, the child was.

The structure has a name in family therapy. Parentification describes a child placed, functionally or emotionally, into a caretaking role toward a parent: managing her moods, absorbing her worry, refereeing between adults, handling practical survival, or simply raising herself because nobody else was going to. Ivan Boszormenyi-Nagy, working in contextual family therapy, named the pattern alongside the related idea of invisible loyalties, the unspoken debts that keep a family arrangement running long after anybody would defend it out loud.

People say it in almost identical language. I felt like with my mom, I had to be the parent. My dad left and I had to be a parent with my mom. I was a little girl. I shouldn't have to raise myself.

What that role installs is a working rule about closeness itself: being close to a mother figure means carrying labor that was never yours. The rule does not retire when you turn eighteen. It changes venue. In adulthood it shows up as being the one who initiates every repair, absorbs the tension, manages her feelings about your own news, or quietly organizes the family around whatever she can and cannot handle. [The full mechanics of that role, and what it costs across a whole adult life, have their own page](/patterns/parentification/); what matters here is that the role is specific to her, which is why you can be perfectly capable of receiving help from a friend and structurally unable to receive it from your mother.

There is a strange after-effect worth naming, because it tends to arrive years into this work and blindside people. Somebody looks back at the arrangement and thinks: probably my mother would not have been so weak if I had not stepped up. Whether that is accurate is unknowable and slightly beside the point. What it reveals is that the caretaking role came with a second job attached, which was believing you were responsible for her capacity as well as her mood. That belief is heavier than the caretaking ever was.

## Are You Holding the Line With Her, or Being Triggered on Cue?

Whoever can dependably produce a reaction in the other person is the one steering, and the answer is frequently uncomfortable.

Picture a specific comment she makes. Something about your job, your parenting, your weight, the state of your kitchen. You respond forcefully, or you go cold, or you deliver the flat line you have been saving. Internally that registers as finally holding the line: I am not putting up with that anymore. It feels like the opposite of being pushed around.

Now check the pattern instead of the moment. If that comment produces that response every single time, on cue, with no variation across five years, the interaction is being driven by her. She has a reliable button and you have a reliable output, and a story about asserting control is running on top of a mechanism where control is being handed over. Those two feel identical from the inside, and only the pattern across many instances separates them.

| What it feels like | What the pattern shows | The test |
|---|---|---|
| Finally standing up to her | The same input produces the same output every time | Could a stranger have predicted your response from her sentence alone? |
| Refusing to play along | The refusal is itself the play, and it is on schedule | Has the shape of the last five arguments changed at all? |
| Setting a boundary | Announcing a boundary and then reopening the subject | Did anything about your behavior change after you announced it? |
| Being finally honest with her | Delivering a line rehearsed for years, at the first opening | Was it a response to what she said, or a response you were already holding? |
| Not letting her get to you | Contact reduced, thinking about her constantly | How much of your week does she occupy compared to how much of it she is in? |

The way out of that loop is unglamorous and does not involve her at all. You stop supplying the output. Not by suppressing it, which just relocates the cost, but by making the response slower and less automatic until it stops being available on demand. A comment that no longer reliably produces anything stops being a lever, and a lever nobody is pulling tends to get put down.

## Why Does One Ordinary Comment Take Your Whole Day?

Because it lands on a rule rather than on its own content.

Carl Rogers gave the clinical name for that rule: conditions of worth. His argument was that a child who receives approval only on meeting specific parental conditions internalizes those conditions as the terms of being acceptable at all, in place of a stable sense of being worth something regardless. The conditions get absorbed young, before there is any capacity to evaluate them, and they keep operating as though the original terms still hold.

That is why the sizes never match. She says nine words about your job. You lose an afternoon. From outside, the reaction looks wildly disproportionate to the sentence, and from inside it is precisely proportionate to what the sentence touched. The remark is a key, and the thing it opens is a contract signed when you were six about what you had to be in order to be alright.

Two consequences follow, and both are useful. The first is that arguing with her about the comment is aimed at the wrong object, which is why the argument never resolves anything even when you win it. The second is more encouraging. The contract is yours, held in your own head, which means it can be examined and amended without her cooperation, her acknowledgment, or her being alive.

## Where Did the Verdict About You Actually Get Made?

In an ordinary moment nobody would have thought worth remembering, which is exactly why it holds up so well.

Ask an adult for their single earliest memory involving their mother and something specific happens. What comes back is often small and physical and oddly detailed. Playing on the floor near her legs in the kitchen. Her stepping away. Reaching upward into the gap and pulling something down onto yourself.

The memory itself is rarely the important event. It is the marker. What matters is the second immediately before it, which is where a decision got made, and the decision is usually something like: I was trying to get her attention. Then, a beat later, the conclusion that outlived everything: I'm not good enough.

Look at the causal chain that a small child assembled there. I reached for her. It went wrong. Therefore I am not worth reaching for. Every link in that sequence really did happen in that order, which is exactly why the conclusion is invisible as an error to the person holding it. A three-year-old cannot distinguish an accident from a punishment, because that distinction requires a theory of other people's inner lives that has not been built yet. Her back was turned for ten seconds while something in the kitchen needed attention. The verdict got issued anyway, permanently, on the evidence available.

After that, the verdict starts filtering. Warmth from her gets discounted on arrival. Distance from her gets logged as confirmation. Thirty years later the relationship is legible only through its worst five minutes, because the counter-evidence exists and never gets retrieved with anything like the same frequency. [Where beliefs like this get formed and how they defend themselves has its own page](/patterns/limiting-beliefs/), and so does [the specific verdict about not being good enough](/patterns/why-do-i-feel-not-good-enough/).

## Can a Belief Form in the Middle of Real Care?

Constantly, and this is the most useful thing on the page for anybody whose mother was not remotely a monster.

Somebody who holds the verdict above, formed in that kitchen, will often turn out to have another set of memories sitting right beside it, from the same year, involving the same woman. She used to sew my clothes. I would have really beautiful dresses. And she put a lot of love and care into the way that I looked. Ribbons in the hair every morning. Real, visible effort, made repeatedly, by the same mother the belief is about.

Both memories are true. Only one gets rehearsed. A single vivid negative moment eclipses a large quantity of undramatic positive context, so a person can carry a belief formed in the middle of abundant care and never once notice the contradiction sitting next to it.

That has a direct practical consequence, and it is the fastest-moving part of this whole subject. Being told the belief is false does almost nothing, because a belief encoded experientially cannot be argued out of position by a sentence delivered from outside. What does move it is retrieving the counter-memory, in detail, in the present tense, until it has comparable weight as evidence: the specific fabric, the specific morning, the specific way she looked at what she had made. The move here is to put a second piece of your own real history beside the first, so the verdict gets examined against the full record rather than against the edited one. Anybody can tell you to think positively about your mother, and it does nothing, because a slogan carries no evidence. A recovered morning does.

## What If She Was Never Cruel, Only Somewhere Else?

Then you are dealing with the sparse version, and it is harder to name rather than easier to carry.

The sparse contract sounds mild in every direction. There were no demands from my mother. I felt my demands through my father, never through my mom. She just really appreciated it when I showed up. Nobody hearing that would identify a problem, and neither, usually, does the person saying it, until somebody asks the same audit question of the mother relationship that everybody has already asked of the louder one: what did she require of you, and what was forbidden?

The answers come back empty. Which sounds like freedom and is closer to a room nobody entered. Asked what would actually have been wanted instead, people frequently pause, say they had genuinely never considered it, and then say something quiet: I would have wanted to be wanted more. Or, about her: she didn't quite know how to. She was a little bit self-absorbed, I guess.

A low-demand mother relationship reads as the easy one by comparison with a demanding father, which is precisely what makes the cost so easy to file away as ingratitude. There is no harsh requirement to point to, only an absence of requirement and, sitting next to it, an absence of pursuit. Both are real. The second one is the injury.

Don't judge yourself for having taken decades to notice. Deprivation by absence is invisible by design, since noticing it means grieving something that never had a shape.

## Is the Contempt Doing the Job of the Grief?

In most cases where a mother gets described as weak, yes.

The harshest descriptions tend to come from the most parentified adults, and they arrive with real force. My mother was weak, totally weak. I had no respect for her. She rejects anything that she doesn't understand. In her core, she's quite primitive. No growth at all.

Every one of those may be accurate as an observation. What the contempt is doing is a separate question from whether it is fair. Judgment gives you a position, an argument, and a stable place to stand. Underneath it sits something with none of those advantages: grief about not having been parented the way you needed, which offers no position to hold, cannot be won, and requires admitting the loss was real and is not going to be made up.

There is a specific version worth watching for, because it disguises itself as high standards. Holding your mother to a personal-growth, self-awareness, or emotional-honesty standard she never agreed to, then treating her failure to meet it as fresh proof she is limited, is a framework mismatch presented as a verdict. She may well be operating from her own unexamined model of how life works and finding yours as baffling as you find hers. Naming the mismatch changes what is possible in a way that upgrading the verdict never does.

[Anger that had nowhere to go and hardened into a position has its own page](/patterns/resentment/), and the mechanism there is the same one running here. What tends to happen once the grief gets felt is that the contempt loses its job and quietly drops in volume, without anybody having decided to be more generous about her.

## Why Did You Become Her Opposite and Still Get Her Result?

Because the surface behavior flipped between generations while the underlying structure stayed exactly the same shape.

A daughter who experienced her mother as weak or unreliable frequently builds herself into the precise opposite. Decisive, capable, unwilling to be steered, entirely self-sufficient. That hardening was genuinely useful and often necessary at the time it was built, which is why it gets filed as personality rather than as strategy. I'm already bored of being strong is a sentence people say decades after the conditions that required the strength stopped applying.

Then something disorienting happens. The same dominance pattern she spent her life escaping starts arriving back at her, out of her own school-age child. The child escalates, the mother responds forcefully, the mother experiences that as establishing control, and the actual mechanism is a child who has learned that escalation reliably produces a parent's full attention. The lever changed hands. The loop is identical to the one she grew up inside, running now with her in the other chair.

Murray Bowen's family systems work gives the formal name for this: the multigenerational transmission process, a pattern of emotional functioning moving down a family line and reappearing in a different surface form. John Bowlby's term describes the same thing from inside the child, an internal working model assembled out of what actually happens between a child and a caregiver rather than out of what the caregiver states is true, later made observable in a laboratory by Mary Ainsworth's Strange Situation research. What a child copies is what a parent does under load, before language, independently of what that parent believes.

Which is the good news, awkwardly. A template built from demonstration stays open to new demonstration, so what you do the next fifty times something goes wrong is fresh material. [The full version of that, written for the parent rather than for the child, is here](/patterns/why-does-my-child-act-just-like-me-at-my-worst/), and it is the better use of an hour if your own child is where you actually feel this most.

## What Does This Sound Like From the Inside?

Almost identical from one person to the next, which is the first surprising thing about it.

The sentences below are the exact phrasing that runs in the head, at that level rather than smoothed into a description of a mood. The first group belongs to the parentified, hardened version, the one that ends up despising a mother for what she could not do:

- "I had no respect for her."
- "My dad left and I had to be a parent with my mom."
- "Actually, probably my mum wouldn't have been so weak if I hadn't stepped up."
- "I can blame my mother for teaching me polarity and that there's strength in weakness."
- "No growth at all. She thinks I'm a complete twit for being here."
- "Yeah, I'm broken. But I function."

The second group belongs to the sparse version, and it has a completely different texture. Nothing here is angry. It is closer to something being noticed for the first time:

- "I mean I felt my demands through my father, never through my mom."
- "She just really appreciated it when I showed up."
- "That's a good question. I hadn't really thought about that one. I guess I would have wanted to be wanted more."
- "Or didn't quite know how to. She was a little bit self-absorbed, I guess."

And underneath both, the verdict that both versions tend to produce:

- "I'm not good enough."
- "My own limiting belief before the other day has always been that I'm not good enough. So I actively look out and put myself in situations where I can't hurt. And I would give my heart to the wrong people to protect myself."

Read them slowly. What matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word, with no translation required. Notice too that the last one describes a whole adult dating history being run by a belief formed in a kitchen thirty years earlier, by somebody who could see the mechanism clearly and had never connected the two.

## Why Don't Boundaries, the Honest Conversation, or Letting It Go Work?

Each of the three standard prescriptions skips a different step, and the step it skips is the one carrying the weight.

| The advice | What it assumes | What it actually skips |
|---|---|---|
| "Just set boundaries with her" | The problem is her present-tense behavior | A boundary manages one instance while the role that generates them stays in place |
| "Have the honest conversation and clear the air" | The belief was argued into position | A belief encoded at three cannot be talked out by a sentence from her at fifty |
| "She did her best, just let it go" | Forgiveness is available before the accounting | Nobody has yet named who was parenting whom, what was required, what was never offered |

Take them in order. Boundaries are useful and they operate at the level of instances. If the thing running the friction is a caretaking role built in childhood, holding a line on Sunday's phone call leaves the operating structure untouched, and by Thursday you are back in the role wearing different clothes, offering to sort out something that was never yours to sort out. The boundary was real. The job description was not touched.

The honest conversation fails for a more interesting reason. A belief formed pre-verbally, in a single ordinary moment, was encoded as experience rather than as an argument, so no verbal correction reaches it. Her saying she always loved you does not land. Neither does your carefully constructed case against her. Only a comparably vivid counter-experience has the right currency, which is why intelligent, self-aware people can spend a decade repeating I know she loves me, I just can't feel it and be entirely accurate about both halves.

Letting it go is the most commonly given and the least workable, because it asks for the last step first. A fast, fluent I've made my peace with her, offered within seconds of being asked, is worth inspecting. Speed and fluency normally read as sincerity, and in this specific pattern they often run the other way: the instant answer is the tell that the old compliance is the thing answering. Pete Walker added a fourth response to the standard three, alongside fight, flight and freeze, and called it fawn, appeasing the more powerful party and becoming what they need in order to make the danger drop. A daughter trained in that response will produce a flawless reconciliation on request, and it will not survive the next visit. [The fawn response has its own page](/patterns/fawn-response/) if that is the half you recognize.

There is a second reason the hardened part resists all three, and Richard Schwartz's Internal Family Systems model describes it well. That model treats the mind as made of parts, including protective ones that took on a role to manage something painful, and its practical instruction is to address a protector directly, thank it for the job it did, and negotiate rather than override it. Applied here, the toughness that got built against a weak mother is a protector with a genuine service record. Attacked, it digs in. Credited first, it tends to loosen its grip on decisions it has been quietly making for thirty years.

## What Does the Resolved End of This Actually Look Like?

Duller than you would expect, and made mostly of things that stop happening.

Describing only the wounded end of a pattern leaves a reader with an accurate account of a problem and nowhere to walk, so here is the same territory as a polarity.

| The old arrangement | The other end of it | What it looks like on an ordinary Tuesday |
|---|---|---|
| Managing her feelings about your own news | Reporting your news and stopping there | You mention the promotion, she says something odd, the day continues |
| Contempt standing in for grief | The loss felt directly | You can say she could not do it, without needing her to be small |
| Grading her against a standard she never took on | Seeing the framework mismatch plainly | Her opinion registers as her opinion |
| One remark costing you a day | The remark sized correctly | Irritation for ten minutes, then it is Tuesday again |
| A rehearsed conversation you never have | Something small actually said, once | You say the true sentence and do not explain it afterward |
| Being reliably triggered, and calling it strength | Nothing available on demand | She says the thing, and there is no output to collect |
| The relationship legible only through its worst five minutes | The full record available | You can hold both the kitchen and the ribbons at the same time |
| Guilt at any distance | Distance chosen and costed | A weekend not spent there is a weekend not spent there |

Notice what is absent from the right-hand column. There is no reconciliation scene, no conversation where she finally understands, no version where she becomes a different woman. Every row is available whether she participates, refuses, or died in 2011.

## What Actually Moves It?

Two separate pieces of work on two very different timelines, and confusing them is the single most common way people conclude they have finished when they have done half.

Insight is not one of the two. Naming the parentification, spotting the mirrored pattern, tracing the founding belief back to a kitchen floor: all of that can be grasped in an afternoon and changes remarkably little on its own, the same way knowing where a habit came from has never once broken the habit. [Why understanding a pattern so reliably fails to shift it has its own page](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/), and it applies here more brutally than almost anywhere.

The first piece of work is fast and entirely internal.

1. **Do the audit you have only ever done on the louder parent.** Two questions, asked specifically about her: what did she require of me, and what was forbidden? Write whatever comes, including nothing. An empty answer is a finding rather than a failure, and it is usually the beginning of the sparse version.
2. **Find the marker memory.** Your earliest memory involving her, in as much physical detail as you can retrieve. Then the harder move: what was happening in the ten seconds immediately before it? That is where the decision got made, and it is almost never the part anybody remembers first.
3. **Say the conclusion out loud, in its childhood wording.** I'm not good enough. She didn't want me. I had to do it myself. Adults tend to translate these into something more respectable on the way out, and the translation is what keeps them out of reach.
4. **Retrieve the counter-memory and re-enter it.** Something from roughly the same period showing care that was genuinely given. The clothes, the meals, the walk, the specific way she said your name. Get it back in the present tense with real sensory detail until it has weight as evidence rather than existing as a debating point. This is the piece that can move inside a single sitting, because it asks nothing of her whatsoever.
5. **Credit the toughness before retiring it.** Name what the hardness did for you, specifically. A survival strategy that has been thanked goes quietly. One that gets attacked digs in and comes back harder.

The second piece is slow, relational, and the part almost everybody skips.

1. **Test the current relationship rather than the remembered one.** One real request, one real limit, one true sentence said on an actual phone call. Rehearsing it for six years is the pattern running, and the rehearsal is designed to never finish.
2. **Say it once and stop explaining.** The explanation is where the old role climbs back in, because explaining is how you have always managed her feelings about your own choices.
3. **Expect her response to be limited.** She may be defensive, blank, hurt, or entirely unchanged. The point is finding out what is true now, rather than securing a particular reaction, and treating her reaction as the scoreboard hands the whole project back to her.
4. **Repeat across months, not once.** One cathartic exchange is a story people tell. A different relationship is built out of thirty unremarkable interactions that went slightly differently from the previous thirty.
5. **Let the guilt arrive without obeying it.** It will show up strongly at the first several limits, because a reversed role reads any distance as abandoning a post. Feel it, keep the limit, and watch how fast it declines by the fifth repetition.

Is any of that comfortable? No. And expect the first piece to feel disproportionately larger and faster than the second, which is exactly how people mistake having made peace with the belief for having actually changed the relationship. Both count. Only one of them requires a phone call.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

The internal half can shift in a sitting. The relational half is measured in months, and it is rarely a straight line.

That gap catches people off guard, so it is worth setting expectations properly. Retrieving a real counter-memory and letting it sit beside a thirty-year-old verdict can produce a genuine, same-day change in how she registers in your chest. It feels enormous, because it is. Then you call her on Sunday and the old sequence runs at full speed, and the temptation is to conclude the shift was imaginary. It was not. You changed one of the two things, and the other one is a habit two people have practiced for decades.

Progress in the relational half runs in a predictable order, and knowing the order lets you recognize the early stages as progress rather than as failure. First you notice an hour after the call, usually while lying in bed replaying it. Then twenty minutes after. Then during, which is the worst stage, because you are fully aware of what you are doing and doing it anyway. Then, eventually, early enough that a choice exists. Each transition takes a stack of real instances rather than any amount of resolve.

Expect the old pattern back under load. Illness, a holiday visit, a family crisis, a bereavement, or simply a run of bad sleep will bring the original version back, sometimes long after you were confident it was retired. The measure that tells you something is how fast you notice and how quickly it settles, rather than whether it arrived.

And be careful with the version of this where she is very old, or ill, or gone. The internal half stays fully available in every one of those cases. What changes is that the relational half turns into something you do alone, which is slower and sadder and still works.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From five bodies of published work, none of which have anything to do with me and all of which can be checked without going through me.

John Bowlby set out the concept of the internal working model across the three volumes of *Attachment and Loss*, published between 1969 and 1980: a template for closeness, dependency and repair assembled from what repeatedly happens with a caregiver rather than from what a caregiver says is true. Mary Ainsworth made it observable, running the Strange Situation procedure in Baltimore through the 1960s and 1970s and reporting it in *Patterns of Attachment* in 1978. Ivan Boszormenyi-Nagy, working in contextual family therapy, named parentification alongside the concept of invisible loyalties, which is the clinical term behind every version of I had to be the parent on this page. Carl Rogers supplied conditions of worth, the idea that approval given only on terms gets internalized as the terms of being acceptable at all. Murray Bowen's family systems theory contributes the multigenerational transmission process, the formal description of a pattern of emotional functioning moving down a family line and reappearing in a different surface form. Richard Schwartz's Internal Family Systems model is where the practice of addressing a protective part directly, crediting it before challenging it, comes from, and Pete Walker's addition of fawn to fight, flight and freeze is the standard reference for the compliant reconciliation described above.

The rest of this is mine and carries no research weight whatsoever. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind, I am not a therapist, and nothing here is an assessment of you or of your mother. None of the researchers named has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, go and check theirs.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Call Her?

Run the audit you have almost certainly only ever run on the louder parent, and give it twenty minutes.

Open a note and write her name at the top. Then two questions, and answer them about her specifically rather than about your family in general. What did she require of me? What was forbidden? Write whatever arrives, including nothing at all, and resist the urge to fill an empty answer with something from your father, which is what the pen will want to do. Then a third question, and this is the one that does the work: what would I have actually wanted from her? Most people have never asked it, get an uncomfortably quick answer, and find it is far simpler than they expected. To be wanted. To be asked about. To be pursued a little.

Then, on a separate line, write one memory of care she genuinely gave, from anywhere in your childhood, in as much physical detail as you can recover. What it looked like, what it smelled like, what she was doing with her hands. Take a full minute inside it rather than noting it down and moving on. Reading those two halves on the same screen does something that arguing with yourself has never once managed, and it costs you twenty minutes and nothing else. It is worth doing whether or not you ever go anywhere or speak to her again.
