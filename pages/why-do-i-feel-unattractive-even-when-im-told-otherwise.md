# Why do I feel unattractive when the person who loves me says otherwise?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

The belief survives because the one piece of evidence against it never gets counted. A partner's attraction arrives and gets filed as kindness, duty, or blindness rather than weighed as data, so a sweeping claim and a conceded exception sit side by side for years. What the belief actually does all day is suppress behavior: the getting ready, the being looked at, the fifteen minutes you stopped spending.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
Read these the way you would hear them in your own head, standing in a fitting room with the door shut.

- "I used to be. I'm not now."
- "When I was a size four, I was pretty freakin' sexy."
- "I look in the mirror and I see the big black eyes and the gray hair."
- "Well, I'm sexy to my husband."
- "I'd probably dress better and take time to take care of myself."

The fourth sentence is evidence, and the first three make certain it never counts as any, which is what turns a passing mood into a decade.

You are getting ready to go out and the person you live with looks up and says you look good. You say thank you, and somewhere inside that same half second, with nothing that could reasonably be called a decision, it has already been established that they had to say that.

Nothing about the exchange registers as strange while it is happening. It takes place in a kitchen, on an unremarkable evening, in about a second and a half, and it will happen again next week in roughly the same words.

What makes it worth stopping on is the arithmetic. You are holding a claim about yourself stated in absolute terms, and you are simultaneously holding a specific fact that flatly contradicts it, supplied by somebody with a decade of evidence and no obvious reason to lie. Both live in the same head. Neither one is ever put in front of the other.

Two different readers arrive at a page like this. One hears the compliment clearly, believes the person means it, and still cannot let it land, which is a problem with receiving and has [its own page here](/patterns/why-cant-i-accept-compliments/). The other hears it just as clearly, accepts that the person is sincere, and files it as untrue anyway. That second one is a problem with believing, and everything below is about that.

## What Is Actually Happening When a Compliment Gets Filed Instead of Counted?

Two claims are being held at once, and only one of them is being treated as information.

The first claim is global, absolute, unqualified: not attractive, period, to anyone, in any form. The second is specific, local, and already conceded: this person, who has looked at you across ten years of ordinary mornings, finds you attractive and keeps saying so out loud. In almost any other domain, a verified specific fact beats a sweeping assertion. Here the sweeping assertion wins every time, and it wins on a technicality. It arrives as a feeling. The fact arrives as an argument.

A felt truth requires no evidence. It shows up already certain, ahead of any inspection, the way a temperature does. A filed exception has to be retrieved, examined, and consciously admitted, and almost nobody does that in a kitchen on a Thursday. So the two coexist, one running as the background weather of your self-assessment and the other sitting in a folder nobody opens.

Watch the filing happen and it is almost administrative in its efficiency. He has to say that. She is just being kind. Love is blind. They have not seen what I look like at seven in the morning. Every one of those is a folder, and each folder is labeled exception, and nothing in there is ever counted toward anything.

| What arrives | Where it gets filed | What it would be if it were counted |
|---|---|---|
| Your partner says you look good tonight | Obligation, said to keep the peace | A repeated observation from the closest witness you have |
| Somebody looks twice in the street | A mistake, or about the coat | Evidence collected from a stranger with nothing to gain |
| A friend says you seem well lately | About your mood, so it does not apply | The broader definition of the word being met in public |
| An old flirtation crosses your mind | It does not count anymore, or was never about you | A datable instance you already accepted at the time |
| A photograph you actually like | A good angle, a fluke of light | One counter-example to a claim that says there are none |

The belief has never had to survive its own most literal version. Said all the way out, it reads: not attractive to anyone, in any form, under any conditions. Almost nobody who holds it would defend that sentence if a stranger said it to them about somebody else. It operates as though it had already passed that test, and it has never once been asked to take it.

## What Does This Sound Like From the Inside?

Close to identical wording, from people who have never met, which is the first genuinely surprising thing about it.

These run at the level they actually occur, before anybody smooths them into a description of a mood:

- "I've gained about 40 pounds after two kids."
- "My husband still likes me."
- "I can still put on a pair of heels and walk down the street and get a whistle."
- "Like I'm falling in a deep abyss, a deep abyss of darkness."
- "I'm getting a little desperate, possibly. Yeah."

Read the second and third of those again, because something remarkable is happening in them. Both are evidence. Both were volunteered by the same person, in the same conversation, minutes after declaring themselves categorically unattractive. Neither one was permitted to disturb the declaration by so much as a degree.

A second set runs alongside the first and belongs to a completely different definition of the word:

- "I have a lot of love to give. I'm able to do more, and people tell me it's infectious, when I'm happy, they're happy too."
- "I believe I have a wonderful heart, and when I feel great, I'm able to do more for other people, and that makes them feel awesome. So, to me, that's sexy."

Those sentences describe somebody passing a test continuously while failing a different one, and grading themselves exclusively on the one they are failing.

## When Does This Need More Than a Page Like This?

Right here, near the top, because the boundary matters more than every practice further down.

If checking, measuring, covering, or correcting a specific feature has become intrusive, if it takes real time out of most days, if it drives repeated cosmetic consultations, or if it keeps you from leaving the house, that sits in clinical territory this page does not reach. There is an assessed clinical picture with the name body dysmorphic disorder behind that description, and it belongs with a licensed professional rather than with a belief-testing exercise or with a self-applied label from a web page. The same goes if eating is being restricted, skipped, or compensated for, or if the distress has hardened into hopelessness that has not lifted for months, or if there is any thought of harming yourself, however quickly it passes. Bring in a clinician. Asking in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve.

One more boundary belongs here rather than at the bottom. Everything below assumes the counter-evidence is real, meaning somebody who is genuinely and repeatedly attracted to you. If what you are actually receiving is criticism, comparison, withdrawal, or warmth that arrives only when you comply, no belief-testing exercise applies, and the problem is not sitting inside your self-assessment. [Whether the relationship itself is the thing to work on is a separate question](/patterns/should-i-leave-my-relationship/), and it deserves a straight look rather than another round of trying to think better of yourself.

## Why Doesn't Being Told You Are Beautiful Change Anything?

It cannot, and asking for it more often tends to make the whole thing worse rather than better.

Reassurance is a flipped statement delivered from outside. Said on its own, a flipped statement is a slogan, and it has to contend with a certainty that is already installed and already feels like fact. So it gets rejected on arrival, or it gets filed with everything else in the exception folder. Volume changes nothing. Saying it louder, more sincerely, or three times a day changes which words are used and nothing about which folder they land in.

Four standard responses get recommended for this, and each one aims at something adjacent to the actual mechanism.

| The standard fix | What it aims at | Where it stops short |
|---|---|---|
| Being told you are beautiful, more often | The content of the belief | A conclusion handed to you from outside never becomes evidence you searched for |
| Broadening the standard, loving every body | The cultural grading scale | Genuinely useful on that layer, and it never reaches the behaviors that stopped |
| Repeating affirmations at a mirror | The sentence itself | Contradiction of an installed certainty gets refused before it finishes |
| Losing the weight, fixing the feature | The body | The file reopens at a new threshold, usually within months |
| Arguing yourself out of the self-criticism | The accuracy of the thought | Accuracy was never what the thought was for |

The second row deserves saying plainly, because it is the most useful of the four and it still leaves the reader stuck. Someone can agree completely that beauty standards are arbitrary, historically recent, and imposed, and then go home and still not spend the fifteen minutes. Winning that argument was never going to restart the behavior. The belief has been operating as a switch on a set of actions rather than as a proposition open to debate, and a switch stays where it is when somebody hands you a better reason to move it.

## What Is the Belief Actually Doing All Day?

Suppressing a short list of specific behaviors, and doing that far more reliably than it has ever reported on your body.

There is a question that exposes this in about ten seconds, and the speed of the answer is the whole point. Ask what you would be doing differently right now if you had never had the thought at all. The answers come back immediately and concretely, and they are never about the body. "I'd probably dress better and take time to take care of myself." Dress well. Take time. Show up. Stop angling out of photographs. Nobody answers that question by describing a different face.

Here is the full working set, laid out as behavior rather than as feeling:

1. **Filing the exception.** Treating a partner's demonstrated attraction as a kindness, a duty, or a blind spot rather than as evidence against the claim it directly contradicts.
2. **The mirror audit.** Scanning for proof features each time you pass a reflective surface, a gray hair, a wrinkle, a stretch mark, a number on a tag, as though enough specific detail could settle a verdict that was decided before the scan began.
3. **Closing the file with used to be.** Describing your own attractiveness in the past tense, which forecloses the present-tense question without ever answering it.
4. **Withdrawing from the self-care that would test it.** Letting the effort lapse: the clothes, the time, the being seen. This removes any possibility of a disconfirming success and keeps the belief permanently untested.
5. **The loyalty-ritual self-critique.** Repeating the same criticism about your looks even though it demonstrably changes nothing, because its real function is proving you still care.
6. **Catastrophizing without naming the fear.** Describing the distress in enormous, vague terms, falling, disappearing, an abyss, rather than naming the specific and checkable thing being feared underneath.
7. **Overgeneralizing one dissatisfaction.** Turning a single disliked feature into a universal verdict about all of you, delivered to everybody, forever.

Now the part that makes this a loop rather than a bad mood. Number four generates fresh evidence for number one. Stop dressing with intention for two years and the reflection at the end of those two years genuinely does look less cared for, and that gets read as proof the original claim was correct all along. The belief has been quietly manufacturing its own supporting data for as long as it has existed, and every year of that data makes the next year's version harder to argue with.

Underneath all of it sits something almost reasonable. As long as you never fully try, you can never receive the more devastating verdict of having tried and still been found wanting. Declaring the case closed in advance, on your own authority, is a genuine form of protection. It is also the most expensive insurance policy most people ever buy.

## Is Saying You Used to Be Attractive Different From Saying You Are Not?

Yes, and the difference is probably the most useful thing on this page.

"I used to be. I'm not now." names a change. A change has a before, an after, and something that happened in between, which means it has a date attached to it whether or not anybody has looked the date up. A fixed characteristic has none of that. Somebody describing a permanent trait would say the trait, in the present tense, with no timeline in it at all.

So the past tense gives the game away while sounding like modest biography. What it quietly admits is that this is a report about an event, and events can be examined.

| | I used to be attractive | I am not attractive |
|---|---|---|
| What it claims | A change, with an implied start date | A fixed and permanent characteristic |
| What follows from it | Something happened, so something can happen again | Nothing available to do about it |
| Where the date usually points | To a year when a set of behaviors stopped | Nowhere in particular |
| How it could be tested | Resume what stopped and watch what happens | It cannot be, which is a large part of the appeal |
| What it feels like to say | Wistful, reasonable, almost humble | Flat, heavy, closed |

Go and find the date. Ask what year the sentence became true, then ask what else changed that year. In a great many cases the honest answer arrives with an uncomfortable order of events attached: the fifteen minutes stopped first, the photographs stopped a few months after that, and the appearance those behaviors had been maintaining followed both of them. That order matters, because it means the loss people describe is real and its cause has been misfiled.

## Where Does This Actually Start?

At an identifiable entry point, usually, and there are only about six common ones.

Physical change after children. An injury or an illness that altered how you move. Menopause, or the year the first gray came in. A birthday that landed harder than expected. A belief formed in childhood, taught early enough that it never felt like a belief. Or a sentence from a previous relationship that somebody said once and never took back. Different doors, one room. The mechanism on the other side is the same in each case, which is why this reads as one pattern rather than six.

That matters practically. People burn years looking for the specific origin, on the assumption that finding it will dissolve the belief. Finding it helps with something else entirely, which is understanding why the standard you are grading yourself against feels non-negotiable. [Where insecurities actually come from is worth reading on its own](/patterns/where-insecurities-come-from/), and the honest sequencing is that the origin explains the belief while the behavior is what moves it.

There is a second and slightly uncomfortable observation about entry points. Almost every one of them is a change that happened to you rather than a choice you made. Bodies change after children. Hair goes gray. Injuries land. What follows the change is where the choosing starts, and it is the only place any choosing is available.

## Whose Standard Are You Actually Failing?

One you did not set, could not have met for more than about a decade of your life, and did not consciously agree to be graded by.

Naomi Wolf's *The Beauty Myth*, published in 1990, is the best known argument that dominant standards of physical attractiveness are historically and culturally constructed, shift considerably over time, and have been used to constrain women in ways that have very little to do with beauty. It is a polemic as much as a survey and worth reading as one. The core observation holds up without needing the whole argument: whatever standard felt like reality when you were twenty-five was a recent convention with a start date of its own.

The academic account of how such a standard gets inside a person is objectification theory, set out by Fredrickson and Roberts in *Psychology of Women Quarterly* in 1997. Their proposal is that living in a culture that habitually evaluates bodies by appearance leads people to internalize that same narrow appearance-based standard and turn it on themselves, so the grading continues even with nobody in the room. That is a real, checkable, peer-reviewed piece of work, and it explains something the reassurance approach cannot: why the harshest grader is never the partner.

Then there is the definition problem underneath all of it.

| The narrow test | The broader test |
|---|---|
| A number on a clothing tag | Whether people feel better after twenty minutes with you |
| Hair color, and how much of it is gray | Warmth that other people describe as infectious |
| Whether the photograph matches the one from 2014 | Whether you are actually present in the room |
| An age bracket you have already left | Generosity, humor, the thing you do when somebody is upset |
| A body part you were told about once | The effect you have on the people you love |

Both of those are legitimate readings of the word attractive. Only one of them is being counted. Somebody who says "when I feel great, I'm able to do more for other people, and that makes them feel awesome, so to me, that's sexy" has already articulated the broader definition, and is passing it continuously, while grading themselves on the narrow one exclusively.

## Is Attraction a Fixed Checklist or Something That Gets Built?

Substantially built, which is exactly why a long-term partner's attraction fails to erode on the same schedule your self-assessment does.

The belief assumes attraction works as a static property: somebody either matches an external template or they do not, so any attraction that persists after you stop matching must be politeness. That binary is where most of the damage lives, because it leaves only two available readings of your partner's behavior, objective grade or charity, and one of them is insulting to both of you.

Attraction is porous to state. The best known demonstration is a 1974 study by Dutton and Aron, published in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, in which men who had just crossed a high, swaying suspension bridge reported markedly more attraction to a woman they met immediately afterward than men who crossed a low, stable one. A single study about strangers on a bridge settles nothing about a fifteen-year marriage, and it does establish the narrow point cleanly: physiological and emotional state feed directly into what registers as attraction.

Now scale that up. Your partner has not been assessing you against a template. They have been accumulating fifteen years of shared state: the difficult week you got through, the private joke nobody else understands, the way you move around a kitchen, the specific sound of you laughing at something. All of that is inside what they mean by the word. None of it gets disqualified by forty pounds or by gray hair, which is why their attraction and your self-assessment are running on completely different inputs and returning completely different answers.

## Why Does This Get Louder in Your Forties and Fifties?

Because what is actually under threat is a strategy, and the strategy was never built to survive time.

If being wanted has run largely on physical appeal, then aging reads as a currency depreciating on a schedule nobody controls, with no available response. That produces a specific flavor of dread that is different from the ordinary version of this pattern. It is oriented forward rather than at the mirror, and reassurance about today barely touches it, since the fear was never about today.

The useful move is to make the fear specific. Ask what precisely you are afraid the aging will cost. Being chosen. Being noticed in a room. Feeling secure in a relationship that currently feels fine. Being interesting to somebody new if this one ended. Each of those is a workable question with an answer that has nothing to do with the calendar, and each of them stops being a countdown the moment it gets named. Vague dread has no handles on it. A specific fear has several.

The longer answer involves finding a basis for feeling valued that is less time-limited than a face, which sounds like a platitude until you notice how much of daily behavior is currently organized around the other one. [When worth is contingent on performance, appearance is only one of the currencies](/patterns/conditional-self-worth/), and for many people it is the first one to come under obvious pressure. Some readers will also recognize a related loss of access to a whole side of themselves, [which is a different pattern with its own page](/patterns/why-do-i-feel-like-ive-lost-my-femininity/).

## Is the Self-Criticism Actually Trying to Correct Anything?

Frequently no, and that explains the thing about it that makes no sense otherwise: it survives being completely useless.

A criticism aimed at correction stops when correction fails. This one does not stop. It runs for decades, changes nothing, and gets repeated with undiminished conviction, which suggests correction was never its job. What it often does instead is function as a ritual, performed for an internal audience, proving that you are still paying attention, still trying, still have standards, still have not simply given up and let yourself go.

Read like that, its persistence makes sense. A ritual does not stop because it is ineffective. Ineffectiveness was never the point of it.

This has a practical consequence that surprises people. Arguing with the content changes nothing, since the content was incidental. What retires the ritual is finding another way to demonstrate that you still care, and the obvious candidate is the behavior itself. Fifteen minutes actually spent getting ready proves the caring far better than twenty minutes spent criticizing, takes about the same amount of the day, and has the additional advantage of producing something. [The habit of running yourself down as a form of self-management is worth understanding in its own right](/patterns/self-loathing/) if the criticism has spread well beyond your appearance.

## Are You Sure That Is the Pattern You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because five adjacent patterns produce sentences that sound almost identical from outside and need different work.

| What it sounds like | What it probably is | The question that separates them |
|---|---|---|
| "I know they mean it, I just cannot take it in" | A receiving problem | Do you believe the statement is true, or only that they believe it |
| "I do not believe them, I think they are being kind" | The pattern on this page | Is there a specific person whose attraction you have already conceded |
| "I am not enough, at anything, generally" | Diffuse inadequacy | Is the claim anchored to appearance or does it cover everything |
| "I am worth something when I am producing" | Worth contingent on output | Does a good week at work change how you feel about your face |
| "I need to hear it again by tonight" | A reassurance loop | How long does the relief last after you are told |
| Hours a day on one feature, canceled plans | Clinical territory | Is it intrusive, and is it taking real time out of the day |

Each of those has somewhere better to go. If the compliment lands and cannot be received, [that mechanism is covered here](/patterns/why-cant-i-accept-compliments/). If the claim is general rather than anchored to appearance, [the broader version is a different pattern](/patterns/why-do-i-feel-not-good-enough/). If the relief from being told lasts twenty minutes and then reopens, [the checking loop is the thing to work on](/patterns/seeking-constant-reassurance/) rather than the belief about your face. Two pages competing for one problem help nobody, and picking the right one first saves months.

The distinguishing feature of the pattern on this page is that it is unusually falsifiable. It makes a specific claim, it has a datable onset, and it is anchored to a piece of counter-evidence you have already conceded out loud. That is unusual, and it is good news. Most self-critical beliefs offer nothing so concrete to work with.

## What Actually Moves It?

Two things, running at completely different speeds, and most people do the fast one and skip the slow one.

The fast part is intellectual and can shift in a single sitting. The four-question form of this is publicly documented and free: Byron Katie's method, called The Work, asks whether a stressful thought is true, whether you can absolutely know it is true, how you react when you believe it, and who you would be without it. Nobody needs to buy anything to run those. What follows is a compressed version aimed specifically at this belief.

1. **Write the belief in its most absolute wording.** Not a softened version. Not attractive, to anyone, in any form, ever again. Then read it back as though a stranger had written it about somebody else.
2. **Name the exception you have already conceded.** One specific person, or one specific moment when you felt unmistakably good, or one photograph you actually like. Write the name or the date down next to the absolute sentence.
3. **Notice that both are on the same page now.** This is the first time in years those two things have been in the same place at the same time. Sit with that longer than feels necessary.
4. **Ask what believing it costs you, specifically, this week.** Not in general. This week: the invitation declined, the photograph avoided, the outfit left hanging, the fifteen minutes not taken.
5. **Ask what you would do differently if you had never had the thought.** Time your answer. Under ten seconds is normal, and the speed is the finding, since it shows the belief has been operating on behavior all along.
6. **Resume one suppressed behavior before you feel entitled to it.** This is the whole slow half, and it is the part that gets skipped. Fifteen minutes on an ordinary Tuesday, for no occasion. The belief will object throughout. Do it while it objects.
7. **Let yourself be looked at once this week.** Stay in the photograph. Do not angle out. Do not check it afterward and delete it.
8. **Say thank you and add nothing.** No rebuttal, no correction, no returned compliment to level the exchange. The internal rebuttal will still arrive for months. It simply stops being given a mouth.
9. **Retire the mirror audit by making it finite.** If you are going to compare against 2014, name the exact photograph and the exact year, out loud, and then name what you were doing differently that year. Specific comparison surfaces behavior. Vague comparison only ever delivers a verdict.
10. **Give the loyalty ritual a different job.** When the criticism starts, treat it as a reminder that you still care, then discharge the caring through an action within the hour rather than through another round of commentary.

Is any of this comfortable? No, and the discomfort is the mechanism rather than a side effect. What is being retrained is a pattern of avoidance, which only weakens against instances of not avoiding. It is small, repetitive, and freaking unglamorous, and it works considerably better than the version where you wait to feel differently first.

There is a real clinical tradition behind the shape of this, and it is worth naming so you can go and check it. Thomas Cash spent decades developing and testing cognitive-behavioral approaches to body image distress, including the Multidimensional Body-Self Relations Questionnaire and *The Body Image Workbook*, built around identifying and testing distorted appearance-related beliefs rather than changing the body. That is clinical work, conducted by clinicians, and the list above borrows its logic without being any part of it.

One handoff worth making here. The ten moves above are aimed at a specific falsifiable claim, and once that claim has stopped functioning as a fact there is a broader practice underneath it that this page deliberately does not cover. [What it actually takes to treat yourself decently over years, rather than to win one argument about your appearance, sits on its own page](/patterns/how-to-love-yourself/), and it is the sensible next thing to read once the fifteen minutes have resumed.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

The intellectual floor can collapse in an afternoon. The behavior takes weeks to months, and the feeling arrives last.

That order is the source of most of the frustration. People run the audit, feel the belief lose its grip, and expect the feeling to follow within days. It does not. Belief in this area lags behavior by a considerable margin, which means the honest version of the middle phase involves dressing well while still privately convinced there is no point, being photographed while still wanting to check the photograph, and saying thank you while a rebuttal runs underneath it.

Nobody enjoys that phase and there is no shortcut through it. What tends to happen instead, somewhere between week three and month four, is that the objection gets quieter without ever being defeated, and one day you notice you got ready without having had the argument at all.

Progress here also refuses to be linear. A bad photograph, a comment from a relative, an illness, or a change of season will reinstall the whole thing for a fortnight. Expect that rather than reading it as failure. The measure worth watching is how many days it takes before the fifteen minutes resume, and that number falls long before the feeling does.

## What If Nobody Has Told You Otherwise in Years?

Then the first question is whether anybody has been given the chance.

Some readers arrive here with no conceded exception to work with, and the reason is worth examining before concluding that the absence of compliments is a verdict. If the withdrawal has been running for a long time, if you have arranged your life so that being looked at rarely happens, if photographs are avoided and effort is never made and no situation arises where anybody would comment, then the silence is a product of the behavior rather than a judgment on the face. That is a genuinely different position from a partner whose compliments get discounted, and it needs the behavioral half of this work more urgently rather than less.

The other version is harder and needs naming plainly. If somebody who is present in your life is actively critical of how you look, if warmth arrives conditionally, or if comparison is used to keep you compliant, none of the belief-testing here applies to that situation. That is a fact about the relationship rather than a distortion in your self-assessment, and the useful work is a different and more difficult conversation. A page cannot do that one for you, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From five public sources, every one of which can be checked without going through me.

Byron Katie's four-question inquiry, The Work, has been publicly documented since the 1990s and is freely available, which is why the belief-testing sequence above points at it rather than at anything proprietary. Naomi Wolf's *The Beauty Myth*, published in 1990, is the widely cited case that dominant beauty standards are historically constructed and shift by era rather than describing anything fixed. Objectification theory, published by Fredrickson and Roberts in *Psychology of Women Quarterly* in 1997, is the peer-reviewed account of how a culture that grades bodies by appearance produces people who grade themselves the same way. The 1974 suspension bridge study by Dutton and Aron, in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, is the standard reference for attraction being shaped by state rather than fixed to appearance alone. And Thomas Cash's cognitive-behavioral body image work, including *The Body Image Workbook* and the Multidimensional Body-Self Relations Questionnaire, is the clinical tradition that established testing appearance beliefs as the route rather than changing the body.

The rest of it is mine and carries no research weight whatsoever. I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind and none of the researchers or authors named above has any connection to this page or to anything I run. Where their published work and my own experience disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Change Anything About How You Look?

Take a piece of paper and write three lines on it, in this order. First, the belief in its most absolute possible wording, the version you would never say out loud to somebody else about themselves. Second, the single most specific piece of evidence against it that you already hold: a person, a date, a photograph, a moment you remember clearly. Third, one sentence answering what you would be doing this week if you had never had the thought.

Then look at the three lines together. Most people write the third one in under ten seconds and are mildly startled by it, because it is always a behavior and never a face. Line one has been running your week. Line two has been sitting in a folder marked exception for years. Line three is the thing that has actually been taken from you, and it is available again on Tuesday, at no cost, whatever you decide about anything else. That exercise costs you a sheet of paper and about four minutes, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever go anywhere.
