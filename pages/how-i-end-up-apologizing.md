# Why does every conversation I start about a problem end with me apologizing?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because a conversation can only hold one subject at a time, and yours got replaced early. You opened with what happened. The reply was about how you said it, when you said it, and what you are like. Sometimes that swap is deliberate. Far more often two people arrived with something to raise and the steadier one kept the floor.

You had it clear in the car. One thing, one sentence, no history attached. The handover did not happen and you found out from somebody else, and you were going to say exactly that and then stop talking.

Nineteen minutes later you are explaining that you did not mean it the way it sounded.

Somewhere in between, the subject changed. Not loudly. There was no moment you could point at. You said the thing, and the answer was that they had been under real pressure this month, and that you have a way of coming at people, and that you did the same thing in the spring, and had you considered that you might be reading a lot into a small oversight. Every one of those got a reply from you, because each one seemed like something that had to be dealt with before you could get back to the handover.

You never got back to the handover.

What you got instead was a new matter, which is your delivery, and which is now open and which you agreed to. You apologized for it. The apology was sincere, because by that point you did feel that you had come in hot, and the feeling of having come in hot arrived during the conversation rather than before it.

Then you stood on the driveway for a while and could not work out what had happened.

That evening the sequence runs again in your head, and again on the stairwell the next morning, and you cannot locate the seam. It did not feel like being attacked. It felt like a series of reasonable points that you kept answering, and the reason you cannot find the moment it turned is that there probably was not one.

That is a real and specific service, staffed by people who do this work daily, and it is far better than anything you can assemble alone or read on a website. If that paragraph fits your situation, the rest of this page is not written for you and you can stop here without missing anything you need. Everything below is written for the other case, where nobody has hurt you, where you are safe, and where conversations you start keep ending with you as the one who was in the wrong.

## What Does the Turn Actually Look Like, Minute by Minute?

Smaller and faster than the word most people reach for suggests.

It starts with a swap you agree to. You say the thing about the handover. The reply is a question about why you always bring these up on a Sunday. You answer the question, because it was asked and because answering questions is what you do, and the conversation is now about Sundays.

It continues with a set of true statements that are not about your subject. They have been busy. They are doing their best. Last time you raised something it turned out to be nothing. All three can be entirely accurate and none of them touches whether the handover happened.

It includes a piece of your own history, produced from somewhere, usually with a date attached. Not as an attack. As context. And the context has the effect of making your current point look like the latest instance of something rather than a thing in itself.

It includes a moment where you notice, faintly, that you are now defending your character, and you decide to deal with that first and get back to the original matter afterward. That decision is the hinge, and it never feels like one.

It ends with a settlement neither of you would describe as a win. You apologize for how you came at it. They accept, warmly, and possibly apologize back for something small and adjacent. The temperature drops. The relief is enormous and it lasts about six seconds after the door closes.

And it leaves a residue you will still be carrying on Thursday, made of two parts. One is that the handover is still unaddressed and now cannot be raised without becoming proof. The other is a small new file with your name on it, in which you are somebody who comes at people.

None of this is dramatic. Nobody shouted. That is precisely why it accumulates unexamined for years, and why people who have been in it since their twenties still arrive at a page like this asking whether it counts as anything.

## What Is the Name for This, and How Much Does the Name Actually Know?

The term is **DARVO**, and it stands for deny, attack, and reverse victim and offender.

It was named in 1997 by a psychology researcher working on how people and institutions respond when they are accused of causing harm. The observation was about a sequence rather than a type of person. Somebody is confronted about something they did. First the account is denied. Then the person raising it is criticized, often for the manner, the motive, or the timing of raising it. And then the positions swap, so that by the end the person who came in with a complaint is the one who has caused the trouble and the one who was complained about is the one who has been treated unfairly.

Written out like that, it describes the driveway exactly. That is why the term has spread so far and so fast.

**What the term genuinely captures** is the ordering. Not that someone disagreed, and not that someone got defensive, but that a specific three-part move happened in a specific order and that the roles ended up inverted. Naming the ordering is useful, because the ordering is the part people cannot describe afterward and therefore cannot explain to anybody else.

**What the term cannot tell you** is anything about why, and anything about who was right. It is a description of a shape. It says nothing about whether the denial was honest, nothing about whether the criticism of your delivery was fair, and nothing about what would change it. There is no scale that settles a case, no threshold, and no agreed number of incidents.

And then there is the evidence, which has to be stated plainly because almost nothing written about this term states it. The sequence is a described pattern rather than a measured mechanism. Scales for measuring it exist, they are recent, they are short, and they rely on people reporting on their own experience or reacting to written scenarios rather than on anybody observing real conversations. A good deal of that measurement work comes from the same research group that coined the term, which means it is better understood as one line of work developing its own idea than as independent confirmation from elsewhere. Nothing establishes how often the sequence occurs in ordinary life, and there is no basis for any figure about how common it is.

The larger problem is what popular use has done to it. In its original setting the word described a response to a serious accusation. In general use it has broadened until it covers any disagreement in which somebody defends themselves. That broadening is not a small drift, and it is the reason the term is dangerous in ordinary conflict.

| What the term was introduced to describe | What it now routinely gets applied to |
|---|---|
| A response to being confronted about real harm | Any disagreement where the other person disagrees back |
| A sequence with a recognizable order | Any single defensive sentence |
| A pattern established across repeated incidents | One conversation that went badly |
| Roles that end up genuinely inverted | A conversation that simply did not go your way |
| Applied after the facts are reasonably clear | Applied to decide who is right in the first place |

The part that makes this genuinely hard belongs in the label section rather than buried later. A person accused of something they did not do will also deny it. They will also feel wronged. They will very often criticize the way the accusation was made, because being accused of something untrue is an unpleasant experience that produces exactly those three responses. Deny, defend, and feel like the injured party is also what innocence looks like from the outside. The sequence cannot distinguish the two, and any page that tells you it can is selling you certainty that the idea does not contain.

There is a related word this page does not own. Gaslighting goes after your record of what happened, so that you leave less sure of the facts, and it is [covered properly on its own page](/patterns/not-trusting-my-own-memory/). The distinction is worth holding. Gaslighting argues with the event. What this page describes leaves the event alone and argues with your standing to have raised it.

## How Do You Tell the Reversal From a Conversation That Simply Went Badly?

This is the question the whole page exists to answer, and almost nobody writes it down, because writing a checklist for identifying a villain is easier and gets shared more.

The honest answer starts with a limit: one conversation cannot tell you. A single exchange in which you ended up apologizing is compatible with a reversal, with two people each having something to raise, with you having opened badly, and with the other person being right. Anybody who offers you a test that resolves a single conversation is describing something the evidence does not support.

What can be distinguished is a pattern across many conversations, and the useful test has three parts.

**Does the subject ever come back?** In a conversation that simply went badly, the original point is available again later. You can raise the handover the following week and get an answer to it. In a reversal, every route back to the original subject is already occupied. Raising it again is itself the problem.

**Does the direction ever change?** In two people with two grievances, the roles trade. Sometimes you are the one who ends up conceding and sometimes they are. Across a year the ledger is untidy in both directions. In a reversal, the direction is stable. You could predict the ending before the conversation started, and you would be right nearly every time.

**Does your own account survive contact with an ordinary outside description?** Try describing what you raised to somebody with no stake in it, using only what happened and none of your interpretation. If the neutral description is still recognizable as a reasonable thing to raise, that is worth something. If the neutral description turns out to be hard to produce at all, that is also information.

| Question | A conversation that went badly | Two subjects, one floor | The reversal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can the original point be raised again later? | Yes, and often is | Usually, once things cool | Raising it again becomes the new evidence |
| Who ends up conceding across a year? | Both, unpredictably | Whoever holds a topic better | The same person, nearly every time |
| Does the other person ever accept a point? | Frequently | Sometimes, later | Rarely in a way that changes anything |
| Was the second subject genuinely real? | Sometimes | Yes, and it deserved a hearing | Usually assembled during the conversation |
| Does the ending feel like relief or resolution? | Resolution | A rough draw | Relief, followed by something worse |

Notice what that table does not do. It does not tell you which column you are in. It tells you what to watch, over months, in a situation where a single instance is genuinely ambiguous and where the temptation to resolve the ambiguity quickly is the strongest force in the room.

## Why Does a Conversation With Two Subjects Always Resolve Toward the Louder One?

Because a conversation is a queue with one position, and nothing decides the order except who holds it.

This is the most common version of the whole pattern and the one nobody names, because it has no villain in it and therefore makes a poor story. You came in with the handover. They came in carrying something too, possibly for weeks, possibly about you, possibly about something entirely unrelated that your opening happened to touch. Two real subjects arrived in one conversation and there is no mechanism anywhere that decides which gets discussed first.

What actually decides it has very little to do with seriousness or fairness. The deciding factors are who can hold a topic while under pressure, who is more comfortable with the other person's discomfort, and who finds an unresolved conversation more expensive to carry. Those three things have nothing to do with who has the better point.

There is research on the shape of this, and it is worth having accurately rather than grandly. Sillars, in 1980, studied roommates in ongoing conflict and looked at how each person explained the disagreement. The pattern was consistent: people attributed their own conflict behavior to the situation they were in, and the other person's to what the other person is like. Each side also misjudged what the other one thought was going on. This was a small study, in a specific population, using self-report, and it should be held at that weight. What it describes is a mechanism rather than a moral failing, and it explains something you already know from the driveway: both of you left convinced the other one had made it about the wrong thing, and both of you were describing your own experience honestly.

Two sincere people can therefore produce a conversation that looks, from inside, exactly like a reversal. Nobody denied anything on purpose. Nobody swapped roles deliberately. One subject simply won the floor and the other did not, and the person whose subject lost carries the conversation home.

The reason this matters more than the dramatic version is arithmetic. If you sort every one of these conversations into the reversal column, you will be wrong most of the time, you will build a case against somebody who was not running a sequence, and you will make the next conversation harder in a way that produces more of exactly the evidence you were collecting.

## Why Do Two People Leave the Same Conversation Apologizing for Completely Different Reasons?

Because the behavior is the same and what produces it is not.

Five roots run underneath this, they look identical from outside, and the response to each one differs completely. Reading them as a list is useful. Deciding which is yours from a list is where it goes wrong, and a later section says why.

| Root | What it feels like from inside | What is actually happening |
|---|---|---|
| They are running the sequence | Confusing, and it never resolves | The reversal is real and this page is not the tool |
| Two subjects arrived | Unfair, and you cannot say why | One floor, two grievances, no agreed order |
| You opened it as a judgment | They overreacted to a fair point | A blame frame reliably produces a defense |
| Conflict is unbearable to you | Better to let it go and stay warm | You traded the matter for the peace, fast |
| Your account rarely survives | You were half conceded on arrival | An old rule about whose version counts |

## What If They Really Are Running the Sequence?

Then this is the root that matters most, and it is named first for that reason.

Some people do reliably answer a complaint by denying it, criticizing the person who raised it, and finishing as the injured party. It is a real thing that happens to real people, and if that is your situation you are not misreading it and you are not being oversensitive.

The safety line applies here before anything else. If you are afraid of them, if you are not free to leave, or if what you raise gets answered with anything touching your safety, your money, your movements, or who you are allowed to see, then that belongs with a licensed professional or a domestic abuse service now rather than eventually, and none of the rest of this page is the right tool for it.

Where there is no safety element and the pattern still holds across years, the honest thing to say is that this page cannot fix it, because the mechanism sits in another person and this site works on what a reader does themselves. There is a page here on [the moves this belongs to](/patterns/emotional-manipulation/), which handles the recognition question properly and includes the harder version of it, which is checking whether you are making any of the moves yourself.

What is left for you, and it is not nothing, is the sorting described at the end of this page. The matter you raised still exists. It did not stop being real because the conversation went the way it went, and deciding what you are going to do about it is available to you whatever the other person is doing.

## What If You Both Arrived With Something to Raise?

Then the conversation had two subjects, and the one that lost was not necessarily the smaller one.

The mark of this root is that the second subject was real. When you replay it, the thing they raised about your timing or your history had not been invented on the spot. They had been carrying it already. You had half known about it. It arrived badly, at the wrong moment, in response to something else, which is how most held grievances arrive.

This is the version that responds best to something extremely unglamorous, which is order. Two subjects in one conversation, with no agreement about which is first, resolves toward whoever holds the floor. Two subjects with an agreed order resolves toward both, slowly, over two conversations. Nothing about that requires the other person to change what they are like.

It also explains a thing that otherwise makes no sense, which is that you can be completely right about the handover and still leave feeling like the one at fault. Both were true. Only one got discussed.

## What If the Way You Opened It Invited the Defense?

This is the only root you can act on entirely alone, and it needs stating carefully, because it sits one word away from telling somebody that being treated badly was their own doing.

That is not what it says. What is true is narrower. A grievance can be opened as a description of an event or as a judgment about a person, both can be completely fair, and the two produce reliably different responses.

Malle and colleagues set out a model of blame that is useful here, and it is a theoretical account rather than an experiment, which is worth saying plainly. In that model, blame is a social act with conditions attached rather than a private feeling. It carries a claim that a particular person caused something, that they could have done otherwise, and that they should have. Once blame is stated, the normal and expected response is a justification or an excuse, because those are the moves the act invites. Defending yourself is what blame is for, structurally.

Which means an opening like "you never tell me anything" is doing something different from "I found out about the handover from somebody else." The first is a claim about a person and it invites a defense of the person. The second is a claim about an event and it invites an answer about the event. Both may be equally justified. They are not equally likely to get you an answer.

Two things have to be held together here and most writing drops one of them. The other person chose their response and owns it. And you control one input to a two-person system, that input has real effects, and it happens to be the only part of the machinery you can reach without anybody's agreement. Working on it is worth doing because it is available, and not because the outcome was ever your responsibility.

The signature of this root is specific. You can usually remember the exact sentence you opened with, it contained a word like always or never or again, and when you replay it you feel a small flinch that you then talk yourself out of.

## What If You Conceded Because the Conflict Itself Was Unbearable?

Then the apology was a purchase, and what it bought was the end of the conversation.

This root has nothing to do with who was right, and people in it often know perfectly well that they were right, which is why the evening afterward is so bad. The apology came out because the tension in the room had reached a level that had to stop, and apologizing is the fastest available way to stop it. It works. That is the problem. It works in seconds, reliably, every time, and nothing else in the situation works at all.

The trade is visible if you look at what you get and what you spend. You get the temperature down immediately and warmth restored. You spend the matter you came in with, and you take on a new charge about your delivery that will be available to both of you in future conversations. That is a poor exchange made under pressure by somebody who genuinely could not stay in the room another minute.

The reason this one does not respond to deciding to hold your ground is that holding your ground is a plan aimed at nerve, when the difficulty is cost. If staying in a conflict for another two minutes is genuinely expensive for you, a resolution does not change the price. It just means you pay it once, have a worse conversation, and conclude privately that raising things makes everything worse.

There is a fuller treatment of the surrounding pattern on [the page about avoiding conflict](/patterns/why-do-i-avoid-conflict/), which covers the version where the conversation never gets started, and [the page on apologizing reflexively](/patterns/over-apologizing/), which covers the word itself arriving before you have decided anything. This page owns the narrower moment where the conversation did start, and was yours, and got traded.

## What If Your Account Has Rarely Survived Contact?

Then you arrived at the conversation already partly conceded, and the reversal did not have far to travel.

Some people learned early that their version of events was the one that gave way. Not through anything dramatic. Through a household where one person's account was simply the account, or through years in which raising something reliably produced a worse outcome than not raising it. What that installs never becomes a belief you could state out loud. Something quieter goes in instead, a working assumption running underneath, that your read of a situation is provisional until somebody else confirms it.

Carried into an adult conversation, that assumption does something specific. It makes the first piece of pushback feel like information rather than like disagreement. Somebody says you might be reading a lot into this, and instead of being one view among two, it lands as a correction, and you update immediately because updating is what you have always done.

This is closely related to the pattern where your record of events itself stops feeling reliable, and that is [covered properly on its own page](/patterns/not-trusting-my-own-memory/). The distinction is that this root leaves your memory intact. You know exactly what happened. What went soft is your sense of being entitled to make anything of it.

The signature is that the conceding happens too early and too easily, before any real argument has been made, and that afterward you can reconstruct your original point perfectly, which tells you it never actually stopped being available to you.

## Which of the Five Is Yours, and How Would You Know?

Probably not by reading the list again, and this is worth being honest about rather than pretending a page can sort it.

Two things make this specific pattern unusually hard to self-diagnose. The first is speed. The swap happens across a few minutes, most of it while you are busy answering something, and self-observation runs slower than that. By the time you can watch it happening you have been in it for six years.

The second is that the only witness is you, and you are the person the conversation was about. Your account of the conversation is produced by the same equipment that produced the conceding, which is the reason people replay these evenings for days and arrive nowhere. More thinking with the same equipment does not add information.

What does help is an outside description from somebody with no stake, given the plain facts and none of your reading of them. That is different from telling a friend, because a friend will agree with you, and being agreed with feels like the matter has been dealt with when nothing has been dealt with at all. That trap has [its own page here](/patterns/pulling-someone-else-into-it/), and it is worth knowing about before you go looking for a second opinion.

There is one more reason to be careful about picking a root off a list. Four of these five point at you and one points at somebody else, and human beings do not choose between those options neutrally. Whichever direction you naturally lean, the list will tend to confirm it.

## Why Can You Never Locate the Exact Moment It Turned?

Because in most cases there was no moment, and you are searching a recording that was never made.

The turn is built out of four or five small transitions, none of which is a turn on its own. A question gets asked and you answer it. A piece of context arrives and you acknowledge it. A general observation about you gets made and you address it before returning to your point, except that addressing it is the return. Each step is locally reasonable and the sum is a different conversation.

Memory makes this worse rather than better. Recall of conversations is reconstructive, it is poor at ordering, and it fills gaps with whatever makes the sequence coherent. So the replay you run on the stairwell is smoother than the original, has a clearer structure, and often contains a decisive moment that you have supplied yourself because a story needs one.

Which is why the useful question is not where it turned. The useful question is whether the thing you came to raise ever got an answer, and that one you can answer with certainty, immediately, on any day, without reconstructing anything.

## Where Does This Hide When It Does Not Look Like Apologizing?

In the places where no apology is spoken and the same transaction happens anyway.

It hides in pre-emptive softening, where the handover complaint arrives wrapped in so much acknowledgment of how busy they have been that there is nothing left in it to answer.

It hides in the version where you raise it, get the pushback, and simply go quiet and change the subject yourself. Nobody apologized. The subject still died and you still filed the conclusion.

It hides in a written message you drafted three times, sent as a much shorter one, and then followed up with a second message an hour later saying it was not a big deal.

It hides in the laundry, or wherever your version of that is, where you conduct the conversation you meant to have with nobody in the room and it goes extremely well.

And it hides in the reasonable-sounding decision that this particular thing is not worth it, made about the ninth thing in a row, each of which was individually not worth it.

## What Does This Cost Over a Year?

More in the accumulation than in any single instance, which is the reason it stays unexamined.

The direct cost is a list of unaddressed matters. Not one handover. Eleven of them, or whatever your number is, each one dropped for a good reason at the time and none of them dealt with. That list has a weight and people carry it as a general heaviness about the relationship rather than as the specific items it is made of.

The second cost is that raising anything gets more expensive over time. Each conversation that ended with you apologizing raises the predicted cost of the next one, so you start pre-filtering, and the threshold for what is worth mentioning rises quietly until it sits somewhere above almost everything.

The third cost is the one people describe last and feel first, which is a slow revision of your own standing. Not confidence in general. Something narrower: a sense that your reading of situations is the one that gives way, held privately, and never tested because testing it is the thing that goes badly.

And the fourth is what it does to the other person, who is often not a villain and who is now in a relationship with somebody who has stopped raising things, which they will experience much later as distance appearing from nowhere.

## Why Does Naming the Term Out Loud Make the Conversation Worse?

Because you have added a second accusation to a conversation that could not hold the first one.

The pull to say it is completely understandable. You have finally found a word for a thing you have been unable to describe, and the word is precise and it fits, and saying it feels like it should end the argument.

What it does instead is change the subject one more time, and to a much worse subject. A dispute about a handover is small and answerable. A dispute about whether somebody is the kind of person who runs a manipulative sequence is enormous, is not answerable in an evening, and produces exactly the defensive response the term describes, which then reads as confirmation. Every route out of that is worse than where you started.

There is a further problem specific to this term, and it follows from the evidence being as thin as it is. The sequence cannot distinguish a guilty denial from an innocent one. So if you are wrong, and sometimes you will be, you have made a serious charge on the basis of a pattern that was never able to support it.

Keep the word. It is genuinely useful for organizing your own understanding of what keeps happening, and that is a real service. It just belongs in your own head, in the day afterward, rather than in the room.

## Which Part of This Did You Actually Choose?

Almost none of the conversation, and one thing afterward, and the difference between those two is the entire practical content of this page.

Inside the room, very little of it was chosen. The swap happened faster than deliberation. The apology came out under a pressure that was real. Whatever the other person did, they did. Treating any of that as a failure of will produces resolutions that do not survive their first test, which is why deciding to handle it better next time has now failed for you several times.

What is chosen sits the following day, and it is smaller and duller than any advice you have been given about this.

The matter you raised still exists. Nothing that happened in the conversation touched it. The handover still did not happen and you still found out from somebody else. That fact survived the whole evening intact, and it is sitting there in the morning entirely unaffected by how badly things went.

So the choice is this: what are you going to do about that specific thing, now, given that the conversation did not deal with it. Not how to raise it better. Not how to make them see. What you are going to do about the actual matter, which might be raising it again in a different form, or handling it yourself, or deciding it genuinely no longer matters and closing it deliberately rather than by default.

That question is available to you without anybody's cooperation, on any morning, regardless of which of the five roots turns out to be yours.

## What Does the Settled Version Look Like on an Ordinary Week?

Less impressive than people expect and considerably more restful.

Conversations still go badly sometimes. You still occasionally lose the floor, and one or two of them still end with you apologizing for a delivery that probably was sharp. What changes is the ratio and the residue.

You notice the swap while it is happening rather than on the driveway, not every time, maybe one in three at first. Noticing does not mean you stop it. Noticing means the evening afterward is shorter, because you are not searching for a moment you cannot find.

You open things differently without it feeling like a technique. Fewer sentences that begin with what somebody always does. More sentences that name a thing that happened on a specific day, which is a smaller and more answerable request.

You start finishing matters rather than conversations. The handover gets dealt with in some form within the week, sometimes by a route that has nothing to do with the person you originally raised it with.

And the list of dropped items stops growing, which you will notice as a general lightness some months before you work out where it came from.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

It depends entirely on which root is yours, and that is a real answer rather than a hedge.

Learning to separate a description of an event from a judgment about a person can move inside a few weeks, because it is a specific, practiceable thing with immediate feedback. Ordering two subjects in a conversation moves at a similar pace once both people know what is happening.

Conceding because the conflict itself is unbearable moves more slowly and unevenly, because the cost is real and does not drop just because you have understood it. Months rather than weeks, with visible regressions whenever you are tired.

A long history in which your account rarely survived is the slowest of the five. It moves in months, it does not respond to encouragement, and it tends to improve in one relationship well before it improves generally.

And a live safety situation is not on this list, because it is a different kind of problem with a different kind of help.

Expect it to come back under specific conditions. Exhaustion, since everything effortful gets dropped when you are depleted. Any period with several disputes running at once. And immediately after an occasion when you genuinely did come at somebody unfairly, which reinstates the old rule almost completely for a while.

## What Is Worth Doing Tomorrow, About the Thing That Never Got Discussed?

One sentence and one decision, and nothing else this week.

Open a note tomorrow morning and write the thing you originally went in to raise. Only that. What happened, on what day, in plain words. No mention of how the conversation went, nothing about their response, nothing about your tone. If you cannot write it without including the conversation, that is worth knowing on its own.

Then decide one of three things about it. That it still matters and you will raise it again in that plain form. That it still matters and you will handle it another way that does not require them. Or that it has stopped mattering and you are closing it on purpose.

Do that for the next four things. Then read the four sentences together in one sitting.

You are looking for something you cannot currently see, which is whether your matters are real and get lost, or arrive already tangled with a great deal of other material. That answer is not available to you at the moment, and it has not been for years, because the evidence keeps getting destroyed by the conversation that would have produced it.

If what landed hardest was something else, the recognition that you can predict the ending before you open your mouth, or that you have not finished a matter with this person in about three years, or that you concede before any real argument has been made, then the thing worth working on sits underneath the conversation rather than inside it.
