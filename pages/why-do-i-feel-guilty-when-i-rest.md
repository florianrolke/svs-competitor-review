# Why do I feel guilty when I rest?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because rest was never established as neutral in the household you learned it in. Rest arrived as something released after enough output, so stopping before you have earned it registers as a small infraction, and guilt shows up to correct it. The feeling is reporting an old rule rather than delivering an accurate verdict on how much you have done.

## What Is the Guilt Actually Reporting?

A rule about when rest becomes allowed, stated so flatly and so long ago that it stopped registering as an opinion.

Ordinary guilt points at a repair. It says you did something to somebody, and it comes with an obvious next move: a call, an apology, a thing returned. This one has no injured party and no repair available. Nobody was harmed by the nap. No debt got run up by the two hours on the sofa. Yet the feeling has the same texture as the useful kind, which is why people spend years treating it as accurate information about their own character rather than as an alarm going off in an empty building. [The difference between guilt that points at a repair and guilt that points at you is worth settling properly.](/patterns/shame-vs-guilt/)

What it is actually reporting is a sequence. Output first, then rest. Rest taken after enough production feels permitted, sometimes even good. The identical rest taken before it feels like theft, which is why so many people describe an ordinary free afternoon in the language of getting away with something. The clock is the same. The activity is the same. The accounting underneath it decides everything about how the hour feels.

That is also why the guilt so often arrives ahead of the tiredness. A person in this pattern can name the moment the guilt starts, usually within ten minutes of sitting down, and cannot name the moment they got tired, because tiredness was never the variable being tracked. The variable being tracked was whether anything visible was being produced.

Notice how little this has to do with your actual workload. People carrying this pattern feel it after a ninety-hour week and after a slow one. If the guilt tracked genuine idleness it would go quiet during the hard months, and for almost nobody does it.

## When Does This Belong With a Doctor or a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, before any of the practical material, because this boundary matters more than every suggestion below it put together.

If what you are living with has turned into hopelessness that has not lifted for months, if you are having any thought of harming yourself, or if you are managing a diagnosed condition, that belongs with a doctor or a licensed professional rather than with a self-directed practice. Contact a crisis line today if that is where you are. Nothing written here substitutes for a person in the room.

Two more situations belong there as well. The first is physical. Months of six-hour nights, a stomach that has stopped working properly, a tiredness that a weekend no longer dents, all of that is a medical question first and a psychological one second. [The exhaustion side of this has its own page](/patterns/emotional-exhaustion/), and asking for help in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve.

The second is specific to this pattern and gets missed often. If the guilt reliably gets switched on by one particular person in your life, if rest becomes expensive only when a specific partner, parent, or manager is watching, then you are looking at a present-day dynamic rather than an old rule, and it wants a different response. [How guilt gets used deliberately is covered separately](/patterns/emotional-manipulation/), and a control dynamic belongs with a licensed professional rather than with a practice you run alone.

## Is This the Pattern, or Are You Just Genuinely Busy Right Now?

The calendar tells you very little. What tells you is what happens on the day the calendar actually empties.

Someone who is simply busy uses the gap to rest, because the pressure lived in the schedule and it lifts when the schedule does. Someone carrying this pattern either fills the gap back in or feels worse inside it, because what is being avoided is the exposure that comes with stopping rather than the idle time itself. Several fairly different situations produce a similar-looking month, and the right response to each one differs completely.

| What you are seeing | What it might actually be | What tells them apart on a genuinely empty day |
|---|---|---|
| A packed few months with no downtime in them | A genuinely full season | It ends. When the deadline passes, the gap gets used for rest, and the rest feels good rather than uneasy |
| Long hours pointed at something you want | Being driven | Drive survives an empty week. Nothing is at stake in the gap, so the gap is simply a gap |
| Hours poured into getting one thing exactly right | Perfectionism | Perfectionism organises around a verdict on the work. This fires once there is no work left for a verdict to land on |
| Needs going unlogged across the board: skipped checkups, unopened post, no dentist in four years | Self-neglect | Self-neglect is passive and broad. This is active and narrow, a charge that fires the instant rest is attempted |
| An hour that gets filled before you have decided anything | Compulsive busyness | The filling happens ahead of the feeling. Here the feeling arrives even on the days you fill nothing |
| Rest actually taken, with a charge of wrongness attached to it | Guilt when resting, the pattern on this page | Time, health, and permission are all present, and stopping still registers as an infraction |

The third row separates more cleanly than people expect. [Perfectionism is a standard applied to output](/patterns/how-to-stop-being-a-perfectionist/), which is why lowering the bar does so little for anybody carrying it. Guilt when resting is what happens once there is no output left to apply a standard to. The two travel together constantly, in the same person on the same weekend, and they want different repairs.

The fourth row matters for a different reason. [Self-neglect is broader and quieter](/patterns/self-neglect/): needs that simply never get logged, a body that only gets attention once something is already serious, a general absence of maintenance. This pattern is much narrower and considerably louder. It fires at a specific moment, with a specific charge, even when time, health, and everyone's blessing are all sitting there in front of you.

The test that separates all of them takes one day rather than a month. Clear a Saturday, put nothing in it, tell nobody it is an experiment, and watch the first two hours. A full season sleeps in it gratefully. Drive sits in it and chooses what comes next. This pattern produces a task by eleven, or produces guilt, or both.

## Why Does an Empty Calendar Feel Like Exposure?

Because stopping is the one condition under which the thing you have been quietly proving could get disproved.

Three separate exposures arrive in the same empty afternoon, and most people can feel all three without ever having separated them. The first is replaceability. If nothing falls over while you are sitting down, a question you have been outrunning for years gets an answer, and it is not the answer you were braced for. Plenty of people discover that being needed had become the main evidence they were worth having around, and an uneventful week without them is a genuinely difficult thing to look at.

The second is competence. Where the household ran on evaluation, output was the surface a verdict could land on favourably. Take the output away and the surface goes with it. A body at rest offers nothing to point at if the verdict arrives, which is why unstructured time gets crowded out first, ahead of anything with a deliverable attached to it.

The third is the quietest and lands hardest. A slow day asks what you want, and the role never required an answer to that. If most of your adult life has been organized around being useful, an open afternoon can reveal a self that was never separately developed from the job, the caretaking, or the reliability. That is a genuinely uncomfortable thing to meet at three in the afternoon with nothing else scheduled. [Where that goes if it turns out to be the main event is covered here.](/patterns/i-dont-know-who-i-am-anymore/)

Underneath all three sits something simpler and much older. An idle body is an unmonitored one, and in a house where things could fall apart quickly, unmonitored was never safe.

## Where Did the Rule About Earning Rest Come From?

From three fairly different childhoods that converge on the same Sunday afternoon.

**A household where the standard set the weather.** Where expectations ran high and worth attached to achievement rather than being given as a baseline, slowing down without having earned it read as a lapse rather than as recovery. The child learns to police their own standard the way an outside evaluator would, and unstructured time becomes one more window where that evaluator is watching. [The parental version of this has its own page](/patterns/unrealistic-parental-expectations/), and [the valuation it installs has another](/patterns/conditional-self-worth/).

**A household carrying more than it could hold.** Where adult-sized duties arrived early, the child concluded that carrying the load alone was the only safe arrangement. Stopping, in that arithmetic, is the exact moment the load could fall and be seen falling. Adults from this route are excellent in a crisis, poor at being helped, and describe their tiredness long before they describe anything else. [Growing up ahead of schedule leaves its own signature.](/patterns/i-had-to-grow-up-too-fast/)

**A household where affection was paid for with caregiving.** Where a child got installed as the family's functional or emotional caretaker, feeding a parent's needs, running a household, absorbing another adult's moods, affection itself arrived conditioned on the caregiving. The child's own needs, rest included, became the first thing traded away, and got relabelled from ordinary requirement into imposition. [That arrangement has a name and a page.](/patterns/parentification/)

Most people recognize one route strongly and a second faintly, and a single household can supply all three without anybody involved intending anything of the kind. What matters is the thing they have in common. In none of them was stopping modeled as something that happens on a schedule regardless of output. It got modeled as the gap right before the next demand, or as the moment competence might finally be questioned.

Not every case traces back to a parent. A first job under a manager who only ever spoke to correct. A field that measures people in visible units and says so out loud. A stretch where the household finances genuinely did depend on you being relentless. A hospital year, a startup year, a year of somebody else's illness. The mechanism holds across all of them: rest became conditional, often enough that the condition stopped needing to be enforced from outside.

## What Is the Guilt Actually Protecting?

Against being the one who let the load drop, and against ever finding out what happens if you were.

It protects against visible failure. In houses run on self-reliance, stopping was the single condition under which a system that depended on you could fail where people could see it. Staying in motion keeps that failure permanently hypothetical, which is a real service rather than a neurotic habit.

It protects against the verdict. Where the environment ran on evaluation, visible output pre-empts the critic. Feedback arrives about the work rather than about you, and there is always something to point at. Rest removes the thing to point at, so the surface available for a bad verdict widens the moment you sit down.

It protects your worth from evaporating. Where self-worth got priced by productivity and caregiving rather than given as a baseline, a low-output stretch reads as a genuine drop in what you are worth. Rest carries an actual cost in self-regard in that arrangement, which is why nobody gives it up because an article told them to. The cost is real inside the system that is running.

And it keeps the caregiving current. If affection arrived attached to usefulness, unbroken usefulness is the closest available thing to a guarantee that the affection keeps arriving.

Seen that way, the guilt is doing something protective and doing it with real efficiency. That is the whole reason arguing with it logically has never worked for anybody, including at eleven at night when you have tried it on yourself.

## Why Does the Guilt Also Cover Enjoyment, Money, and Being Helped?

Because the rule is about anything unearned, and idle time is simply its most obvious form.

This surprises people who have only ever thought of it as a rest problem. The same charge fires when money gets spent on something with no use attached, when a friend takes a task off your plate, when a promotion arrives, when an afternoon goes on something enjoyable that produces nothing. Rest is where it shows up most often, and it extends a good deal further than that.

| Where it shows up | What it looks like from outside | What the guilt is enforcing |
|---|---|---|
| An unscheduled afternoon | The free hour gets filled with something that did not need doing today | Time has to be paid for with something produced |
| A holiday | An itinerary solid through day four, restlessness on the one open evening | Unmonitored time is unsafe time |
| Money spent on pleasure | A purchase justified by usefulness, or deferred for another year | Enjoyment has to be earned before it is permitted |
| Help offered to you | A reflexive no, delivered before you have actually considered it | Receiving without contributing first breaks the arrangement |
| Help you give | Jumping into a fixing role nobody asked for, at your own expense | Usefulness is the route to belonging |
| A task handed to somebody else | A private audit of everything that might go wrong in your absence | Letting the load out of your hands is when it falls |
| Praise and milestones | A promotion shrugged off as an expected duty rather than let in | Nothing counts as evidence, so nothing gets banked |
| Illness | Symptoms deferred through the busy stretch, arriving all at once when it ends | Being unwell is permitted only once nothing depends on you |

The two help rows are the ones people miss in themselves for longest, and they work together. Help offered gets refused on reflex, faster than any decision gets made. Help given runs compulsively and automatically, arriving before anybody asked for it. Between those two habits there is no space anywhere in a week in which rest could be received rather than earned, which is a large part of why this pattern is so stable. [Carrying what belongs to other people is its own source and has its own page.](/patterns/feeling-responsible-for-everyone/)

The praise row deserves a line of its own. A milestone that gets filed as an expected duty never converts into evidence of anything, which means the ledger never moves. That is why a decade of achievement can leave someone exactly as unable to sit down as they were at the start of it.

## Who Else Benefits When You Cannot Stop?

More people than anybody carrying this expects, and mostly without anyone deciding to.

Guilt is a lever, and it can be pulled from outside. Being made to feel responsible for outcomes that were never yours, or reminded of an old mistake in a way that implies an outstanding debt, keeps a person working, giving, or complying without any direct pressure being applied. The mechanism is efficient precisely because the enforcement is already installed. It only has to be pointed at. Anyone who has spent a career unable to say no to an extra request has usually met at least one person who noticed that about them and used it.

Most of the time nobody is doing this deliberately. A workplace that quietly runs on one person's inability to stop is an arrangement everybody stopped noticing years ago, and it holds because the person inside it keeps volunteering. A family that has one member who handles things has no incentive to discover whether that member is tired. Households and teams both settle into whatever shape keeps working, and yours has been working.

The deliberate version is worth being able to recognize, because the response differs. If the guilt reliably switches on around one specific person, if warmth from them tracks how much you have done lately, if requests arrive wrapped in a reminder of something you owe, that is a present-day dynamic rather than history. [The specific moves involved are covered separately.](/patterns/emotional-manipulation/)

The practical implication is worth expecting in advance. Some of the resistance to your rest will not come from inside you. Somebody's convenience is genuinely built on your availability, and when you start taking afternoons, a certain amount of quiet pushback tends to follow. Knowing that ahead of time keeps you from reading it as evidence that the guilt was right.

## Why Does It Not Help When Everyone Tells You That You Deserve a Break?

Because permission from outside does not reach the part of the system enforcing the rule.

That part was installed by an environment, over years of daily repetition, rather than by an argument. It responds to accumulated proof and it has no mechanism for processing reassurance. This is why a partner saying you have earned it produces about nine minutes of relief, and why reading a compelling piece about the value of rest changes almost nothing by Thursday.

There is a more uncomfortable version of the same point. Most people carrying this already know rest is important. They know it better than the people reassuring them, can explain the physiology of recovery, and often give excellent advice about it to friends. The knowledge was never the missing piece, which is why more of it does so little. [Why understanding a pattern changes so little on its own is worth reading separately.](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/)

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. What took me longest to accept about this one is that the argument had already been won. I could have written a better defense of rest than anyone who tried to give me permission, and I still worked through the afternoon I had deliberately cleared for nothing. Where it sits today is worth saying plainly. An unearned afternoon still comes with a low hum underneath it, and these days I take it anyway, most of the time.

## Why Did the Break You Actually Took Not Feel Like a Break?

Because the pace came with you into it, so the time off ran at working speed and never converted into anything restorative.

The first line of the identify box above is this section in miniature. You clear a Sunday afternoon on purpose, and by the second hour you are answering an email that could have waited until Monday. Stretch that same afternoon across five days and you get something worth naming on its own: a break correctly identified, deliberately taken, protected from everybody else, and still spent at full speed.

People describe it in nearly the same sentence every time. "I probably needed a timeout, but I've been pretty busy in that so-called timeout. This was supposedly my taking it easy, and it hasn't actually felt like it." Notice how much of that was already settled before the week began. The need was real, the permission was there, the days were cleared. What carried on regardless was the machinery that fills a working day, the sorting and the planning and the catching up and the garage that finally got cleared out, because that machinery has no idle setting and nobody ever asked it to grow one.

What shows up in the middle of a week like that reads differently from the guilt the rest of this page describes. Guilt arrives when you stop. This arrives when you almost did, and it sounds more like a scoreboard somebody left running: "It was always this mental battle of what's going to win out that day, and always a feeling that I never managed to get everything done." The second half of that sentence is worth sitting with, because of where it was said. It was said about days on which nothing had to be done at all. The ledger followed the person into the holiday and went on scoring a week that had no entries to score.

The shortest report in this group is also the strangest. "I'm almost feeling like I'm not doing enough." That is somebody on a break they arranged themselves, measuring the day against a quota nobody set, in the one stretch of the year where the quota was formally suspended. Tiredness gets tracked no better here than it does in a working month. What gets tracked is throughput, and throughput on a holiday will always come back low.

There is a version of the same week that does convert, and it is worth being able to tell them apart. "I was actually feeling guilty that I should have been doing some work, but it was nice just to relax." The guilt showed up there too, and the rest happened anyway. That is the ordinary version this page has been describing from the top, and it is the one the practice further down actually loosens. The version in this section never reaches that point. Nothing gets enjoyed, nothing gets recovered, and the guilt does not even get a clear shot, because the work never stopped long enough for it to fire at.

Three other pages each hold a piece of this, and knowing where they end saves you reading the wrong one. [If your version is a whole weekend spent horizontal that still leaves you flat, the drain underneath that is covered separately.](/patterns/emotional-exhaustion/) [If the reflex is that any empty hour gets filled before you have decided anything, that is the busyness page rather than this one.](/patterns/why-am-i-always-busy/) [And if the break in question runs to months rather than days, a sabbatical taken at the same speed as the job it interrupted, that longer version has its own page.](/insights/sabbatical-career-break/) None of the three covers the small, deliberate, hard-won timeout that you took correctly and then ran like an ordinary Tuesday, which is what this section is for.

One practical thing follows from all of it. Booking more days rarely repairs a week like this, though almost everybody tries that first, stretching one week into two and reporting the identical result at the end of it. The variable is the speed inside the days rather than the number of them, which is why what changes this sits closer to what a quiet hour costs you than to how much time you can get signed off.

## Why Do You Get Sick the Moment You Finally Slow Down?

Because a body running on vigilance holds a great deal at bay until the demand passes, and then it stops holding.

The pattern is familiar to almost everyone who recognizes this page. Symptoms get dismissed or deferred through the busy stretch, and then arrive together on the first day of a holiday, or on the Saturday after a project ships, or in the week between two jobs. Nothing mysterious is happening. A system that has been suppressing whatever would interrupt the work stops suppressing it once the work ends.

The problem is what this teaches. Stopping and getting ill keep landing next to each other, close enough in time that the association forms without any conscious help. Rest starts to look like the thing that caused the crash, which makes the next rest harder to take, which lengthens the stretch before it, which makes the eventual crash bigger. The loop is self-reinforcing and almost nobody notices it running.

Two things are worth saying plainly. The first is that the ordering is the reverse of how it feels. The illness belongs to the months that preceded the rest rather than to the rest itself. The second is that persistent symptoms, or exhaustion that a full weekend no longer dents, deserve a doctor rather than a better calendar. [The physical tail of this is covered in more depth alongside the exhaustion that sleep does not repair.](/patterns/emotional-exhaustion/)

## What Does Comfort in Rest Actually Look Like?

Less like leisure and more like an absence of static.

Most people imagine the far end of this as a slower life: a smaller job, fewer commitments, a personality that enjoys hammocks. Somebody at the comfortable end can still work hard, still ship things, still have a full calendar during a full month. What has changed is what a quiet hour costs them. Here is that translated out of abstraction and into an ordinary Tuesday.

| The guilty end | The at-ease end | What it looks like on an ordinary Tuesday |
|---|---|---|
| Rest has to be earned with output first | Rest is maintenance owed on a rhythm | You sit down at four with things still on the list, and no case gets made for it |
| A weekend with nothing in it reads as dangerous | A weekend with nothing in it reads as spacious | Saturday arrives empty and stays empty, and none of that registers as significant |
| Stopping needs an excuse: illness, undeniable exhaustion, a calendar nobody could object to | Stopping needs nothing | You say you are taking the afternoon, with no reason attached to the sentence |
| Delegating starts a private audit | Delegating ends the matter | You hand it over and do not check it that evening |
| A nap runs a mental ledger underneath it | A nap is a nap | You wake up without adding it to anything |
| Leisure produces guilt | Leisure produces enjoyment | Two hours go on something with no result, and nobody hears about it |
| Enjoyment needs a productive justification | Enjoyment is its own justification | You buy the thing because you want it, and the sentence stops there |
| Help offered gets refused on reflex | Help offered gets accepted | Somebody takes a task off you, and you say thank you and add nothing |
| Spontaneity costs too much control | Spontaneity is available | A plan changes at short notice and the day survives it |
| A slow week costs you something in self-regard | A slow week costs nothing | A slow Tuesday and a busy one carry the same sense of your own worth |

Read the right-hand column carefully, because it is deliberately unimpressive. Nobody in it has downshifted, quit, moved to the countryside, or had a realization. There are no long weekends in a cabin and no scene where something clicks. The clearest marker is the last row, and it is worth reading twice: a slow Tuesday and a busy one carrying roughly the same sense of personal worth, because worth stopped being priced by output somewhere along the way.

Notice also what is missing from that column. Bliss is missing. Most days at the comfortable end are milder than anybody hopes, and the change registers as an absence rather than as a presence, which is exactly why people who have made this shift often struggle to describe it. Nothing arrives. Something stops.

Don't judge yourself if that column reads as slightly out of reach today. The left-hand column took years of daily repetition to build, and nobody crosses to the other side in an afternoon. What makes it approachable is that every entry on the right is a specific, small, repeatable behavior rather than a change of temperament.

## What Actually Moves This?

Small, repeated, thoroughly boring evidence that stopping does not cause the collapse the system is braced for.

The reason it has to be that, rather than a better understanding of why rest matters, is mechanical. The rule was installed by experience and it updates through experience. Each repetition contradicts something that took years to build, which is why the discomfort does not lift on the first attempt, or the tenth, and why people who understand this pattern perfectly still answer the email on the Sunday they cleared.

1. **Take the unscheduled hour instead of filling it.** When something cancels, leave the gap where it is. The pull to fill it usually arrives inside ninety seconds, ahead of any decision, which is what makes it worth catching.
2. **Leave one thing visibly unfinished overnight.** The dishes, the document, the inbox at eleven instead of zero. Then look at it in the morning and register, deliberately, that nothing happened while you slept.
3. **Let somebody's help stand.** Accept the offer, and then decline to quietly redo it later to your own standard. The redoing is the part that keeps the whole arrangement intact.
4. **Reverse the sequence once a week.** Rest first, then work, on a day when nothing has been produced yet. Almost everything in this pattern depends on the order, and breaking it on purpose produces more information than a month of thinking about it.
5. **Announce it in one plain sentence.** "I'm taking the afternoon." No reason, no justification, no illness invented to cover it. The sentence is the exercise, and it is harder than it looks.
6. **Spend on something purely enjoyable and refuse to justify it.** Money or time. Watch for the reflex to attach a use to it, calling it research or an investment in your health, and let the reflex pass unobeyed.
7. **Let the guilt arrive and do nothing about it.** Take the rest, feel the charge, and decline to act on it. This is the whole practice compressed into one instruction, and everything else on the list is a way of setting it up.
8. **Keep a one-line record of what actually fell apart.** One line per rest taken, for a month. The list stays close to empty, and an empty list is evidence in a way that reassurance from another person can never be.

Is any of this comfortable? No. It is small, repetitive, and it takes real, freaking stubbornness to leave the dishes a ninth time when the eighth taught you nothing you could point at. The honest description is months of unremarkable repetitions rather than one afternoon of realizing the guilt is inaccurate to your present life.

Two caveats matter more than any item on that list. The first is that some seasons genuinely are full. A newborn, a first year of a business, a family member who needs care. You cannot practice unscheduled hours in a month that contains none, and where that is the case the honest first move is arranging one hour rather than correcting a belief. The second is that unhooking worth from output happens underneath the calendar, and this page does not finish that job on its own. [The valuation itself sits here](/patterns/conditional-self-worth/), and [whether putting yourself first is selfish tends to be the actual blocker rather than a lack of technique](/insights/is-it-selfish-to-put-yourself-first/).

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Months of small repetitions, and the feeling is the last part to move.

That order surprises people, so it is worth stating directly. What usually shifts first is the behavior of taking rest anyway, guilt and all. The felt sense of deserving it catches up afterwards, sometimes long afterwards, and almost nobody can name the week it changed. People generally notice it in retrospect, on some ordinary Sunday when they realize an afternoon went by without an argument happening in their head.

Expect regression, and expect it specifically under load. A stretch of high stress pulls the old rule straight back online: money pressure, a new job, a quarter where somebody is watching your output closely, a family crisis. That is normal relapse rather than proof the change did not take. The rule was built for exactly those conditions, so those are the conditions in which it reappears first.

Does it disappear entirely? Most people describe it thinning rather than vanishing. The charge gets smaller. The gap between feeling it and obeying it gets longer. The Sunday afternoon stops needing to be defended, then stops needing to be planned, and then one passes without you noticing that it did. That last part, noticing afterwards rather than during, is what the change looks like from the inside, and it is considerably less dramatic than anybody hopes.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Reschedule Anything?

Run a collapse log, and run it four times. Take one hour this week that you would normally fill, and before you sit down, write one line predicting what will go wrong if you spend it doing nothing. Be specific: the email that will go unanswered, the person who will be let down, the thing that will slip. Then take the hour. At the end of it, write a second line recording what actually happened. Do that four times over a fortnight, then read the eight lines together in one sitting. Almost everyone finds the prediction column full and the outcome column close to empty, and seeing those two columns next to each other in your own handwriting does more than another year of being told you deserve a break. It costs four hours you were going to feel guilty about anyway, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever go anywhere or buy anything.
