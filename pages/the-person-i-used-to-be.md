The current self is being lived from inside, without an outline, without an ending, and with all the contradictions still visible. You are comparing an edited account to raw material and concluding that the raw material is worse."

# Why am I not the person I used to be?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

You changed, which is what a decade does, and you can still describe the earlier version clearly enough to keep measuring against it. The measuring is doing most of the damage. That earlier version is a remembered draft rather than a record, quietly edited to stay coherent. The workable question is narrower: which one specific thing do you want back?
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
Sentences people run privately for years before ever saying one of them out loud.

- "I could describe who I was at thirty better than I can describe myself now."
- "Somebody told me I had changed and I could not work out whether to say thank you."
- "I liked that person. I do not dislike this one. I just cannot get back."
- "I keep waiting to feel like myself again and I am not sure which self I mean."

</div>

There is a video of you somewhere from a few years ago. Not a photograph, a video, where the person moves and talks and interrupts somebody and laughs about a second too early.

You watch it twice.

The first time you are watching the event. The second time you are watching the person, and specifically you are checking one thing, which is whether they seem more alive than you. Whatever answer you arrive at, you did not arrive at it by weighing evidence. You arrived at it in about the time it takes to read a face.

That is the whole pattern in one gesture, and almost nobody who does it thinks of it as a behavior. It has a hundred other forms. A reference letter somebody wrote about you in 2016 that you find while looking for something else, in which four adjectives appear and none of them describe you now. A bicycle in the attic belonging to a person who rode it. A box of things kept by somebody whose reasons for keeping them are no longer available to you. An old message thread you scroll for two minutes with a strange feeling of reading somebody else's correspondence, which stops being strange when you notice that the somebody else signed off with your name.

And the version that arrives without warning, which is a friend of seventeen years saying, kindly and with no edge in it at all, that you have gone quiet.

You know they are right. That is the difficult part. There is nothing to argue with and no case to make, and the only sentence available in reply is a question you have been carrying around unopened, which is whether going quiet was a loss or a promotion. You cannot tell. Nobody can tell you. And the person who could have judged it, the one in the video, has been gone long enough that you would not trust their opinion anyway.

This page is written for that reader specifically. If your experience is that the question returns nothing at all, that you cannot say what you like or what you want and no earlier version comes to mind either, [that is a different pattern with a different first move and it has its own page](/patterns/i-dont-know-who-i-am-anymore/). The reader here has the opposite problem. You have too much. The earlier version arrives instantly, in detail, with opinions and a laugh, and refuses to be reconciled with the person reading this.

One thing before anything else. If this sits inside a flatness that has not lifted for months, if nothing in your week registers as pleasurable anywhere, or if there is any thought of harming yourself, however briefly it passes through, that belongs with a licensed professional rather than a page. There is a fuller version of that boundary two sections down and it matters more than everything written after it.

## What Does This Actually Look Like Across an Ordinary Month?

Smaller than the video and much more frequent, which is why it never gets counted as one thing.

You start telling a story that used to be yours, and you stop halfway through, because you can hear that it belongs to somebody else now and you are doing an impression. Nobody at the table notices the pause. You finish it anyway and the whole thing tastes wrong.

You catch yourself using a phrase in a meeting and you know exactly which year you started saying it, and it was not a year you would have predicted.

You meet somebody new and give them the current account of yourself, and it takes ninety words and covers the work, the location and one hobby, and you notice afterward that the account you would have given at thirty was longer, funnier and contained several opinions you would now decline to defend.

You see a plan on a calendar for something you agreed to weeks ago and your first reaction is a small dread, and you remember with total clarity being a person for whom that would have been the good part of the week.

You do something well at work, and the thought that arrives is that the earlier version would have found this boring, and you cannot tell whether that means you have settled or grown up.

You look at a group photograph and you can name the year from your own face rather than from anyone else's, because your face is the thing you have been tracking.

None of these is a crisis. Every one of them is a comparison, and there are perhaps forty of them a month, and together they build a running verdict that nobody ever sat down and delivered.

## What Is the Name for This, and How Far Does the Name Actually Reach?

The phrase almost everybody arrives with is **identity crisis**, and it is worth having on the condition that you also know exactly where it stops.

The term comes from mid-century developmental writing. Erikson used it for the task of adolescence, in which a person works out a workable sense of who they are against the alternative of staying scattered across roles. Two things about that origin get dropped in almost every popular retelling and both of them matter to you.

The first is that it was never a diagnosis and was never proposed as one. What the writing named was a normal developmental task, something expected to happen to everybody, with a range of outcomes rather than a pass and a fail. The word crisis in that writing meant a turning point where something had to be settled, closer to the sense a doctor means by a crisis in a fever than the sense a newspaper means. Everything alarming about the phrase in ordinary use has been added since.

The second is that it was built on adolescents. Extending it to a forty-year-old who cannot recognize themselves in a video from 2019 is an extension rather than a finding. It may well be a good extension. It has not been established as one, and anybody who tells you the adult version is a documented condition with a course and a prognosis is telling you something the underlying work does not support.

**What the term genuinely captures.** It captures that the experience is a period rather than a permanent state, which is the single most useful thing about it. It captures that the difficulty is about self-definition rather than about mood. And it captures that something has to get settled for the period to end, which is accurate and is why waiting it out works less well than people hope.

**What the term does not tell you.** It names a *what* and says nothing at all about a *why*. Two people can arrive at the identical experience, described in the identical words, for reasons that have nothing in common. One has quietly lost a capability they valued. One has ended a role that had been supplying the whole self. One is comparing themselves against a memory that has been improved in the retelling for eleven years. No label separates those three, and the separation is the only part that changes what you do on Monday.

There is also no accepted measure of it in adults. No number of missing adjectives makes you a case. There is no threshold, no cutoff, and no scale a clinician would use, which means that any confident statement about whether you are having one is a description of your week wearing the costume of an assessment.

## When Does This Belong With Somebody Qualified Rather Than a Page Like This?

Four situations, and none of them describes the ordinary version everything below is written for.

If the flatness has run for months. Not a bad fortnight and not a heavy season, but a long stretch in which nothing anywhere registers as pleasurable, energy does not return after rest, and hopelessness sits there without lifting. That needs assessing by somebody licensed rather than reframing by anybody, and asking in month one costs a great deal less than asking in month twelve. The same applies to any thought of harming yourself, however briefly it passes through, and everything else here can wait indefinitely.

If the change followed violence, abuse, or a betrayal you have never fully described to anybody. If memories arrive uninvited, if there are stretches of time you cannot account for, or if somebody told you repeatedly who you were until you believed them, bring in people trained for that. A page cannot do it and should not try.

If the change arrived rather than accumulated. Everything here assumes a gradual drift you noticed afterward. A sharp, dated change in how you think or feel, especially one other people noticed before you did, deserves a proper medical opinion rather than a psychological reading, and that includes anything running alongside a physical illness or a treatment you are already under.

And if saying what you actually want at home carries a real cost, if money or movement is controlled, or if stating a preference produces something well past awkwardness, then the situation is about safety rather than identity, and it belongs with people who can help including people who can help you leave.

For everybody else, and that is almost everybody reading this, the rest applies.

## Is Being Different From Who You Were Actually a Problem?

For a large number of readers, no, and burying that answer at the bottom of the page would be a way of avoiding giving it.

So here it is first, and at length, because most people arriving at this question have been handed the word crisis by somebody and have not been told the base rate.

When large samples of adults are asked directly how much their personality, their core values and their preferences changed over the previous ten years, the reported amount is substantial. Not at one particular age. At every age that has been asked. A twenty-five-year-old reports having changed a great deal since fifteen. A fifty-year-old reports having changed a great deal since forty. The size of the reported change declines somewhat with age and it never approaches zero.

Set that beside your own situation, because it reframes the whole thing. You are describing an outcome that the majority of people report. The unusual part of your week is the looking rather than the change itself.

Most people do not look, and the reason is entirely practical. While something is being built, a career, a house, a family, a body of work, the current version of you is fully occupied and never gets compared to anything, because the comparison would not be useful and there is no time for it. The moment the building slows down, even briefly, attention becomes available. That is when the video gets watched twice. This is why the comparison so reliably shows up in a quiet stretch rather than a hard one, and why people find the timing insulting: it arrives when things are objectively fine.

There is a further thing worth saying plainly to the reader who has just been told they have changed. Almost every ordinary life event produces exactly this. Working somewhere for six years changes what you find funny. Living with somebody changes your vocabulary. Being responsible for a child changes what you consider risky. A body at forty-two runs on different settings from a body at twenty-eight, and it will do it again at fifty-five. An illness changes what you spend energy on and does not always give it back. Sustained tiredness, over years, removes the surplus that the loudest parts of a personality run on, and the surplus is the first thing to go and the last thing to return.

None of that is decline. All of it registers as decline if you are measuring against a version who had more surplus and fewer obligations, because the thing you are actually measuring is available energy and you have coded it as character.

So the honest position. The change itself needs no explanation and no repair. What sometimes needs attention is one specific thing inside the change that you actually want back, and that is a much smaller and far more answerable question. Everything below is aimed at finding it.

## Why Can You Be Completely Certain and Completely Lost at the Same Time?

Because certainty and searching are two separate measurements rather than two ends of a single line, and running high on both at once is a recognized combination.

This is the piece of vocabulary I would most like you to walk away with, because it dissolves a contradiction people spend years treating as a personal defect.

The research tradition that grew out of the developmental writing above separated two things. **Commitment** is what you have settled on: the work, the person, the city, the stated values, the things you would defend if challenged. **Exploration** is the activity of examining alternatives to any of it. Marcia built the framework in the 1960s by measuring the two independently and sorting people by which combination they showed, and the combinations are the useful part rather than the sorting.

A person with high commitment and low exploration has settled and stopped looking. A person with low commitment and high exploration is in open search. And a person with high commitment and high exploration is fully committed and actively examining, at the same time, without contradiction.

That last combination is the one you are in, and it explains an experience that is otherwise incoherent. You would not leave your marriage. You are not looking for another job. You have no intention of moving. And you feel, most evenings, like somebody with no idea what they are doing. Those facts do not conflict. They are two separate readings on two separate dials, and being told to pick one is bad advice built on the assumption that they are the same dial.

The honest limit on this. The measurement is self-report, taken mostly from students and young adults, and the four-way sorting that came out of it has been argued over for decades, chiefly on the grounds that a typology built from an interview does not travel well into questionnaires. Treat the two dimensions as a useful separation rather than as a validated reading on a scale. The separation is the part that has held up.

## What Is Reconsideration, and Why Does It Feel Like Failure?

Because the activity of comparing your commitments to alternatives got named, measured and found to be ordinary, and nobody told you.

Later work in the same tradition, principally by Crocetti and Meeus, split exploration further and added a third measurement they called **reconsideration of commitment**: the extent to which a person is currently comparing what they have committed to against alternatives, and weighing whether to change.

Two findings from that work are worth having.

The first is that reconsideration rises during transitions and falls afterward. It behaves like a state as much as a trait, and it goes up when circumstances shift, which is precisely when you are most likely to conclude that something is wrong with you.

The second is that it correlates with both openness and unease at the same time. High reconsideration goes with being more flexible and more uncomfortable. That combination is what you are feeling. It has a name, it shows up on the measures, and it does not predict that your commitments are wrong.

Which brings the practical point. Most people running high reconsideration change nothing. The comparing is an activity, and its most common outcome is a stronger attachment to what was already there, arrived at deliberately this time. The mistake is treating the presence of the comparison as its own verdict, as though the fact that you are weighing something proves the answer should be no.

The honest limit again, and it is the same one. These are self-report measures, mostly validated on adolescents and people in their early twenties, and the wording of the questions carries some of the result. What the work establishes is that the activity exists and can be measured separately from commitment itself. It establishes nothing about what a given person should do with it.

## Why Does the Change You Have Already Made Look Bigger Than the Change Still Coming?

Because remembering is easier than imagining, and the gap between the two is the spine of this whole page.

Quoidbach and colleagues ran the study that shows it most clearly. They surveyed roughly nineteen thousand adults across a wide age range and asked two questions. How much have your personality, values and preferences changed over the last ten years? And how much do you expect them to change over the next ten?

At every age, the reported past change was substantially larger than the predicted future change. Twenty-year-olds knew they had changed enormously since ten and expected to change modestly by thirty. Forty-year-olds knew they had changed enormously since thirty and expected to change modestly by fifty. The pattern held all the way up the age range, which means it cannot be true of everybody, because the fifty-year-olds were reporting exactly the change that the forty-year-olds had said would not happen.

The interpretation the authors put on it is that people tend to believe they have just now finished becoming themselves. Whatever version you are at any given moment feels like the settled one, and every previous version is visible as a stage.

Sit with what that does to your comparison, because it changes the object rather than the feeling.

The person in the video, the one you are measuring against, was also convinced they were finished. They were not looking forward at you. They were looking backward at somebody else, running exactly this comparison, and probably losing it. The reference point you are using was never a fixed point, only one frame of a process, and it felt permanent from inside for the same reason today feels permanent from inside.

And there is a forward consequence people find genuinely useful. The version of you reading this, the one you are quietly grading as the diminished one, is a waypoint. In ten years there will be a person who can describe you the way you are currently describing the video, and who will be equally convinced that they are the final draft. Whatever you conclude about the comparison, you are not concluding it about a destination.

## How Good Is the Evidence for That, Honestly?

Better than most things quoted in this territory, and weaker than the way it usually gets repeated.

The design is cross-sectional. It asks different people at different ages on a single occasion rather than following one person forward for a decade, which means it cannot show that anybody actually underestimated anything. It shows a mismatch between two kinds of report.

Those two reports are also not the same kind of thing, and this is the real limitation. A past-change report depends on memory of an earlier self. A future-change prediction depends on imagining one. Those two mental operations have different error profiles, and the study cannot separate underestimating the future from overestimating the past. It is entirely possible that some of the gap is people exaggerating how much they have already changed rather than failing to see what is coming.

Notice that the second possibility is at least as interesting for you and arrives at the same conclusion by a different route. If part of the effect is over-reported past change, then the earlier version you are grieving is even less of a fixed object than the first reading suggested.

The finding has also been repeated in popular writing far more often than it has been examined, which is a reason to hold it as a strong and well-replicated pattern of self-report rather than as a law about how people develop.

## Is the Person You Are Measuring Against a Record or a Draft?

A draft, and it has been revised more times than you have counted.

This is the root nobody names, and in my experience it is the one that actually moves things for people who arrive here with a very clear picture of who they used to be.

Autobiographical memory does not work like a stored file being opened. It gets rebuilt at the moment of retrieval, out of general knowledge about your life, specific fragments, and whatever frame you currently need. Conway and Pleydell-Pearce set out the account most people in this area work from, in which the rebuild is shaped by the goals and the self-concept of the person doing the remembering right now.

The consequence for you is specific and slightly uncomfortable. The earlier version of yourself is reconstructed every time you consult it, and the reconstruction is done by the current you, who has a complaint. If your current complaint is that you have gone quiet, the version that gets built will be louder than the person actually was. If your complaint is tiredness, the version will have more energy. The old self drifts toward being exactly the thing you feel short of, which is what makes the comparison feel so precise and so devastating, and which is also why it never resolves.

There is a second mechanism sitting on top of that one, and it is simpler. The earlier version has been finished. It has an ending, a shape, and a set of adjectives that survived being told out loud a few dozen times, because the details that did not fit the shape got dropped somewhere around the fifth retelling. The current version has none of that. It is being lived from inside, with every contradiction visible, no ending available, and no editing done.

So the comparison is between an edited account and raw footage, and raw footage loses that comparison every time regardless of content.

None of this means the change is imaginary. You did change. It means the *size* of the change, and especially its direction, is being measured against something that has been quietly moving in order to make the measurement worse. [If the general question of how far to trust your own memory is what interests you, that has a page of its own](/patterns/not-trusting-my-own-memory/), and [the way a single early scene gets kept in circulation for decades has another](/patterns/what-does-my-earliest-memory-mean/).

The honest limit. This is a well-supported general account of how remembering works, and it licenses no claim at all about any particular memory of yours. It predicts a direction of drift across many retellings. It cannot tell you that the person in your head was quieter than you think, and anybody who says otherwise is overreading it.

## Why Do Two People Run the Same Comparison for Completely Different Reasons?

Because the comparison is a common outcome of at least five very different situations, and they need different things.

Two people can describe this in almost identical words. The same sentences, the same detail about the old version, the same inability to say whether the change was good. Underneath, one of them is describing ordinary aging and needs to be told the base rate. One is describing a lost context. One is grieving a specific capability and has never said so out loud. Looking the same from outside says nothing about coming from the same place.

The five below are a sorting device rather than a taxonomy. Most people find that two of them apply and one of them is the answer.

## What If Nothing Happened and You Simply Got Older?

Then you are the largest group on this page, and the useful move is to stop treating an ordinary fact as a symptom.

This root has no event in it. Nothing ended, nothing broke, nobody left. A decade passed, and a decade does what it does. Your energy budget changed. Your obligations multiplied and became less optional. The people you spend time with changed and took some of your vocabulary with them. The things that used to be effortless now cost something, and the things that used to cost something have become effortless, and only the first category gets counted.

The reason this root is so hard to see is that the change is distributed. There is no moment to point at, so the mind supplies one, usually the wrong one, and the search for a cause turns a background process into a mystery that needs solving.

What tells you this is yours: the earlier version is separated from you by years rather than by an event, the changes are broad rather than specific, and when you try to name what you actually lost you produce adjectives instead of nouns. Quieter. Less spontaneous. More careful. Adjectives are the signature of this root, because a distributed change does not leave nameable objects behind.

What helps is narrow and slightly deflating. Compare like with like. You are measuring a thirty-year-old with free evenings against a forty-two-year-old with a mortgage and a person who depends on them, and calling the difference a personality change. Some of it is. Most of it is the arithmetic of a different week. Take the ten items on your list of what you have lost and mark which ones would come back tomorrow if you had four uninterrupted days and slept properly. It is usually more than half, and knowing which half is worth the exercise.

## What If What Ended Was a Context Rather Than a Person?

Then what you are missing lives outside you, and looking for it inside is why the search keeps returning nothing.

Some versions of a person only exist in a setting. The one who was funny in a particular group, sharp in a particular job, patient in a particular house. The traits were real and they were also jointly produced, requiring other people, a room and a set of conditions to exist at all. Remove the setting and the trait goes with it, and the person left standing concludes, wrongly, that something inside them died.

This is the root where the search feels most futile, because you keep excavating a self that was never stored there. The thing was distributed across a room.

The test is simple and quite fast. Take the trait you miss most and ask when you last had a setting that called for it. If the answer is that no such setting has existed for six years, you have your explanation, and it is a considerably better one than anything about your character.

Where the ending was large, where a role or a relationship had been carrying the whole thing rather than one trait, [that has its own page and it is a fuller account than this section can give](/patterns/who-am-i-without-the-thing-that-defined-me/). And where the setting is still present but only reappears when you go back to it, so the old version switches on the moment you walk into a childhood kitchen, [that specific effect is covered separately](/patterns/why-does-my-change-collapse-around-my-usual-people/).

## What If the Old Version Is an Edited Draft Rather Than a Record?

Then the comparison has been rigged for years, and finding that out is worth more than winning it.

The mechanism is the one described above, so what belongs here is what it looks like in a life rather than how it works.

It looks like a version of yourself who is suspiciously well suited to your current complaint. It looks like an old self who is always the antidote: louder when you feel quiet, freer when you feel trapped, warmer when you feel closed. It looks like a story told so many times that the contradictions have been sanded off, so the earlier version has no bad weeks in it, no boredom, and no evenings of exactly the flatness you now think is new.

The strongest test I know is to find external evidence and read it against your account. Old messages you sent. Something you wrote at the time. Somebody who knew you then and has no stake in flattering either version.

What people find is remarkably consistent. The earlier version was more anxious than they remember, less certain, and complaining about several of the same things. Often they find that the exact sentence they are now saying about themselves was already being said, about an even earlier version, by the person they now miss.

That discovery does not fix anything on its own, and it does something better. It removes the ground from a comparison that was never going to be won, and it frees you to ask the only question here with a workable answer, which is the next section.

## What If You Are Grieving One Specific Thing You Could Do?

Then you have the most solvable version of this and it has been hiding inside a much larger question.

Sometimes the general feeling is a wrapper around something entirely specific. A physical capability that went and has not come back. A way of speaking to strangers that you could once do without preparation. An appetite for going out that disappeared and has never been mourned because it did not seem like the kind of thing you are allowed to mourn.

Losses of this kind rarely get named, for a reason worth stating. They feel too small to grieve. Nobody holds anything for the ability to stay up until two and enjoy it, or for a knee, or for the version of you who could walk into a room of strangers and be fine within four minutes. So the loss goes unnamed, and unnamed losses do not stay put. They spread out and reappear as a general statement about who you have become.

The tell is that finishing the sentence produces a noun rather than an adjective. Not quieter, not less spontaneous, but *the running*, *the way I was with new people*, *the thing I did on Saturdays for eleven years*.

If a noun arrives, treat it as a loss rather than as an identity question. Losses respond to being named, said out loud to somebody, and mourned in proportion. Several of them also respond to being tested, because a surprising number of capabilities are not gone but unused, and the difference between the two is discoverable in about a week. [If nothing about this has ever been grieved properly, the general mechanism for that is worth reading](/patterns/unprocessed-grief/).

## What If You Changed to Fit Somebody and Cannot Find the Join?

Then the thing you are looking for is a seam rather than a self, and the seam is usually findable.

This is the root people are most reluctant to look at directly, because looking at it seems to require a verdict about somebody they love.

It works quietly. Two people adjust to each other, which is what living together is, and one of them does most of the adjusting, and neither notices because each individual adjustment was small and reasonable and made freely. Four years later there is a set of tastes, opinions, friends and Saturdays that arrived from one direction. There was no moment of surrender. There is no scene you could point to. There is just a person you can no longer locate underneath a set of arrangements that all made sense at the time.

The test is directional rather than moral, and it is worth running before you conclude anything. Take five changes of the last few years and ask, for each, in whose direction it went. If the answer is mixed, you are looking at an ordinary mutual adjustment and there is nothing here. If all five went one way, that pattern is information, and it is information about the arrangement rather than about anybody's character.

What this page will not do is turn into advice about handling another person, because the pattern here is yours and it will travel with you into any relationship you enter next. [The broader business of becoming whoever you are with is covered elsewhere and is worth reading if this section landed](/patterns/becoming-who-im-with/).

## Which of the Five Is Yours, and How Would You Know?

By what comes out of your mouth when you finish one specific sentence, which sorts them faster than any amount of reflection.

| If what you miss comes out as | The likely root | The move that fits it |
|---|---|---|
| Adjectives, spread over years, with no event attached | Ordinary change over time | Compare like with like, and mark which items return with rest and time |
| A trait that only ever appeared in one room, job or group | A context ended | Look for the missing setting rather than the missing trait |
| A version who is always the exact antidote to your current complaint | An edited draft | Find external evidence from the time and read it against your account |
| A noun. A capability, a body, a specific way with people | A specific loss | Name it as a loss, say it out loud once, then test whether it is gone or unused |
| Five changes that all went in one direction | Adaptation to somebody | Look for the seam, and date the changes rather than judging them |

Sit with that table using the last three months rather than the worst evening. Most people arriving here have run the argument in their own head several hundred times without once separating those two questions.

Two of those rows will probably feel true. That is normal, and the one that matters is the one that produces a concrete next action rather than a stronger description.

## Why Does Being Told You Have Changed Land So Badly?

Because it arrives sounding like a verdict, it usually is not one, and you have no way to check which.

The sentence is almost always well meant. Somebody has noticed a real difference and has no neutral vocabulary available, so what comes out is a comparison with no direction attached to it. You have gone quiet. You used to be the one who organized everything. You seem more serious than you were.

Every one of those may be accurate and none of them contains a judgment, and all of them land as one, because you have been running the same comparison privately and losing it, and an outside voice saying the same words feels like confirmation from a second source.

It is not a second source. What it is instead is a person describing what changed for *them*, which is a different measurement from what changed in you. Somebody who found you funnier, more available or easier to plan with is reporting a real loss from their side of the relationship. That report is genuinely useful information about the relationship. It is weak evidence about which version of you is better, because they are not comparing you against anything except their own experience of you, and their experience of you was always partial.

There is a reply worth having ready, and it is a question rather than a defense. Ask what specifically they noticed. Most people cannot answer immediately, and what they produce after a few seconds is usually one concrete thing rather than a global assessment. That one concrete thing is worth ten of the general sentence, because it can be checked, and occasionally it is something you would want to know.

## Why Can You Not Tell Whether the Change Was Good?

Because the only person qualified to grade it changed along with everything else, and they took the grading criteria with them.

This is the part that keeps the question open for years, and it is a structural problem rather than a failure of insight.

To judge whether the change was an improvement you need a standard. The obvious standard is what the earlier version would have thought. But the earlier version had different values, and it is precisely those values that changed. A person at twenty-eight who valued intensity above almost everything will grade a calmer forty-year-old as a disappointment, accurately by their own standard, which is no longer yours. Run it the other way and the current version grades the earlier one as exhausting, also accurately, also by a standard that did not exist at the time.

Both verdicts are valid within their own frame and neither of them settles anything, and this is why the question can be turned over for a decade without resolving. There is no neutral position from which to compare two sets of values, because occupying one of them is what having values means.

So the honest answer to whether the change was good is that the question does not have an answer of that kind. What it has is a set of smaller questions that do have answers. Is there something you have lost that you want back? Is there something you gained that you would keep? Is there anything you are currently doing only because an earlier version of you decided it?

Those three are answerable this week. The big one is not answerable at all, and continuing to work on it is where most of the time in this pattern gets spent.

## What Does Checking Against the Old Version Cost Across a Year?

More than it looks, because the cost arrives as a low tax on everything rather than as an event.

The first cost is that the present gets graded rather than lived. Every reasonably good evening gets a second layer added to it, in which the evening is compared to an evening from 2017, and the comparison arrives before the evening is over. Nothing is allowed to simply be what it was.

The second is that new things do not get started. Starting something requires being willing to be a beginner, and a person running a continuous comparison against a more capable earlier self finds beginning almost unbearable, because the gap is widest exactly at the start. So the guitar stays where it is, the class does not get booked, and the pattern quietly confirms itself.

The third is that current relationships get compared to older ones and lose. Not the people. The version of you inside them. A friendship of three years is measured against a friendship of twenty, on the grounds that you were more open in the older one, when the actual difference is twenty years of accumulated context that the new one has not had time to build.

The fourth is the one people notice last. The comparison consumes the attention that would otherwise be building the current version into somebody describable. A self becomes describable through accumulated history, and accumulating history requires attention pointed forward. Every hour spent grading the last decade is an hour not spent giving the current decade anything to be remembered for, which is what makes this pattern quietly self-sustaining.

## Why Does "Just Be Yourself Again" Fail Here?

Because it names an operation that cannot be carried out, and the failure gets read as further evidence of decline.

There is no stored version to return to. What exists is a memory of one, which is rebuilt at retrieval and drifts. Asked to be that person again, you have nothing to load. What you have is a description, so what actually happens is one of two things.

Either you perform a set of behaviors the earlier version used to produce, which comes out as an impression and feels worse than doing nothing, because you can hear yourself doing it. Or you freeze, because the description is made of adjectives and adjectives cannot be executed. Nobody can do *spontaneous* on Tuesday at seven.

Both outcomes get filed as proof that the old version is really gone, which is what makes the advice actively harmful rather than merely useless.

The instruction that does work is narrower to the point of seeming trivial. Take one thing the earlier version did and do that thing, once, this week, badly. Not the trait. The action. Not more open, but call the person you would have called. Not more spontaneous, but say yes to the thing on Thursday without checking your energy first.

And expect the first attempt to be awkward, because that is what a first attempt is. If you have not danced in a long time and somebody asks you up, it is going to be a bit wonky, and the wonkiness is a report on the newness rather than on whether you can dance. [Why understanding all of this changes so little on its own has a page of its own](/patterns/why-insight-doesnt-change-anything/), and it is the reason this section ends in an action rather than in an insight.

## Which Part of This Did You Actually Choose?

Almost none of it, and the one part you did choose is the whole of your workable surface.

Worth being precise here, because it decides where effort goes.

You did not choose to change. Change of this size and at this rate is what a decade produces in most people, and the reported base rate is high at every age. You did not choose to notice. Noticing happened because a gap opened in your week and attention became available. You did not choose which version of the earlier self your memory would build, and you did not choose that it would drift toward being the antidote to whatever you currently feel short of.

You did not choose the comparison either. It runs on its own, forty times a month, triggered by video and old messages and other people's sentences.

What is available to you is one thing and it is genuinely available. You can choose which specific item on the list you actually want back, and then find out whether it is gone or merely unused.

That is a much smaller object than the one you arrived with, and it is the only part of this with a handle on it. The general question of whether you are still yourself will not be answered by working on it. It tends to get answered sideways, months later, by somebody who stopped asking it and spent the same energy testing one item.

## What Does the Settled Version Look Like on an Ordinary Friday?

Unremarkable, and considerably less triumphant than the phrase suggests.

Every pattern of this kind has two ends, and describing only the difficult end leaves you with an accurate account of the problem and nowhere to walk. So here is the other end, translated out of abstraction.

| While the comparison is running | At the settled end | What it looks like on an ordinary Friday |
|---|---|---|
| Old video watched twice, second time for evidence | Watched once, for the event | You notice you are in it and then you notice what everyone was laughing at |
| "You have changed" lands as a verdict | It lands as a description | You ask what they noticed, and you are curious rather than defending yourself |
| A general sense of having lost something | One named item, tested | You can say what you miss in a noun, and you know whether you have tried it recently |
| Grading whether the change was good | The question left open on purpose | Somebody raises it at dinner and you find you have no strong position and do not need one |
| Not starting things, because the gap is widest at the start | Beginning things badly | You book the thing and are visibly the worst person in the room for a while |
| The current version has no description | It has one, built from recent history | Somebody asks what you have been up to and the answer has three things in it that happened this year |

Read the right-hand column carefully, because it is deliberately small. Nobody in it has recovered a former self, resolved anything, or arrived at a conclusion about who they are. Two things are true of all of it: the earlier version is still gone, and it has stopped being the measuring stick.

The last row is the one people underestimate and it is the most reliable indicator. At the settled end, the current version has accumulated enough of its own history to be describable, which is the actual mechanism by which the comparison stops mattering. It does not get resolved. It gets outcompeted.

Don't judge yourself if that column reads as distant right now. It is built out of a year of small things and nobody gets there by deciding to.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

It runs at three separate speeds, and confusing them is why people conclude nothing is happening.

**The recognition takes an afternoon.** Finishing the sentence and finding out whether a noun or an adjective comes out is a same-day piece of work, and most people are startled by how quickly the list arrives once the question is asked in those words. Several report that the answer they got was not the one they expected to give.

**Testing one item takes weeks and produces a clear result.** This is the fastest useful thing on this subject. A capability you have not attempted for six years gives you a definite answer within about three attempts, and the answer is usually more encouraging than the prediction. Where it is not encouraging, at least the loss is now specific and can be mourned properly instead of spreading out into a general verdict.

**The comparison itself fades on a slower and much less predictable schedule.** It goes quiet as the current version accumulates enough recent history to be describable, which takes a year or so and cannot be forced. Expect it to return in gaps: a quiet stretch, a birthday with a round number in it, somebody from the old setting getting back in touch. Its return is what an available afternoon does rather than a relapse.

Is that frustrating? Yes, and it takes real patience, and I would rather say so than sell a faster route that does not exist. The people who get somewhere with this are the ones who did the small specific thing repeatedly, long after the insight had stopped being interesting.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

The research and the reasoning, separated, so you can weigh each one.

Erikson introduced the term identity crisis in developmental writing from the 1950s and 1960s, naming the adolescent task of identity against role confusion. Two honest points about it are made on this page and both matter: it was never proposed as a diagnosis, and it was built on adolescents, so its application to a forty-year-old is an extension rather than a finding. Marcia's 1966 work operationalized it by measuring exploration and commitment separately, which is where the two-dimensional framing here comes from. That work has been argued over for decades, chiefly on whether a typology derived from an interview survives the move into questionnaires, and the part I have used is the separation of the two dimensions rather than the four-way sorting built on top of it. Crocetti and Meeus later added reconsideration of commitment as a third measurement. Those two researchers publish together on this line of work, so treat their contribution here as one source rather than as two independent confirmations of each other.

Quoidbach and colleagues published the survey of roughly nineteen thousand adults reporting past change and predicting future change, and it supplies the spine of this page. Its limits are stated in the body at some length and are worth restating: it is cross-sectional, so it never follows one person forward, and it compares a memory-based report against an imagination-based prediction, which means it cannot separate underestimating the future from overestimating the past.

Conway and Pleydell-Pearce set out the account of autobiographical memory as constructed at retrieval and shaped by the current self-concept, which is where the central claim on this page comes from, that the earlier version is a draft rather than a record. That account is well supported in outline and it licenses nothing about any particular memory of yours. It predicts drift across many retellings rather than error in a specific case, and I have tried to keep it to that.

The rest is mine and carries no research weight at all. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind and I am not a therapist. The five roots are a sorting device built from watching the same sentence turn out to be doing five different jobs in five different people, and their value is whether one of them makes you recognize something rather than whether they correspond to any published taxonomy.

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before building anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. The thing I got wrong for the longest time on this particular question was assuming the version of me I was measuring against had ever existed in the form I was remembering. When I finally went and read what I had actually written at the time, he turned out to be more anxious than the version in my head, less certain about nearly everything, and complaining about two of the same things I was still complaining about a decade later. Where it sits for me now is worth saying plainly: I still catch myself running the comparison, I catch it faster than I used to, and I would rather tell you that than sell a finished version of something nobody finishes.

## What Is Worth Doing This Week, Before You Try to Change Anything Back?

One list and one test. Change nothing else, announce nothing to anybody, and make no decisions about your life.

**The list.** Sit down with a sheet of paper and finish this sentence as many times as you can in ten minutes: *the person I used to be could ___ and I cannot.* Write whatever arrives, including the small and slightly embarrassing items. Most people produce between five and nine.

Now go through and mark each one **N** or **A**. **N** if it is a noun: a capability, a body, a specific way with particular people, a named activity. **A** if it is an adjective: freer, funnier, warmer, more spontaneous.

Look at what you have.

If it is nearly all A, you are almost certainly looking at ordinary change over time and possibly at a context that ended, and the accurate response is to stop treating a base rate as a symptom. Take the adjectives and ask which of them would return with four uninterrupted days and proper sleep. The honest answer is usually most of them, and knowing that is worth more than another year of the question.

If there are one or two Ns, those are the whole of your workable surface, and they are far smaller and far more specific than what you arrived with. Pick the one that still genuinely matters and run the test.

**The test.** Do that one thing once this week, badly, without preparing for it. If it is a capability, attempt it and record what actually happened rather than how it felt. If it is a way of being with people, do it with one person in a low-stakes setting. If it is an activity you stopped, do forty minutes of it rather than committing to anything.

You are testing exactly one question: is this gone, or is it unused? Those two produce identical descriptions from the inside and completely different answers, and no amount of reflection will separate them. Only contact will.

Expect the first attempt to feel wrong, and read that correctly when it happens. That is the very definition of change, that you are doing things differently than you had before, and so there is inevitable friction in that. The friction reports on the newness rather than on your capacity.

[Our Sacred Valley seminar](https://florianrolke.com/the-soulsearching-experience/) works at the layer underneath the list. You can write the list on your own and you should, before you go anywhere. What is hard to do alone is the part after it: working out which of the five roots is actually running, because they produce identical sentences and the sorting is where nearly everybody goes wrong by themselves, and then building something you will still be doing in March rather than a good week you look back on. Everybody is screened before arriving, which keeps the group small enough that a person who has spent six years quietly grading themselves against a video gets worked with properly rather than handed a worksheet. One limitation worth stating plainly: exact dates, the price and the final cap on group size are still being settled, so anybody comparing programs on those numbers this week cannot yet compare ours. Ask every program you are weighing the same questions, this one included, and decide from what comes back.
