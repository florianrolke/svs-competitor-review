# Why does the extra money from a raise never show up anywhere?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Because most of it went on the quiet removal of small annoyances rather than on anything you own. Each removal was sensible, none was memorable, and what you actually bought was the absence of an irritation. An absence leaves nothing behind to remember. So the raise lifted very little, and what it mostly did was lower what you are willing to put up with.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
Sentences people say about money that arrived and then went missing, usually while looking at a bank statement and finding nothing wrong with it.

- "I earn a lot more than I did and I honestly could not tell you what changed."
- "There is nothing to show for it. No holiday, no car, nothing in the account."
- "I am not extravagant. I do not know where it goes."
- "The idea of going back to how we lived four years ago feels like a punishment, and I could not tell you why."

Start with what this is not going to be, because you have almost certainly read the other version already.

Search this behavior and you get a bank, a brokerage, an encyclopedia entry and a personal finance publisher, and every one of them tells you the same thing. Your spending rose with your income. Automate the difference, bank the next increase, set a percentage, and the problem is solved. That advice is fine. It is arithmetically correct and it works for the people it works for, and none of them needs me to repeat it.

It also does not answer the question you actually asked. Your question was where did it go, which is a very different question from how do I stop spending more.

Because that part is genuinely strange, and nobody treats it as strange. A raise is a measurable, repeated, permanent increase in what arrives. It is one of the least ambiguous events in an adult life. And a person who has had two of them, across four years, frequently cannot name one single thing it bought. Not an object. Not a trip. Not a number in an account that is higher because of it.

That is not what spending more usually looks like. People who spend more on something can point at the something. Somebody who upgraded their car will tell you about the car. Somebody who moved will show you the room they moved for. The money is visible in the life, and if you asked them where it went, the question would seem odd to them.

Here the question is the whole experience, and nobody hearing it finds it odd.

So the useful thing to ask has nothing to do with stopping, and nothing to do with whether you were disciplined enough. The narrower and much more interesting question is this one: **why is the increase invisible?**

## What Does the Missing Money Actually Look Like on a Bank Statement?

Like nothing.

That is the part people find hard to explain to anyone else, and it is why the conversation usually stalls. There is no line item to point at. There is no month where something obviously changed. If you scroll back thirteen months and read every transaction, the account of a person earning meaningfully more and the account of the same person earning less look like the same person, doing similar things, with slightly different numbers next to them.

What has actually happened is spread so thinly that no single row carries it.

The grocery order is a little bigger and it arrives at the door. The phone plan is the one that does not need thinking about. There is a standing order for something that used to be a decision each time. Two subscriptions have been on the statement for so long that they read as fixed costs rather than as things somebody once chose. The trip involved a direct flight rather than a connection, and the difference between those two prices is sitting inside one airline charge that looks exactly like the airline charges from before.

None of that is a purchase in the way you mean the word. There is no unboxing. Nothing arrived that you kept.

And here is the specific thing that makes the money untraceable rather than merely hard to trace. The value of every one of those changes lies in something that stopped happening.

You stopped making the trip to the store on a wet evening. You stopped sitting in an airport for three hours between flights. You stopped doing the small calculation before adding something to a basket. You stopped rearranging a Saturday around one errand that had to be done in person.

Those are the purchases. Each one is real, each one cost money, and each one is defined entirely by the absence of an event.

## Why Can You Not Name a Single Thing the Raise Bought?

Because you are searching your memory for objects and events, and you bought neither.

Memory is built around things that happened. An evening where you drove out in the rain to collect something is a scene, with weather in it and a route and a particular irritation. It goes into memory as an episode, and it comes back when you look for it.

Four hundred evenings on which that trip did not happen are not four hundred episodes. They are not anything. Nothing occurred on them that could be filed. When you go looking for what the increase bought, you run a search over a system that only ever records occurrences, and the answer comes back empty, not because the money is unaccounted for but because the thing it purchased was specifically the non-occurrence.

This asymmetry is a well studied property of how learning works, and it has a name.

In 1970 Jenkins and Sainsbury described what became known as the feature-positive effect. The original work was with pigeons, and the finding was that animals learn a rule easily when the signal is the presence of something and struggle badly when the signal is its absence. Sainsbury published further work on the boundaries of the effect the following year. Hearst spent decades on the same asymmetry between what is there and what is missing, and the general result held up across a lot of different arrangements: presence is learnable, absence is not, or not without a great deal more effort.

The human version has been examined too. Cherubini and colleagues published a paper in 2012 with a title that gives the whole idea away, about missing the dog that failed to bark in the nighttime, reporting that people testing an idea systematically overweight what occurred against what did not occur.

**What that research covers and where it stops.** It establishes an asymmetry in learning and in reasoning tasks. It was built in laboratories, on discrimination problems and hypothesis tests, and none of it was ever about household spending. Extending it to a bank statement is my extension rather than a measured finding, and you should treat it as an explanation that fits rather than as evidence that has been collected. What I can say is that the shape matches exactly: a category of purchase whose entire content is a non-event will be systematically under-recalled by a mind that records events.

There is also a plainer version of the same point and it needs no research at all. Ask somebody what they did last Tuesday and they can usually tell you. Ask them to list what they did not have to do last Tuesday and they will look at you blankly, even though the second list is longer and, on this particular question, more informative.

## What Is This Called, and Where Does Each Name Stop?

Three separate vocabularies touch this, from three different fields, and none of them is a diagnosis. They are worth having accurately, including the parts that are considerably weaker than the way they get repeated.

**Lifestyle creep**, also called lifestyle inflation, is the name for the shape. It describes spending rising alongside income so that the increase produces no change in what is left over. It comes from personal finance writing rather than from research, and the honest status of it is worth stating plainly. There is no instrument. There is no measurement. No study has established it, no scale exists for it, nobody has ever produced or removed it under controlled conditions, and there is no figure anywhere for how common it is or who it happens to. It is a folk observation, widely recognized because it is accurate, and it explains nothing whatsoever about mechanism.

**The ratchet effect** comes from economics and it is the closest thing to a formal account of the second half of this page. Duesenberry published it in 1949 as part of the relative income hypothesis, arguing that a household's consumption is anchored to the highest level it has already reached, which is why spending rises easily with income and comes back down only under real pressure. He also argued that consumption depends on what people around you consume rather than on your income in isolation.

Its status needs saying too, and it is a good example of a strong idea losing an argument. Within about a decade it had been largely displaced, by Friedman's permanent income account in 1957 and by the life-cycle model that Modigliani and Brumberg had set out in 1954, both of which explained the same aggregate data with fewer moving parts and neither of which needed a ratchet. Duesenberry's version spent fifty years as a historical footnote. Relative consumption ideas have since been partly revived, and the specific claim relevant to this page, that removed frictions do not get reinstated when income falls, has never been cleanly tested by anybody as far as I can establish.

**Temporal reframing** explains why almost none of this ever registered as a decision about money. Gourville published a study in 1998 on what he called pennies-a-day framing, showing that the same expense described as a small ongoing amount is evaluated completely differently from the same expense described as a total, because a small recurring figure gets compared against trivial daily costs rather than against other large commitments. That is precisely the form nearly every convenience purchase arrives in. Nothing costs a lot. Everything costs a little, forever.

Where it stops: those were controlled comparisons run in a marketing context, about how a seller frames a price to a buyer, some decades old now, and they were never about a person's own drift across years. I am borrowing the mechanism and saying so.

| The name | What it genuinely covers | Where it stops |
|---|---|---|
| Lifestyle creep | The shape of the pattern, recognizably and memorably | A phrase from finance writing with no scale, no measurement and no study behind it |
| The ratchet effect | Why consumption resists coming back down once it has risen | Displaced within a decade by rival models, never cleanly tested for this specific claim |
| Temporal reframing | Why a recurring convenience is never weighed as a decision about money | Built on seller-side price framing in controlled comparisons, not on how a household drifts |

All three name the *what*. None of them names the *why*, and the why is what decides whether anything you do about it will work. Two people with identical statements, identical increases and identical missing money can be running the pattern for reasons that have nothing to do with each other.

## When Does Money Trouble Belong Somewhere Other Than a Page Like This?

This section goes early and deliberately, because the greatest failure of writing about money is addressing somebody in real difficulty as though their difficulty were a pattern.

I am not a financial adviser. There is no advice on this page about what to do with money, no percentages, no products, no accounts, no rules and no recommendations, and there will not be any further down either. That is a boundary rather than modesty, and writing outside it would be both useless to you and wrong of me.

If the actual situation is that what arrives does not cover what has to go out, this page describes something else entirely and you should stop reading it. Debt, a shortfall, arrears, an interest rate that has moved, a job that ended, a cost that appeared and did not leave. Those are practical problems with practical help attached to them, and the help is real. Free debt advice services exist in most countries and they are staffed by people qualified to look at an actual situation. That is a better call than anything here.

If the spending is compulsive rather than gradual, if it is hidden from somebody it affects, if it happens in episodes you cannot account for afterward, or if it runs alongside a persistent flatness that does not lift, that belongs with somebody licensed. Those things are treatable and this page is not the right instrument for any of them.

What is left, and what this page is about, is the specific case where nothing is wrong. The bills are paid. Nothing is hidden. There is no crisis. There is simply an increase that arrived and cannot be found.

## Why Do Two People Absorb the Same Raise for Completely Different Reasons?

Two households. Both earning meaningfully more than three years ago. Both unable to name what it bought. From outside, and inside a conversation about it, the two cases are indistinguishable, and the same advice will help one of them and do nothing at all for the other.

One of them bought time back and does not know what happened to the time.

The other never had a moment of choosing anything, because the money went before it arrived, into obligations that had already grown to meet it.

A third was buying relief from a specific daily irritation and would have paid considerably more than they did.

A fourth was keeping level with people whose lives they can see in detail, and would tell you honestly that they do not care what anybody thinks.

Handing the first one a rule about percentages does nothing, because their problem is an hour rather than a number. Handing the fourth one a lecture about comparison does nothing either, because they are not aware of doing it and the awareness is the whole difficulty. The four roots below are the ones this pattern most often grows from, and knowing which one you are standing in is what decides whether anything changes.

## Did You Buy Time, and Then Never Look at What Happened to It?

This is the most common root and the most defensible, which is exactly why it goes unexamined for years.

The exchange is straightforward and often excellent. You pay somebody to do a task, or you pay for a version of a thing that takes less of your day, and what you get back is hours. A cleaner every other week. Groceries delivered instead of collected. The direct flight instead of the connecting one, which buys back an afternoon rather than any comfort worth the name. Somebody else assembling the thing. The dry cleaning rather than the evening.

None of that is extravagance and I am not going to pretend it is. Trading money for hours is one of the more sensible things money does.

The question this root turns on is a different one, and almost nobody asks it. **What happened to the hours?**

Most people, asked directly, go quiet. Then they say something honest, which is that the hours did not appear as hours. They got absorbed, mostly by work expanding into them, sometimes by tiredness, occasionally by nothing identifiable at all. The Saturday that used to have an errand in it now has no errand and also nothing else, and the week feels exactly as full as it did.

If that is your root, the money did buy something real and the something never got used. That is a very different situation from having wasted it, and it points somewhere different too. The problem is downstream of the purchase, in what the freed time is for, and no amount of examining your spending will touch it.

**The tell for this root:** you can name the conveniences quickly when asked, you do not feel any guilt about them, and you cannot say what you did with the time they produced. People with this root often find the conveniences are worth keeping and that something else entirely needs attention.

## Was the Raise Spent Before It Ever Arrived?

Some people never had a version of their life with the increase in it.

This root has a specific shape. The raise gets absorbed by commitments that expanded in anticipation of it, or that were already sitting there waiting. A larger place taken when the offer came. A payment schedule started on the strength of the new figure. A family member who now receives something monthly. School fees. A repair on a bigger thing that only exists because of the bigger income.

The chain runs in one direction and it runs quickly. You need the job, and then you need the car in order to get to the job, and then you need the insurance in order to have the car, and then you need all kinds of things in case the car goes wrong. Complexity accumulates without anybody adding any of it on purpose. Each element is required by the element before it, and the whole structure was assembled without a single decision that could be pointed to.

People in this root frequently describe feeling poorer at a higher income, which sounds absurd and is accurate. What they mean is that their obligations became less flexible. More arrives, more is committed, and the portion that could go anywhere at all is smaller than it used to be.

**The tell:** you can actually name most of where it went, unlike the other three roots, but every item on the list feels compulsory. If asked what you would remove, you would say none of it, and you would be at least partly right.

## Who Are You Keeping Level With?

Handle this one carefully, because it is the explanation everybody reaches for first and it is right less often than it is invoked.

Duesenberry's original argument was that consumption is relative rather than absolute, and the intuition behind it is sound: people's sense of what a normal life costs is assembled from the lives they can see. That reference group used to be a street and a workplace. It is now considerably larger and considerably more detailed, and it consists disproportionately of people who are doing well, because that is what gets shown.

The reason to handle it carefully is that almost nobody experiences this as comparison. From inside, it feels like an updated sense of what is ordinary. Nobody thinks they upgraded a thing to keep up. They think the older version had become noticeably inadequate, which is a completely different sentence and it is the sentence people actually say.

**The tell, and it is a good one:** try to remember when the older version stopped being fine. People with this root cannot locate the moment, and when they reconstruct it they usually find the object did not change and did not break. Something around it did. A different job, a new circle, a move, a promotion that put them in different rooms.

I want to be careful not to overstate the evidence here. That comparison influences consumption is well supported in economics. That it explains any particular person's missing raise is not something anybody can establish, and the version of this root that circulates online, where everybody is performing wealth at everybody else, is far broader than anything that has been shown.

## What Were You Buying Your Way Out Of?

The fourth root is the one that produces the strongest feeling when a convenience gets removed, and it is the one people are most reluctant to describe honestly.

Some of these purchases were not buying time and were not buying status. They were buying the end of a specific recurring irritation, and the irritation was doing more to the person than they let on.

The checkout line at the wrong hour. Carrying something heavy up stairs. The particular quality of a Sunday evening spent doing the thing that has to be done before Monday. Waiting on hold. Not knowing whether something would arrive. Having to ask somebody for a lift.

For a person in this root, removing that irritation was worth substantially more than it cost, and they would have paid more. The purchase was genuinely effective, which is why it never came under review. It is also why reversing it feels so disproportionate later, because reinstating the task does not simply cost time. It reinstates the feeling.

**The tell:** there is one specific convenience on your list that produces an immediate no when you imagine giving it up, and the no arrives before any reasoning does. That one is worth understanding rather than removing. What it is doing for you is the useful information on this whole page, and it is rarely about the task itself.

## What Actually Rose, If Your Standard of Living Did Not?

Here is the reframe this page exists for, and it is the sentence worth taking away from it.

The raise did not raise your life. It lowered your tolerance.

Ask somebody in the middle of this to describe how much better their life is now than four years ago, at a meaningfully lower income, and watch what happens. They hesitate. They eventually say it is not really better, it is just normal. And they are being accurate. On any measure they can produce, the two lives are close to identical in quality.

Then ask them to go back to the earlier version for a year and watch what happens instead. That produces an immediate and physical no, and the no is completely out of proportion to the difference they just described.

Both reactions are correct, and holding them together is the whole thing.

The quality of the life barely moved. What moved is the level at which annoyance starts. Two years of not waiting makes waiting register as a problem. Two years of not checking a price makes checking one feel like an imposition. Two years of a direct flight makes a connection feel like a bad trip rather than like a normal one.

Tolerance moves down easily, because each individual downward step is an improvement and improvements are accepted immediately. It moves back up slowly and unpleasantly, because each upward step is experienced as a loss in the present tense.

Which produces the actual cost of this pattern, and it has nothing to do with money at all. **You now require more to be equally content.** That is a worse position than the one you started from, on any horizon longer than a few years, and it happened without a single decision being taken.

| What you would say | What is measurably true | What actually changed |
|---|---|---|
| "My life is not really any better." | Correct. On most measures the two lives are close. | Nothing about the quality of the life. |
| "Going back would be awful." | Also correct, and it feels physical. | The point at which irritation starts. |
| "I am not extravagant." | Usually correct. Nothing here is a luxury. | The number of things you decline to put up with. |
| "I have nothing to show for it." | Correct, and it is the defining feature. | Absences leave nothing to show. |

## Why Does Going Back Down Feel Like Losing Something?

Because a reversal works nothing like a purchase running backward. What actually happens is that a task gets handed back to you.

The two directions are wildly asymmetrical in how they feel, even where the arithmetic is identical. Adding a convenience costs money, which leaves in a form you barely perceive, at a moment when the benefit is immediate. Removing that same convenience costs time, attention and irritation, all paid in the present, in a currency you feel directly.

That asymmetry is why willpower does so badly here. Resolve has remarkably little to do with it. You are comparing an abstract number against a concrete Sunday afternoon, and the Sunday afternoon wins every time it is asked to compete.

It is also why the ratchet holds. Duesenberry's observation about consumption resisting downward movement was made about households in aggregate and it describes an individual week uncomfortably well. Things go up smoothly, in pieces, without discussion. They come down only when something forces them.

## Where Does This Hide When You Would Not Call It Spending?

In the places where nobody is looking for it, which is most places.

It hides in speed. Faster shipping, faster travel, faster food, the version that arrives sooner. Speed is almost never recorded as a purchase because it produces no artifact.

It hides in avoided friction of the social kind. Paying rather than asking. Booking rather than arranging. Hiring rather than borrowing. Those are purchases of not having to have a conversation, and they are among the least visible of all.

It hides in defaults. The tier that was chosen once, years ago, in a situation that no longer exists, and that has been renewing ever since. A default is not experienced as spending because no decision is being made this month.

It hides in the absence of comparison. Not the cheaper option chosen over the expensive one, but the moment where you simply stopped looking. No purchase happens there either. What ends is a habit, and its cost is spread across everything you have bought since.

And it hides in things that look like necessities from where you are standing now. The category that is hardest to see is the one that has been reclassified. Something that was a treat four years ago and is now simply how the week works has changed category without changing anything else, and reclassification leaves no trace at all.

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Four things get mistaken for this pattern and each one needs a different response.

**Real increases in what a life costs.** Rent, a child, care for a parent, a health condition, a currency, a rate. These are not a pattern and they do not need decoding. The distinguishing test is naming: real increases have names and dates, and you can list them in about a minute. This pattern is defined by having nothing nameable in it.

**Emotional buying.** That has a trigger, a time, a mood and an object, and it feels completely unlike this from the inside. If your spending clusters after difficult days and produces things you do not use, [that pattern has its own page and a different mechanism underneath it](/patterns/buying-things-when-upset/).

**Deferral.** Some people are not absorbing an increase at all. They are holding it against a condition that keeps being replaced, so the money accumulates and the life does not start. [That is a different pattern with its own page](/patterns/the-life-i-will-start-later/), and it also carries the honest version of the adaptation research that gets quoted at people on this subject, including the well known 1978 study with the half that usually gets dropped. If the mechanism you are looking for is why an achieved condition stops registering, read that one rather than this one.

**A standard that rises after every result.** If nothing you achieve ever counts and the target moves each time you reach it, the money version you are reading about here may be a symptom of a much larger pattern, and [the page on nothing ever being enough](/patterns/why-is-nothing-ever-enough/) holds it.

## Why Does the Usual Advice Bounce Off This?

Because it is aimed at a decision, and there was never a decision.

Every standard recommendation on this subject assumes an actor who is choosing to spend and who could choose otherwise. Bank the increase. Set a rule before the money arrives. Automate the difference. All of those are interventions on a moment of choosing.

Now go back through what actually happened in your case and find the moment. There is not one. Nobody sat down and decided to stop checking prices. Nobody chose a permanent shift in what they consider a normal amount of waiting. The delivery started one week when it was raining and simply continued. The plan was upgraded during a call about something else. The direct flight was booked because the connection was inconvenient on that particular occasion, and every subsequent booking was made by a person who now flew direct.

A rule applied to the total does nothing about any of that, which is why people who apply the rule frequently report that the money reappears somewhere else within a few months. They removed the arithmetic without touching the mechanism.

There is a second reason too, and it is more uncomfortable. The advice assumes the goal is to have more left over. For a lot of people running this pattern, that was never the goal. The goal was time, or ease, or a specific irritation ending, and they got those. The money went somewhere real. It was exchanged for something they wanted and never counted.

The thing that has actually gone missing is the headroom, which is the difference between what arrives and what leaves. That is the only part of a raise that behaves like a raise, and it is the first thing consumed, because it has no name, no date and nobody defending it.

## What Was the Raise Actually For?

This is the question that does the most work and almost nobody is asked it.

Most people took a raise for something specific and can still say what, if the question is put to them directly rather than through a spreadsheet. More time with somebody. Getting out of a particular situation. A door that opens in five years. Somebody else's security. Not having to think about one thing anymore. A place with a garden in it.

Write down the answer you gave yourself at the time. Then check whether the thing it was for has received any of it.

For most people the answer is no, and the reason is not a failure of will. The frictions ate it. Every one of them was individually reasonable and every one of them took a small piece of what the money was for, and because none of them left a trace, nothing ever announced that the thing it was for had been quietly defunded.

That is the sentence worth sitting with. Not that you spent too much. That the specific thing you were earning it for is now receiving nothing, and no event ever occurred to tell you.

## Does the Research Say Money Fails to Deliver?

Worth addressing directly, because somebody will quote a study at you on this subject within about a week of your mentioning it.

Whether higher income makes people more satisfied has been argued over in economics and psychology for fifty years. The argument is still live. Different groups have reached different conclusions depending on whether they compared people at one moment or followed the same people over time, whether they measured satisfaction with a life or feelings during a day, which countries they looked at, and over what span. Findings on both sides have been challenged on their methods, and at least one widely quoted threshold was later revised by a collaboration that included one of the people who originally reported it.

I am deliberately not quoting a number at you, because the honest summary is that the question is not settled and any single figure you have seen is one camp's answer.

None of it is needed here anyway. Whether money buys satisfaction is a separate subject with a separate literature. This page concerns an increase that produced no observable change in either direction, which is a narrower and stranger fact and one that no happiness study was ever designed to explain.

There is also a finding that runs against the direction of this page and it should be said. Sussman and Alter published work in 2012 on what they called exceptional expenses, reporting that people systematically underestimate and overspend on costs they treat as unusual, precisely because those costs get filed separately from the ordinary budget. That is the opposite of the claim here, where the ordinary is what goes missing. Both can be true, since they concern different categories, and I would rather show you the finding that complicates this than leave it out.

## Where Does This Thinking Come From?

From several public bodies of work, none of which require going through me to check, plus a good deal that is simply mine and carries no research weight at all.

On the asymmetry between presence and absence, Jenkins and Sainsbury described the feature-positive effect in 1970, finding that a signal defined by the presence of a feature is learned far more readily than one defined by its absence, and Sainsbury published further work on its boundary conditions in 1971. Hearst worked on the same presence and absence asymmetry across decades of learning research. Those two lines are closely related rather than independent, and should be counted as one body of work rather than as two confirmations. Cherubini, Rusconi, Russo and Crippa published the human version in 2012, on the overestimation of occurrences over non-occurrences in hypothesis testing. Rassin published on individual differences in susceptibility to the effect in 2023, which is worth knowing because it indicates the effect is not uniform across people and because the paper is recent enough to have been cited very little.

On consumption resisting downward movement, Duesenberry published the relative income hypothesis in 1949, including the ratchet argument that spending anchors to the highest level previously reached. It was largely displaced within a decade by Friedman's permanent income account in 1957 and by the life-cycle model of Modigliani and Brumberg from 1954. That displacement is worth knowing, because a great deal of writing quotes the ratchet as though it were settled economics rather than an argument that mostly lost.

On why a recurring cost never registers as a decision, Gourville published pennies-a-day framing in 1998 in the Journal of Consumer Research, finding that the same expense described as a small ongoing amount is evaluated against trivial daily costs rather than against comparable commitments.

On what complicates the picture, Sussman and Alter reported in 2012 in the same journal that people underestimate and overspend on expenses they categorize as exceptional, which points the other way from the argument made here.

The rest is mine. I hold no clinical qualification of any kind. I am not a therapist, a financial adviser, a debt counselor or an accountant, and nothing on this page is advice about what to do with money. None of the researchers named here has any connection to this page or to the seminar. Where their published work and my reading of it disagree, theirs is the one to go and check.

## What Does the Other End of This Look Like?

Considerably less dramatic than people expect, and mostly a change in visibility rather than in spending.

Somebody who has worked this out has not cancelled everything. They still have most of the conveniences, because most of them were good exchanges. What changed is that the list exists. They can name it, they know roughly what it costs across a year, and each item on it has survived being looked at rather than having never been looked at.

Two or three items usually go, and they are rarely the expensive ones. They tend to be the ones that turn out to have been solving a problem that no longer exists, or that were reclassified as necessities during a period that has ended.

One or two get deliberately kept and defended, including some a stricter person would remove, because they buy back something that is genuinely used.

And the next increase gets a written sentence attached to it before it arrives, saying what it is for. Not as a rule. As a record, so that in two years a comparison is possible, which is the one thing this pattern cannot survive.

The feeling at that end has nothing of virtue or restriction in it. What it mostly resembles is the ordinary relief of knowing where something went.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

The seeing part is fast and the changing part is not, and they run at completely different speeds.

Reconstruction takes an evening. Most people who sit down and describe an ordinary week from four years ago in physical detail, and then describe this week the same way, have their list within about half an hour and find the exercise slightly unsettling. That part is genuinely quick and it is the part that matters most, because everything else depends on having the list.

Deciding what stays takes a few weeks, because the items have to be considered one at a time and some of them need a trial rather than a judgment. The ones you are certain about are usually the ones you are wrong about in both directions.

Changing what your week structurally depends on takes months, because you are rebuilding a routine rather than making a decision, and routines move at the speed of routines. Anything you try to strip out all at once tends to collapse inside a month and teaches you nothing except that stripping things out all at once does not work.

The tolerance part is the slowest of all and it never fully reverses, which is worth knowing so it does not feel like failure. Having been used to a direct flight, you will always notice the connection. What changes is whether noticing it decides anything.

## What Is Worth Doing This Week, Before You Change Anything?

Reconstruct two weeks and compare them. That is the whole assignment and it requires you to spend exactly as you already do.

Here is the method.

1. **Pick a point three or four years back**, before the increase, and ideally one you can anchor to something you remember clearly. A place you lived, a job you had, a season.
2. **Describe an ordinary week from then in physical detail.** Not the money. The physical facts. What you carried, how you got places, what you waited for, what you did on a Saturday morning, what you checked before buying something, what you did yourself.
3. **Describe this week the same way**, in the same categories, with the same level of detail.
4. **Read the two accounts side by side and list every difference.** Every single one, including the ones that seem too small to write down. The small ones are the point.
5. **Beside each difference, write what stopped happening.** Not what you gained. What ceased. That column is the purchase you could not remember making, and seeing it written out is what this exercise is for.
6. **Then write one sentence at the bottom**, without thinking about it too hard, answering what you wanted the raise for at the time. Compare that sentence to the list above it.

That costs you an evening and no money at all, and it produces the one thing no article can hand you, which is a record of your own week in your own handwriting.

A few things to expect, since they catch people out.

The list will be longer than you think, usually between fifteen and thirty items. Several of them will surprise you, in the sense that you had genuinely forgotten the earlier version existed. At least one will produce an immediate defensive reaction, and that one is the most informative item on the page. And a portion of the list will turn out to be excellent value that you would choose again knowingly, which is a perfectly good outcome and the reason this is a reconstruction rather than a purge.

What the exercise deliberately does not do is tell you what to change. That decision is yours, it depends on things about your life I know nothing about, and I am not qualified to make it for you.
