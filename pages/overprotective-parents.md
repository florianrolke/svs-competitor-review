# What does growing up with controlling or overprotective parents do to you?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

It teaches a child that their own judgment cannot be trusted and that somebody else decides what is safe, and that lesson surfaces later in relationships rather than only in decisions. Closeness starts reading as the first step toward being managed, or the same worry runs outward and you become the one watching. Both directions are learned, and both loosen.

- "I always need to have an exit strategy."
- "Conflict in relationships is too distressing."
- "I'm afraid of becoming like the controlling people I've known."

The distance you keep from people, and the checking you do on the people you love, are two answers to the same childhood question.

You open the map on your phone and check where they are, just to see. The dot sits exactly where they said it would be, and about four seconds later a small flicker of shame arrives about having looked at all.

Thirty seconds, start to finish, and most of the pattern is inside them. A gap had opened between what you knew and what was happening. The gap registered as danger rather than as an ordinary Tuesday afternoon. Closing it produced relief, and the relief was followed immediately by the private knowledge that somebody at ease in their own life would probably not have needed to look.

Most people arrive at this question carrying one of two situations. Either a childhood where somebody worried about them constantly, and now, twenty years later, being loved has started to feel a lot like being supervised. Or the discovery that they have become the worrier: hovering at the edge of a playdate, sending the second text, watching themselves do it with something close to horror. Those look like opposite problems from outside. They grow from one root, which is why this page covers both.

One line before anything else, because it outranks everything below it. If somebody in your life controls your money, your movements, your phone, or your access to other people, if disagreeing with them produces threat, that describes a situation happening now rather than a childhood that ended, and it belongs with a licensed professional and often with people whose job is safety specifically. A fuller version of that sits a couple of sections down, and it applies in both directions.

Where all of this comes from has its own page. ## What Does This Sound Like From the Inside?

It sounds like a running risk assessment of closeness, phrased so reasonably that it passes for self-knowledge.

The sentences below are what people actually say in their own heads, in their own phrasing rather than a tidied-up version of it. They recur with unnerving consistency across people who have never met, who grew up on different continents, and who would describe their families as nothing alike. That consistency is the first clue that this is something installed rather than something anyone is. The wary version sounds like this:

- "Getting too close in a relationship might mean losing control over my life."
- "Relationships can trap you; better to keep some distance."
- "New relationships are risky; they might not understand my need for independence."
- "I need to be in control to be safe."
- "Commitments are traps."

There is a second set, and it is the one that surprises people, because it shows the pattern watching itself:

- "I don't want to be seen as overprotective or clingy."
- "I might be overprotective without realizing it."
- "I mustn't force my opinions; others might see it as controlling."
- "I hope I don't come off as rigid or inflexible."

Read both lists slowly. What matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word, in your own phrasing, with no translation required.

Notice how few of them sound like control. The first set reads as independence, maturity, a healthy sense of self. The second reads as conscientiousness. Almost nobody experiences either one as an injury, which is exactly how this runs for thirty years without ever being examined. There is no support group for people who value their independence and worry about being too much.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, before anything practical, because this boundary outranks every technique below it.

Some of what gets searched under these words describes a present situation rather than an old one. If an adult in your life controls your money, your movements, your phone, or who you are allowed to see, if disagreement produces threat, if you are managing somebody's mood to keep a household calm, or if there is violence of any kind in the picture, bring in a licensed professional and people whose job is safety specifically, including help with leaving if leaving is what the situation requires. Nothing written on a website substitutes for that.

The same holds in the other direction, and it deserves saying without shaming anybody, because the people it applies to are usually the ones most horrified by their own behavior. If your own checking has moved into somebody's phone, their accounts, or their movements without their knowledge, if it has kept escalating despite your intentions, or if the thought of stopping produces genuine panic, that has passed the point where a reading list helps. It is treatable, it is more common than the silence around it suggests, and a professional is the right address for it.

And the usual line, which matters here too. If closeness reliably brings back memories of being harmed, if you are carrying an addiction or a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, if hopelessness has sat on you for months without lifting, or if there is any thought of harming yourself however briefly it passes, that belongs with a clinician. Asking in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve.

What follows is built for the everyday version. The location check. The six-hour silence. The message rewritten four times.

## Why Does Closeness Itself Start Reading as Risk?

Because for a great many people the restriction arrived wrapped in worry rather than in orders, so proximity was the delivery mechanism, and the nervous system learned the association exactly as you would expect it to.

Consider how the two versions actually feel to a child. In the first, decisions are announced and the child's job is compliance. In the second, decisions are quietly taken over because the child could not possibly manage them, and every removal comes attached to genuine, visible concern. The second version is harder to complain about and does the same damage, because the outcome sits in what was never practiced rather than in what was intended. Both children finish childhood with an untested judgment and a standing sense that somebody else's read on a situation is the one that counts.

For the child raised inside the worried version, something extra gets learned. The person who took over was close. The taking over came with a hand on your shoulder and a real fear in their voice. So closeness gets filed alongside supervision, and the file never gets reopened, because nobody thinks to reopen it. Twenty years later a partner asks what time you will be home and something tightens for half a second before the ordinary answer arrives.

That is why the trigger in adulthood is so often affection rather than conflict. Somebody starts leaving things at your place. Somebody says they love you in the middle of a normal Wednesday. Somebody wants to know how the meeting went, out of nothing but interest. Any of those can produce a tightening and an urge to make a little room, and from inside it registers as a preference for space rather than as an alarm going off.

The other thread runs from the same root and travels outward. A child who absorbed the message that they could not be trusted to manage themselves sometimes grows into the adult holding that same worry over somebody else. The worry has to go somewhere, and a person you love is where it lands most easily. Then, characteristically, they catch themselves at it and are appalled, which produces the second list of sentences above.

## What Are the Two Directions This Runs In?

One holds people slightly further away than the situation calls for. The other holds them slightly closer. Both are answers to the same question, which is what happens when somebody else's decision lands on your life without warning.

| The moment | The wary direction | The protective direction |
|---|---|---|
| A partner mentions a solo weekend with old friends | A small jolt of dread, then a convincing performance of being fine with it | An immediate need for the details, who and where and what time, and a check-in agreed in advance |
| Somebody starts leaving things at your place | A tightening, and an urge to create a little distance this week | Relief, and a quiet plan for how to keep them there |
| A text goes unanswered for six hours | Withdrawal, and a decision to need less from this person | Three escalating messages and one phone call |
| A disagreement starts | It gets ended early, at almost any cost | It gets managed, resolved on their behalf, closed before it can open |
| Somebody you love takes a real risk | You step back and let it be their business, with a coldness inside the detachment | You step in before anything has gone wrong |
| Things are going genuinely well | The exit gets checked, quietly, in the background | The vigilance rises, because there is now more to lose |
| From outside | Independent, hard to pin down, slow to commit | Devoted, involved, a shade too involved |
| Underneath | "Close relationships can become suffocating." | "I need to be in control to be safe." |

Plenty of people run both columns, in different relationships or in the same one. Guarded with a partner and hovering over a child. Detached in romance and unable to let a friend struggle for a single afternoon. If both columns feel partly true, that is ordinary rather than confusing, and it is what you would predict from two branches on one root.

The version that confuses people most is the two running inside a single relationship at once. Clinginess and reassurance seeking on one axis, sudden coldness on the other, sometimes inside a single week. From the outside that looks like inconsistency. From the inside both moves are aimed at the same thing: keeping the relationship's temperature somewhere you can still read it.

## Why Do You Watch Yourself for Being Overprotective?

Because the vigilance turned around and pointed at itself, and this particular loop is the detail almost nobody expects.

Most descriptions of this pattern assume the person carrying it is oblivious. The opposite is closer to the truth. People raised under a watchful parent tend to run a background audit on their own behavior that is far harsher than anything an outsider would apply. The message gets rewritten four times so it does not read as too much. The follow-up question goes unasked because asking twice might look like checking. The reasonable request stays unmade for a fortnight while it is examined for signs of neediness.

Then, having said nothing and asked nothing, the person concludes privately that they are difficult, and the whole thing runs again next week.

This costs more than it looks like it costs. Two things get suppressed by the same audit. The genuinely excessive move, which is the one you were auditing for, and the completely ordinary one, which had nothing wrong with it. Somebody who cannot ask where their partner is on a Friday evening, out of a fear that asking makes them their mother, has lost access to a normal question. The relationship gets slightly starved by the very carefulness meant to protect it.

Don't judge yourself if you recognize this one. Anxiety about being controlling belongs to this pattern rather than being evidence against it, and it lands hardest on the people least likely to deserve it. The people who actually take over rarely lie awake wondering whether they take over.

## What Is the Distance Actually Buying You?

Insurance against a decision landing on you unannounced, the way one apparently did once before.

That is worth stating plainly, because arguing with the behavior has never worked for anybody and understanding what it purchases usually does something. Staying overly cautious in a new relationship, keeping a measure of emotional detachment once things get close, never quite letting a relationship become the main event of your life, all buy the identical product. Nobody gets close enough to make a choice that reshapes your week without your say-so.

The reassurance-seeking and the overanalysis buy the same product from the opposite angle. Staying so alert to a relationship's temperature that nothing can shift without your noticing first is a form of insurance too, and it is the version that gets mistaken for care most often, by the person doing it as much as by anybody watching. Reading a reply four times for tone is monitoring wearing the clothes of affection, and it exhausts the person doing it.

Where the pattern runs outward, the protecting is itself the function. Holding tightly onto somebody else's safety puts an old, unmetabolised fear to work on a target that feels more manageable than the original one ever was. A parent watching a nine-year-old at a playground is managing something that happened long before the nine-year-old existed. This is why the reassurance rarely lands: telling someone their child is fine addresses the wrong decade.

Seen that way, every branch of this is doing something protective and doing it efficiently. The problem is the price, which gets paid in relationships that stay slightly thinner than they could be, over decades, without any single moment where the cost becomes visible.

## Where Does It Hide When You Would Not Call It Control?

In more places than most people connect to a childhood, which is why this usually gets filed as five unrelated personality quirks instead of one thing.

|---|---|---|
| With a partner | Reading a reply for tone, knowing the plan for the evening before agreeing to it, a jolt at the mention of a solo trip | "Getting too close in a relationship might mean losing control over my life." |
| With somebody new | The message rewritten four times, the deliberately slow warm-up, the long pause before anything gets called anything | "New relationships are risky; they might not understand my need for independence." |
| With friends | Difficulty keeping anything light, an unanswered text expanding to fill an afternoon | "Relationships can trap you; better to keep some distance." |
| With your children | Hovering at the edge of the playdate, stepping in a half second before anything has actually gone wrong | "I might be overprotective without realizing it." |

Difficulty sustaining casual, light company is a genuine marker here, because a light friendship offers nothing to monitor and no way to steer what it becomes. Someone who can do intense and can do nothing, with no comfortable middle available, is usually looking at this rather than at introversion.

The work row splits by branch, exactly as it does with people. Refusing to delegate and refusing to be managed are two versions of the same sentence. ## Why Does a Six-Hour Silence Turn Into a Catastrophe?

Because an information gap fills with worst cases considerably faster than it fills with ordinary ones, and this pattern treats the gap itself as the danger.

Watch the sequence in slow motion. A friend does not reply. Within about ninety seconds the mind has produced three explanations: they are annoyed with you, something has happened to them, they have decided you are too much. The boring one, that they are in a meeting with their phone face down, arrives fourth if it arrives at all. It is also correct roughly every time.

The reason for that ordering is worth understanding, because it stops the whole thing feeling like a character flaw. A system built to prevent surprise cannot afford to guess low. When your job is to make sure nothing lands on you unannounced, generating the worst case first is efficient, and the cost of being wrong in that direction feels much smaller than the cost of being caught out. Thirty years of that produces an adult who is genuinely faster at catastrophe than at Tuesday.

Then the second move happens, which is the one that costs. The gap gets closed. A message goes out asking if everything is okay. A location gets checked. A question gets asked twice in slightly different words. Relief arrives within seconds, and the relief is the trap, because it teaches the system that the checking worked. Nothing gets learned about whether the silence was ever dangerous, because the silence was never allowed to finish.

This is the exact mechanism that makes the pattern so durable in otherwise self-aware people. Every closed gap is a rehearsal. Every open one would have been evidence.

## Is This Overprotection, Enmeshment, or Something Else?

Worth checking carefully, because six fairly different histories produce a similar-looking Thursday, and the useful response to each one differs.

| What you notice | What it might actually be | What tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Cannot make a decision without checking it against somebody's imagined reaction | The authority version of this same wound | [Controlling parents](/patterns/controlling-parents/) attacks whether you may decide at all. This attacks whether closeness is survivable |
| Closeness that leaves no room for a separate self, feelings treated as shared property | Enmeshment | [Enmeshment](/patterns/enmeshed-family/) is a question of whether a separate self is permitted to exist. A family can be deeply enmeshed with nobody protecting anybody |
| Threat scanning that never switches off, in any setting at all | Hypervigilance | [Hypervigilance](/patterns/hypervigilance/) needs no relationship to run in. It works the same way in an empty car park at midday |
| Suspicion about specific people's honesty and motives | A breach of trust | [Trust issues](/patterns/trust-issues/) follow a deception and produce wariness about being deceived. Restriction produces wariness about being steered |
| Distress when a partner's attention goes somewhere else | Jealousy | [Jealousy](/patterns/why-am-i-so-jealous-in-relationships/) runs on being replaced. This runs on being managed, or on losing the ability to manage |
| Managing everybody's feelings until you are exhausted | A caretaking pattern | [Feeling responsible for everyone](/patterns/feeling-responsible-for-everyone/) adds a duty. This adds a supervision, which is a different weight |
| Panic at the possibility somebody will leave | Abandonment fear | [Fear of abandonment](/patterns/fear-of-abandonment/) fires on losing them. This fires on losing yourself inside the relationship |
| Over-planning, rigidity, real distress when plans change | A need for predictability | [The certainty version](/patterns/need-for-certainty/) has no person inside it. It runs on outcomes rather than on someone's involvement in your life |

The enmeshment row is the one worth reading twice, because these two get collapsed constantly and the practical routes out of them differ. Enmeshment is about permission to be a separate person inside a relationship, with feelings and decisions treated as jointly owned. This pattern is about authority and safety specifically: whether somebody else's involvement reads as interest or as a takeover, and whether your own involvement in their life has quietly become supervision. A relationship can be thoroughly enmeshed with nobody protecting anybody, and a relationship can be thick with protection and control anxiety while both people remain entirely separate selves.

Most people find two or three rows partly true. Households rarely specialize, and neither do wounds. The value of the table is directional: it tells you which practice went missing, which tells you which repetitions to run first.

## What Is the Difference Between Protecting Someone and Controlling Them?

Protective concern stops once it has been heard. Control continues past a stated preference against it.

That test is short enough to use in the moment, and it survives contact with real situations better than any list of behaviors, because the identical behavior can belong on either side of the line. Asking a partner to text when they land is care in one relationship and a tracking requirement in another. What separates them is what happens on the evening they forget.

Two more markers make it usable:

1. **The involvement has an off switch that somebody else can reach.** They say they would rather handle it themselves, and the matter closes. It does not reopen on Thursday in a slightly different form.
2. **Disagreement costs a normal amount.** Someone declining your help produces mild disappointment rather than distress out of proportion to what is actually at stake. Distress in the wrong size is the most reliable tell in the whole pattern, in either direction.

Run the same test on yourself from the receiving side. If somebody's involvement in your life leaves your judgment intact and consults it, that reads as care whatever tone it arrives in. If it substitutes for your judgment, the tone stops mattering. Plenty of takeovers arrive gently, and plenty of blunt people leave your decisions entirely alone.

There is one honest complication worth naming. Sometimes the thing you are worried about genuinely warrants attention. A friend really is drinking too much. A child really is too young for that road. The test still holds, because it says nothing about whether your concern is accurate. It only asks whether your involvement continues after a clear preference against it, and a correct worry expressed as an override is still an override.

## Why Do You Keep Ending Up in the Same Two Kinds of Relationship?

Because familiarity reads as safety, and there are exactly two ways for that to play out.

The first is a partner who takes over. Somebody with strong opinions about your choices can register as decisive, grounded, someone who knows what they want. The nervous system recognizes the shape of it and relaxes into something it can predict, which is a real form of comfort even when the thing being predicted was the original injury. Nobody chooses this consciously and nobody deserves it. [Why the same person keeps arriving in different bodies is worth reading separately.](/insights/why-do-i-attract-the-same-people/)

The second is a partner who needs rescuing. This one gets less attention and costs just as much. A relationship with somebody genuinely struggling gives the protective impulse a legitimate home, so the vigilance can run at full volume and be praised as devotion. The trouble surfaces years in, when the other person steadies and the relationship discovers it has no other shape available to it. What looked like love turns out to have been a job, and the ending of it is confusing for both people.

There is a third arrangement, quieter than either, where you end up with somebody undemanding and permanently at arm's length, which keeps the question of closeness comfortably theoretical. That one can last decades without any crisis, and it rarely gets examined, because from outside there is nothing to examine.

## What Do Balanced Relationships Actually Look Like?

Unremarkable, which is the part most people are unprepared for.

Every pattern like this has two ends, and describing only the wounded end leaves you with an accurate account of the problem and nowhere to walk. So here is the other end, translated out of abstraction and into an ordinary Thursday.

| The guarded or watchful end | The balanced end | What it looks like on an ordinary Thursday |
|---|---|---|
| Closeness read as the start of a takeover | Closeness read as closeness | A partner asks how your day went and it lands as interest, first time, with nothing to decode |
| Monitoring | Ordinary not knowing | Four hours pass with no idea where they are, and nothing about that is loud |
| Concern repeated until it is satisfied | Concern said once and set down | You ask if a friend is alright, they would rather not go into it, and you actually let it go |
| Their plans as your problem | Their plans as theirs | A solo trip gets a real "have a good time" on the first attempt, with nothing rehearsed behind it |
| An exit kept ready in the background | Commitment without a back door | The lease gets signed and no escape route gets sketched that evening |
| Going quiet requires a reason | Going quiet requires nothing | You are unreachable for an afternoon and narrate none of it afterward |
| Conflict avoided or managed on their behalf | Conflict simply had | A disagreement runs its course on Thursday and the relationship is still there on Friday |
| Casual friendship as impossible | Casual friendship as easy | Somebody you like moderately stays somebody you like moderately, and that is a whole category again |
| Reassurance sought on a schedule | Reassurance rarely needed | You notice you have not asked whether things are fine in a fortnight |
| A child's risk as unbearable | A child's risk as theirs to meet | The first solo drive happens and you sit in the kitchen with the feeling rather than sending a text |
| Help offered to you read as an angle | Help offered to you read as help | Somebody does something for you and you say thank you, adding nothing, repaying nothing |

Read the right-hand column carefully, because it is deliberately unimpressive. Nobody in it is having a breakthrough. There are no reckonings, no long conversations, no announcements. What changes is the overhead: the decoding, the scanning, the rehearsing, the four rewrites. Somebody's involvement in your life reads as interest rather than as a takeover, and somebody else's freedom stops registering as a threat to the relationship. Closeness stops requiring a plan for how to get out of it.

Don't judge yourself if that column reads as slightly science-fictional today. The distance between the two columns took a childhood to build, and nobody crosses it in a fortnight.

## What Actually Moves It?

Tolerating a gap in information on purpose, over and over, until the gap stops being read as an emergency.

Nobody has a clean five-step protocol here that survives contact with a real relationship, and anyone selling one is selling something. What the mechanism suggests, and what people who shift this tend to report, is smaller and duller than that. The vigilance was built by evidence, so evidence is the only currency it accepts.

1. **Leave one gap open a day, deliberately.** Do not check the location. Do not open the app. Let the afternoon contain a hole in your information and let the hole stay there. This is the whole exercise in miniature, and it is much harder than it reads.
2. **Let the six hours pass.** An unanswered message gets to sit unanswered. No second text in different words, no reading of the last exchange for tone, no calling to check. What you are collecting is one more instance of nothing happening.
3. **Wait for the fourth explanation.** When the mind produces three worst cases in ninety seconds, stop and deliberately generate the boring one. Meeting. Battery. Asleep. Then wait for reality to say which was right, and keep a private count of how often the boring one wins.
4. **Say the concern once, out loud, then physically do not follow up.** This is the practice for the protective direction and it is the harder of the two. Name it clearly, in one sentence, then let their handling of it be the data instead of your handling of it. Circling back on Thursday in slightly different words undoes the whole repetition.
5. **Ask for room in plain words instead of taking it.** "I want a quiet evening on my own this week" costs one sentence and no withdrawal. The silent version costs the other person a fortnight of wondering what they did.
6. **Send the first version of the message.** The one you wrote before the four rewrites. It was fine. The rewriting is the audit running, and the audit has never once improved a message it touched four times.
7. **Let one thing be handled badly by somebody allowed to handle it badly.** Hand over something real, resist the check-in, and let the result be slightly worse than yours would have been. The non-collapse is the point.
8. **Notice which weeks it spikes in.** A genuine bad stretch in the relationship, an anniversary you had forgotten, a child reaching the age you were during the worst year of your own childhood. The spike is information about the past rather than about the present, and knowing that in advance takes some of its authority away.

Is any of this comfortable? No. It is small and freaking repetitive, and it works for exactly that reason, because it stays under the threshold that would set the alarm off properly.

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. The thing I underestimated longest with this particular pattern was how much of the vigilance felt like love from the inside. Every check had a reason attached, every reason was defensible on its own, and it took a long time to see the shape they made together. Where it sits for me now is worth saying plainly: the gap between the urge and the action is much wider than it was, and the urge still turns up during a hard week, and I would rather tell you that than sell a finished version of something nobody finishes.

## What If You Are the One Being Watched?

Then a different set of moves is available to you, and only one of them involves the other person changing.

Describe the specific behavior without attaching a label to the person, because "I'd rather you didn't check where I am" opens a conversation and "you're controlling" closes one. Say what you want in plain words, once, without three supporting reasons attached, since a request that arrives with a legal case attached invites a counter-case. Then watch what happens over a couple of months rather than a couple of days.

Somebody genuinely working on this will show you uneven progress: a stretch where nothing gets checked, then a wobble during a hard week, then another good stretch. Somebody with no intention of working on it will show you the same month repeatedly with apologies attached. That difference is legible over eight weeks and almost illegible over eight days, which is why the timeline matters.

Set your own limit privately and quietly. A limit announced as an ultimatum produces compliance, and compliance looks almost identical to change for about six weeks. A limit you hold without announcing produces information, which is the thing you actually need. [Holding a line without detonating the relationship has its own page](/patterns/how-to-set-boundaries/), and it is worth reading before any big conversation.

And the line from earlier applies here more than anywhere else on this page. If what you are inside involves threat, restricted access to your own money or your own people, or any violence at all, that is a safety matter and it needs professionals rather than patience.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Months rather than days, and the first stretch feels considerably worse than doing nothing.

That last part is worth knowing in advance, because it is where most people quit. The vigilance was built to prevent something that once felt genuinely dangerous, so the first several unmonitored gaps get treated as an emergency rather than as evidence. You leave the location unchecked and the afternoon is loud. You let the message sit and the sitting costs you something real. The relief people expect to feel immediately shows up much later, and only after enough gaps have passed uneventfully for the system to update.

Recognition, as usual, arrives fast. Most people can spot the sequence inside a few weeks of paying attention: the gap, the three worst cases, the closing move, the flicker of shame. Catching it counts even on the days you check anyway. Watching yourself do something while knowing precisely what it is feels terrible and is a genuine step forward.

Expect the old habit to reassert itself hardest during an actual bad week in the relationship, when the original fear it was built to manage gets even slightly confirmed. A canceled plan, an evasive answer, one genuinely late night. The pattern will present itself as vindicated. Read that as the design working as designed rather than as your progress being fake, and go back to the repetitions once the week is over.

Does the urge disappear entirely? Honestly, no. What grows is the gap between the urge and the hand reaching for the phone, and eventually the ability to feel the whole thing and let the afternoon stay unknown anyway.

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Change Anything With Anyone?

Run a gap log for forty-eight hours. Open a note on your phone. Every time you close an information gap, write down three things only: what you did, what you were afraid of, and what turned out to be true. Checking a location counts. Asking a question twice counts. Rereading an exchange for tone counts. Rewriting a message counts. No analysis, no resolutions, two days.

Then read it on the second night and look at two numbers. First, how many entries there are, which is almost always higher than people expect, because the small closings are invisible while they happen. Second, how many times the feared thing was actually true, which is almost always zero or close to it. Seeing that written in your own handwriting does more than a month of deciding to trust people, because it argues in the only language this pattern accepts, which is evidence rather than reassurance. It costs you nothing but the noticing, and it works whether or not you ever do anything else about it.
