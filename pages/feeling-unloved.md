# Why do I feel unloved?

<div class="answer-box" markdown="1">
<span class="answer-label">The short answer</span>

Feeling unloved usually comes from one of two histories. In the first, warmth genuinely did not arrive, and a child with no cause to point at concluded the reason was them. In the second, warmth did arrive, sporadically or on conditions, and never got taken in as real. Both end in the same sentence, and they ask for different work.
</div>

<div class="identify-box" markdown="1">
These get thought rather than said out loud. One of them usually lands harder than the rest.

- "There is something fundamentally unlovable about me."
- "Even when someone shows me affection, I'm waiting for it to end."
- "I have to go above and beyond to keep them interested."
- "No matter how much affection I get, it never feels enough."
- "Even when I'm surrounded by people, I feel unloved and alone."

Sentences like those keep running underneath relationships that are genuinely affectionate, and more reassurance rarely reaches them.

</div>

Somebody compliments your work and the words "oh, it was nothing" are out of your mouth before the sentence has landed anywhere. Later that evening you open a message a parent sent you eight months ago, the one warm one, and read it again for maybe the tenth time this year, while the hundred ordinary messages either side of it have gone from memory entirely.

Those two moments look unrelated and they are the same mechanism running twice. Warmth arrives and gets refused at the door. Warmth is scarce, so the rare piece of it gets carried around for years like a receipt.

Most people who search this phrase have already tried to settle the question by argument. They have listed the people who clearly care about them, reminded themselves that their parents did their best, noticed that by any external measure they are not short of affection, and found that none of it changes the feeling by a single degree. That is worth knowing early, because the argument was never going to work. The feeling is not being produced by a shortage of evidence.

This page splits the question in two, because two quite different histories produce the identical adult sentence, and almost nothing written about this distinguishes them. In one, love genuinely did not arrive. In the other, love arrived in a form you were never taught to read. What follows covers how each one forms, how to tell which is yours, why being told you are loved changes so little in either case, and what the other end of it actually looks like in an ordinary week. If you are reading this while something acute is happening, hopelessness that has not lifted for months or any thought of harming yourself, that belongs with a doctor or a crisis line today rather than with a page like this.

## What Does Feeling Unloved Sound Like From the Inside?

Like a set of conclusions reached a long time ago and repeated so often that they stopped presenting themselves as opinions.

The lines below are what people in this pattern actually say, in their own phrasing rather than a tidied-up version of it. They recur with an unnerving consistency across people who have never met each other and whose lives look nothing alike. Read them slowly. What matters is the small physical jolt when one of them turns out to be yours word for word, with no translation required.

The first group is the flat verdict, usually arrived at before adolescence:

- "I'm not the kind of person people form deep connections with."
- "No matter what I do, I'm never going to be worthy of anyone's love or affection."
- "I'm just not the kind of person who gets loved deeply."
- "I'm not meant to be loved the way I want."
- "It's inevitable; I'll always end up unloved."
- "Every failed relationship is a reflection of my inadequacy."

The second group is the audit, which runs quietly on every relationship you have:

- "They seemed distant today. It must be because I'm not lovable enough."
- "They didn't explicitly say they love me today; they must be losing interest."
- "Every word they say is a clue about how little they love me."
- "If we're not constantly in touch, it means the love is fading."
- "They didn't like my post on social media; I must be unlovable."
- "I'm always on alert for signs that someone doesn't like me."

The third group is the price list, and it is the one that quietly organises whole careers and whole marriages:

- "I need to earn love and affection by being what others want me to be."
- "I must perform perfectly to keep someone's love."
- "I have to put their needs first; that's the only way to make sure they love me."
- "I need to mirror what my partner wants to be loved."
- "I have to be careful not to express too much emotion; it might scare them off."
- "Affection always seems to have strings attached."

The fourth group is what happens when love does arrive:

- "They're just saying that. They don't really mean it."
- "People only show me affection out of pity or obligation."
- "Even when they love me, I feel overlooked and secondary."
- "Any affection I receive is tinged with the fear of its loss."
- "In love, I always feel like I come second, never first."

And a fifth, about being on your own, which people tend to admit last:

- "I can't stand being alone; it reminds me of how unloved I am."
- "Being single means I'm unlovable."
- "Being in any relationship is better than being single."

Notice how few of these are complaints about anybody else. Almost every one is a verdict on the self, and that self-directed shape is the clearest signature this pattern has. Somebody who was actively mistreated has a person to be angry at. Somebody whose warmth simply never registered has no visible cause anywhere in the room, so the cause gets assigned inward.

Notice the fourth group especially. Those are the sentences of a person who is receiving affection right now and cannot use any of it. That group is why this page has to split the question in two.

## When Does This Belong With a Professional Rather Than a Page Like This?

Right here, near the top, because this line matters more than everything practical below it.

If somebody in your life uses warmth as a lever, restoring it the moment you back down and withdrawing it the moment you assert something, you are looking at a control dynamic rather than at your own history, and it belongs with a licensed professional. Bring in people who can genuinely help, including people who can help you leave if that is what the situation requires.

The same holds looking backward. If your childhood contained violence, sexual harm, or an adult whose cruelty you can describe in specifics, that is different territory from the one this page covers and it deserves a clinician rather than a self-directed practice.

The present tense counts too. An addiction, a diagnosed condition you are currently managing, hopelessness that has not lifted for months, or any thought of harming yourself, however quickly it passes, all belong with a professional. Asking for help in month one costs far less than asking in month twelve, and nobody hands out a prize for doing this part alone.

Nothing here is a diagnosis. Feeling unloved describes an experience rather than a condition anybody gets diagnosed with, and no page can tell you what happened in your house. What a page can do is hand you accurate language for something you have probably been carrying without any.

## Why Do Two Very Different Childhoods End in the Same Sentence?

Because there are two ways to arrive in adulthood without the felt experience of being loved, and only one of them involves anything having been missing from the room.

The first is the straightforward one, and it is the version almost every article on this subject assumes. Attention, mirroring and affirmation genuinely did not arrive. Nobody was curious about what you felt, nobody noticed when you were upset, nobody asked. There is no scene to remember because a non-event leaves nothing behind that behaves like a memory, which is exactly why an adult searching their own childhood for evidence keeps coming back empty and misreads the empty result as proof that nothing is there. [The full account of how that forms, and why it hides so well, has its own page.](/patterns/childhood-emotional-neglect/)

The second is subtler and it gets almost no coverage anywhere. Warmth did arrive. It arrived rarely, or unpredictably, or attached to conditions, or from a person whose mood decided the weather that week, and a child living inside that learns something specific: affection is a reward, its arrival cannot be predicted, and the sensible response is to watch for it constantly and never quite trust it. That child grows into an adult standing inside real affection, from a partner who means it, and registering almost nothing.

The vocabulary people reach for is the same in both cases. Emotionally starved. Deprived. Invisible. Unseen in their own house. What differs is where the gap actually sits, and that difference decides what to do about it.

| | Warmth that did not arrive | Warmth that arrived unreadable |
|---|---|---|
| What the house looked like | Safe, functional, and emotionally quiet. Nobody asked anything | Warm in bursts, then flat. A mood decided the temperature that week |
| What you remember of it | Very little, and a shrug when somebody asks | Two or three warm scenes, in extraordinary detail, decades later |
| The sentence that fits | "I often feel invisible in social situations." | "They're just saying that. They don't really mean it." |
| Where the gap sits | Nothing was supplied | Supply arrived and got refused at the door |
| What happens when love shows up now | It has nothing to attach to, so it slides off | It gets inspected, discounted, and filed as insincere |
| The honest question | Was it ever actually given | Was I ever able to take it in |
| What the work looks like | Building a function that was never installed | Practicing reception, one small signal at a time |

Both routes end with the same final move, and it is the move that does the real damage. The absence gets explained as a property of the self. A child cannot conclude that the adults around them were overwhelmed, unwell, or never shown how to be curious about anybody's inner life, including their own. That conclusion needs a vantage point nobody has at seven. What a child can conclude, and reliably does, is that the warmth other children appear to get is being withheld for a reason, and the reason must be them. Almost everything downstream follows from that single misattribution, and [the shape of that first wrong conclusion is worth understanding on its own terms](/insights/the-core-wound/).

## Which of the Two Is Yours, and Does It Change Anything?

It changes what you practice, which is the whole reason for separating them.

Three questions get most people to a usable answer within a few minutes. Ask them slowly and take the first response rather than the considered one.

1. **When you try to remember affection from childhood, what happens?** A blank, a shrug, and a genuine inability to name a single scene points toward the first version. Two or three specific moments, held vividly, replayed often, still carrying weight thirty years later, points toward the second. The second one is easy to mistake for a happy childhood, and the vividness is the tell. Scenes get held that tightly when they were rare.
2. **When somebody is warm toward you today, what is the first thing that happens inside?** Nothing much, a kind of flatness, no reaction to speak of, suggests the function was never built. A fast, specific, almost argumentative rebuttal, they are being polite, they want something, they will find out eventually, suggests the function exists and is set to reject.
3. **Was affection at home predictable?** Consistently thin is one story. Feast and famine, generous one week and unreachable the next, is a different one, and it produces the watching.

Most people find they are a blend, in some proportion, and the proportion matters more than the label. The practical difference sits in the homework. Somebody who never received warmth is building a capacity from scratch, which is slower and needs more outside help, since a function that formed in the absence of another person's attention tends to rebuild in the presence of one. Somebody who received warmth and could not read it is running interference on an existing capacity, and the useful work is catching the rejection in the two seconds it takes place.

Getting this wrong sends people to the wrong task for years. Somebody in the second group who assumes they were simply never loved goes looking for more supply, more reassurance, more proof, and stays hungry inside relationships that are objectively feeding them. Somebody in the first group who assumes they must be blocking something blames themselves for a capacity nobody ever taught them.

## Why Does Real Affection Sometimes Fail to Register at All?

Because there is a door, and most of what arrives gets turned away at it in under two seconds.

Watch the move happen in slow motion and it is remarkably consistent. A piece of warmth comes in. It contradicts an account of yourself that has been running since primary school. The mind now has two options, update the account or discount the evidence, and it takes the cheaper one every single time. The compliment gets reclassified as politeness. The invitation gets reclassified as somebody being short of options. The apology gets reclassified as somebody managing you. The account survives intact, and it survives the next thousand pieces of evidence exactly the same way.

Four kinds of incoming warmth get refused, and people usually notice only one of them in themselves:

1. **Praise.** The compliment gets deflected, corrected, joked away, or answered with a compliment inside the same minute, which levels the exchange and cancels the debt.
2. **Help.** Somebody does something for you and a favor goes back within the hour. Reluctance to accept help is the same reflex operating on a bigger unit.
3. **Reassurance.** You ask, you receive an answer, and the relief lasts about ninety minutes before the question re-forms. Reassurance that gets sought from outside always expires, which is why it needs re-supplying so often.
4. **Ordinary, undramatic consistency.** This is the one almost nobody catches. Somebody who is reliably kind, week after week, produces no spike, no rescue, and no dramatic moment. A system trained on rare intense warmth reads a flat consistent signal as no signal at all. Steady affection can be arriving daily for years and get filed as nothing much.

That last one explains a complaint that sounds ungrateful and is genuinely bewildering from inside: restlessness and dissatisfaction inside a relationship that is going well, with no trigger anybody can find. The relationship is supplying love in the format the wound cannot read. It shows up as a low hum of something being missing, on an ordinary Tuesday, in a life that photographs well.

I spent five years and more than five thousand hours doing this kind of work on myself before I built anything around it, and more than fifty thousand dollars on seminars, therapy, books, and teachers, some found in the jungles of Vanuatu, some in the jungles of Peru. What took me longest to accept was how much of it sat on my side of the exchange. Warmth had been arriving for years and I had been discounting it at the door, fast and automatically, without ever catching myself doing it. Where it sits for me now is worth saying plainly: the discount still happens, it happens less, and I would rather tell you that than sell a finished version of something nobody finishes.

## Why Would Feeling Unloved Be Protecting You From Anything?

Because the conclusion that it was your fault is the more survivable of the two conclusions available to a child.

This is the least intuitive part of the whole pattern and the most important. Put yourself in a house where warmth is thin, and consider what a seven-year-old can actually think. One available conclusion is that the adults responsible for you are unable to give you what you need, which means it is not coming, and you cannot leave, and there is nothing to be done. The other available conclusion is that something about you is causing it, which is unbearable in a different way and carries one enormous advantage. A fault can be fixed. Be better, be quieter, be more useful, be perfect, and the supply might start.

Self-blame buys hope. That is why it gets chosen, why it holds so tightly, and why arguing with it as an adult has never worked for anybody, including you. Underneath "there is something wrong with me that makes me unlovable" sits a child's plan to earn their way out, and the plan is still running.

Everything else in the pattern grows from there and sorts into four jobs, which often run in the same person at once:

1. **Manufacturing what did not come freely.** Approval-seeking, over-accommodation, excessive gifting, chameleon-like adjustment of your opinions to match whoever is in the room, and achievement pursued as a way of becoming worth keeping. [Perfectionism as a strategy has its own page](/patterns/how-to-stop-being-a-perfectionist/), as does [the pleasing version of the same move](/patterns/what-is-people-pleasing/).
2. **Pre-empting the next absence.** Guardedness, deliberate indifference, keeping friendships pleasant and shallow, preferring relationships that are long distance or otherwise structurally limited, and hunting for a flaw in somebody right after things go well. [The distancing end of this is covered separately.](/patterns/emotionally-unavailable/)
3. **Detecting absence early enough to brace for it.** Hypervigilance to a mood shift, reading a flat tone as a verdict, twenty minutes spent deciding what a one-word reply means about you. [The anxious version of that scanning has its own page.](/patterns/anxious-attachment-style/)
4. **Substituting something lower risk.** An animal that does not go quiet on you. A career that returns reliable approval. Fictional relationships, online communities, and objects kept for the connection they represent. Every one of them supplies a diluted version of what is missing, at a price the nervous system will accept, and none of them can withdraw.

The fourth job deserves more sympathy than it usually gets. Choosing the company of an animal over a person is a completely reasonable trade for somebody who has learned that people go quiet without warning. It is worth noticing, not correcting, and the useful question is what the substitute is protecting you from testing.

## Where Does It Show Up in an Ordinary Week?

In seven places, and most people recognize themselves in two or three before the others land.

| Where it shows up | What it looks like from outside | The sentence underneath |
|---|---|---|
| With a partner | One quiet evening from them, three explanations run before bed, all of them about you | "Every word they say is a clue about how little they love me." |
| With friends | You do the generous thing, then spend the rest of the day watching to see whether it was noticed | "No matter how much effort I put into relationships, it feels unseen and unappreciated." |
| With family | A curated version of your year on the phone, and one warm message from years ago reread again | "Even when they love me, I feel overlooked and secondary." |
| At work | Extra taken on without being asked, accommodation offered before anybody requests it | "I am only worthy of love if I am useful or pleasing to others." |
| In a group | You clock that nobody looked up when you started talking, and you finish the sentence anyway | "I often feel invisible in social situations." |
| When care comes toward you | A compliment deflected before it lands, a favor returned inside the hour | "They're just saying that. They don't really mean it." |
| On a Friday with nothing on | The empty evening gets read as information about your worth rather than as an empty evening | "Being single means I'm unlovable." |

The work row is the one people defend hardest, because it gets rewarded. Taking on more looks like conscientiousness from every angle and can run an entire career without anybody noticing that the effort is buying something other than a salary.

The friends row catches people who consider themselves sociable. A busy, warm social life containing nobody who knows what you are genuinely dealing with is one of the most effective hiding places this pattern has, since from outside it looks like the opposite of isolation. [The quieter version of that move is covered separately.](/insights/why-do-i-isolate-myself/)

## Are You Sure That Is What You Are Looking At?

Worth checking, because six fairly different situations produce a similar-sounding week, and what to do about each of them differs completely.

| What you notice | What it might actually be | What tells them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Feeling unloved that started inside one specific relationship this year | An accurate read on that relationship | An accurate read has a start date. This pattern has been running as long as you can remember |
| A general greyness where nothing lands, food, music and friends included | A low mood that needs a clinician rather than a page | Feeling unloved is selective and stays sharp about people. A flattening of everything is a different signal. See the section above on professional support |
| It arrived after a bereavement, a breakup, or a move | Grief doing its ordinary work | Grief reorganises across months and responds to time. This does not soften on its own |
| Wanting noticeably more contact than the people around you offer | A plain mismatch of needs, with nobody at fault | Ask once, in plain words, for what you want. A mismatch usually adjusts. A pattern reappears with the next person |
| Very few people around and almost nobody to call | Loneliness, which is a supply problem | More company genuinely helps loneliness. It rarely touches this, which is the whole diagnostic |
| Warmth restored when you back down, withdrawn when you assert something | A control dynamic | The warmth tracks your compliance rather than anybody's capacity. This belongs with a professional |

Most people arriving here have had this argument with themselves many times over. Sitting with this table and picking the row that matches the last three years, rather than the row that matches the worst evening, is worth more than another round of it.

## Why Do Clinging and Pushing People Away Come From the Same Place?

Because both are strategies against the same absence, which is why the two can live in one person and alternate week to week.

The reaching version is easier to spot. More contact, more reassurance, more giving, more monitoring, the alarm going off at any drop in temperature and the response being to move closer. From inside it feels like love. Much of it is a threat-detection system trying to close a gap by force. [The anxious end of that has its own page](/patterns/anxious-attachment-style/), as does [the fear of being left that drives it](/patterns/fear-of-abandonment/).

The withdrawing version does the same job from the other direction. If nothing is fully risked, nothing can be confirmed. Going quiet after a close weekend, keeping things light, choosing partners who are structurally unavailable, all of it protects against the same verdict by making sure the verdict is never delivered. Attraction to unavailable people is more logical than it looks: a relationship where love has to be earned is familiar territory, and a question left permanently open never gets answered badly. [Why the same type keeps reappearing is worth settling separately.](/insights/why-do-i-attract-the-same-people/)

The two often pair up. One person reaches, the other retreats, each escalating in response to the other, and both experience the whole thing as being caused entirely by their partner. Nobody in that loop is behaving badly. Two protective systems built in two different houses are running at full volume in one kitchen.

The useful part is that either person can slow it unilaterally. One round of pursuit withheld, or one extra hour of staying, changes the signal the other system is reading. That is a much smaller thing to attempt than fixing a relationship, and it is available tonight.

## What Does the Other End of This Look Like?

Considerably quieter than most people expect, and mostly a question of where the verdict comes from.

Every one of these patterns has two ends, and describing only the wounded end leaves you with an accurate account of your problem and nowhere to walk. So here is the other end, translated out of abstraction and into things you could notice happening.

| The wounded end | The resolved end | What it looks like in an ordinary week |
|---|---|---|
| Feeling unloved | Feeling cherished | An ordinary Tuesday kindness registers as affection while it is happening, rather than being reconstructed later |
| Feeling unworthy of love | Feeling worthy of love | An invitation arrives and you accept it without working out why they asked you |
| Fear of being unlovable | Confidence in being loved | A quiet evening from somebody stays a quiet evening from somebody |
| Emotional deprivation | Emotional nourishment | A twenty-minute conversation leaves you fuller than it found you |
| Feeling invisible | Feeling acknowledged | You say something in a group and let it land without scanning faces afterward |
| Feeling expendable | Feeling essential | You take a week off and do not spend it wondering whether you will be missed |
| Skepticism of compliments | Acceptance of compliments | Praise arrives, you say thank you, and nothing gets added after it |
| Seeing love as conditional | Understanding love as unconditional | An evening where you gave nothing and got warmth anyway lands without feeling like a debt |
| Craving constant attention | Assurance in occasional distance | A short reply on a busy day stays a short reply on a busy day |
| Desiring constant reassurance | Trusting other people's affection | A week goes by without you asking whether things are alright between you |
| Self-invalidation | Self-affirmation | A feeling gets named on the day it happens, with no audit of whether you are allowed it |
| Longing for affection | Self-compassion | An evening alone gets treated kindly rather than used as evidence |
| Chronic dissatisfaction | Inner fulfillment | A good weekend gets enjoyed while it is happening instead of inspected for what is missing |
| Compromising boundaries | Maintaining boundaries | A no gets said without rehearsal and without bracing for the relationship to end |
| Reluctance to express love | Confidence in expressing love | You tell somebody you are glad they exist, first, without waiting to hear it back |
| Feeling unqualified for success | Feeling worthy of success | Something goes well and you take it as yours, without immediately looking for the next thing |

Read the right-hand column carefully, because it is deliberately unimpressive. Nobody in it is being adored. What changed is the location of the validating function: it moved from outside the person to inside them, and the practical result is that other people's warmth becomes pleasant rather than load-bearing.

The single most telling marker sits in that first row. Ordinary, consistent, undramatic affection starts registering as love, instead of being discounted for not being intense enough to trust. That is a smaller change than it sounds and it reorganises everything, because ordinary consistent affection is what most love actually looks like once you can see it.

None of this requires deciding your childhood was fine, forgiving anybody, or reaching a settled account of your parents. Being alone stops being evidence. A delayed reply stops triggering a scan. Needing something stops requiring an apology in front of it. Those are the real markers, and they are small enough to be missed while they are happening.

Don't judge yourself if that column reads as slightly fictional right now. The distance between the two columns took two decades to build, and nobody crosses it in a season. [Self-compassion is its own separate skill](/insights/what-is-self-compassion/) and for most people it arrives late rather than early.

## How Do You Start Letting Love Register?

By running repetitions small enough that you will actually finish them, in situations where almost nothing is at stake.

1. **Take one compliment a day without paying for it.** Say thank you. Add nothing. No returned compliment in the same minute, no correction, no self-deprecating joke to level the exchange. For anybody whose place has always felt earned, this is harder than any confession, and it works straight on the root.
2. **Log the discount.** Every time warmth arrives and gets reclassified, write down what arrived and the exact sentence you used to dismiss it. Change nothing. Fourteen days of that produces a list of your own rebuttals in your own handwriting, and the rebuttals turn out to be about four sentences repeating.
3. **Count the ordinary evidence rather than the dramatic evidence.** At the end of a day, write the small consistent things: who replied, who remembered something, who made you tea without being asked. This trains the reading of exactly the signal the pattern is blind to.
4. **Ask the plain question instead of running the audit.** When a short reply sets the scan off, ask once, in ordinary words, whether everything is alright between you. Ten seconds of asking beats an afternoon of interpretation, and it gives you real information rather than another simulation.
5. **Make one want visible per week.** Ask for something small you would like, in plain words, without three supporting reasons and without a favor paid in advance. The thing itself hardly matters. What gets rebuilt is the muscle that registers a want and then says it out loud.
6. **Stop paying for the affection you already have.** Watch the hour after somebody is warm to you and notice the extra favor, the gift, the over-helpfulness that goes out. Skip it once and sit with the discomfort, which is where the belief that love has a price is actually stored.
7. **Sit alone on purpose for twenty minutes with nothing to do.** No phone, no podcast, no task. Watch what verdict shows up in the silence, because a person who cannot be alone for twenty minutes without producing evidence about their worth will keep needing company for the wrong reason.
8. **Put it in the body rather than in the argument.** When you catch yourself reasoning about whether somebody loves you, drop the reasoning and locate the feeling physically: chest, throat, stomach. [Feeling something rather than analyzing it is its own skill.](/insights/how-to-feel-your-emotions/)
9. **Say the warm thing first.** Tell somebody you appreciate them without waiting to hear it back and without a reason attached. Going first is the practice, because a person who only ever responds to affection never finds out what it feels like to start it.

Is any of this satisfying while you do it? No. It is slow, unremarkable, and freaking boring in the way that actual repair usually is. It also holds, which the dramatic version generally does not.

## How Long Does This Take, Honestly?

Longer than anybody wants, and the honest answer runs at two speeds.

Recognition is fast. Most people can catch the discount happening in real time within a few weeks of paying deliberate attention, and catching it counts even on the days you go ahead and discount anyway. Watching yourself talk a compliment out of existence while knowing exactly what you are doing feels ridiculous, and it is a genuine step forward.

Reception takes far longer, because it is being built rather than repaired. The original loop, warmth arriving and being taken in, either ran thousands of times in your childhood or it did not, and assembling a rough adult version of it takes a serious quantity of small unremarkable repetitions spread across months, in rooms where nothing much is at stake. Reading this page changes very little on its own. Fifty compliments accepted plainly, on the day they were given, change a great deal.

For most people this gets quieter and smaller rather than disappearing, and it tends to resurface at genuine wobbles: an illness, a move abroad, a breakup, a bereavement. What changes is the gap between the alarm and the behavior, and how long an episode runs. An afternoon lost to decoding a short message becomes a ten-minute detour, and that is a real difference to live inside.

Is that frustrating? Yes, and I would rather say so than sell a faster route that does not exist. Plenty of people also do this alongside a therapist, and for something that formed in the absence of another person's attention, rebuilding part of it in the presence of one makes obvious sense. [The link between this and how you value yourself generally is worth reading too.](/insights/root-cause-of-low-self-esteem/)

## What Is One Thing to Do Today, Before You Decide Anything About Anybody?

Run an arrivals log for seven days. Open a note on your phone, and every time any piece of warmth comes toward you, however small, write two lines: what arrived, and what you did with it in the ten seconds afterward. A colleague said the thing you wrote was good, and you said it was mostly somebody else's work. Your sister sent a photo for no reason, and you replied with logistics. Somebody held a door and you apologized. It takes fifteen seconds an entry and you can do it from a supermarket queue.

Then read the whole list on the eighth day and count two things: how many pieces of warmth actually arrived that week, and how many of them you let land. Almost everybody is surprised by the first number, which is usually far higher than the felt experience suggested, and sobered by the second. Seeing both, in your own handwriting, across a week of ordinary Tuesdays, does more than another year of asking whether you are loved enough, because it moves the question from a place you cannot check to a place you can.
